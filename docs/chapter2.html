Section 2.1

What Makes a Battery? First Principles

Every engineer who works with batteries benefits from a clear mental model of what is actually happening inside the cell. Not just "electrochemistry" as a black box, but the specific physical processes — electron flow, ion migration, crystal lattice changes — that determine every measurable parameter the BMS must track and protect.

The Electrochemical Cell — Four Essential Components

Every rechargeable battery cell is an electrochemical cell: a device that converts chemical potential energy into electrical energy through redox reactions at two electrodes separated by an ion-conducting electrolyte. Four components are essential, and each has a precise, non-negotiable role.

🔑
The Critical Distinction: Electrons vs. Ions

The electrolyte conducts lithium ions (Li⁺) but blocks electrons. This is the entire operating principle. Because electrons cannot flow through the electrolyte, they are forced through the external circuit (your load). That forced electron flow is the current you use. Remove the external circuit and the cell has no way to equilibrate — it holds its charge indefinitely (ignoring self-discharge). This is why "open-circuit voltage" is a stable, meaningful measurement.

Where voltage actually comes from: Cell voltage equals the electrochemical potential difference between cathode and anode — the Gibbs free energy released per mole of reaction, divided by the charge transferred. At standard conditions:

Cell Voltage — Origin in Reduction Potentials E_cell = E°_cathode − E°_anode LFP: E°_cathode (Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ in PO₄ lattice) ≈ +3.45 V vs Li/Li⁺ E°_anode (Li⁺/LiC₆ graphite) ≈ +0.15 V vs Li/Li⁺ → E_cell ≈ 3.45 − 0.15 = 3.30 V nominal The ~0.15V anode potential varies slightly with SoC. The Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ redox couple in the phosphate lattice pins the plateau. This is why LFP voltage barely changes with SoC.
Intercalation — What Physically Happens When a Li-ion Cell Charges

The word "intercalation" comes from the Latin intercalare — to insert between. In lithium-ion batteries, it describes the process by which lithium ions are inserted into (or extracted from) the crystal lattice of an electrode material, without permanently altering that material's structure.

Think of it like this: imagine a stack of pancakes. Each pancake is a graphene layer in the graphite anode. The gaps between pancakes are where Li⁺ ions park during charging. When you charge the battery, Li⁺ ions squeeze between the graphene layers and sit there, electrostatically held. When you discharge, they unsqueeze and leave. The pancakes (graphene layers) barely change shape — just expand slightly (3.5% for graphite) when the Li⁺ ions are inside.

This is fundamentally different from lead-acid, where the electrode is consumed in the reaction (lead converts to lead sulfate). In intercalation, the electrode hosts lithium reversibly — like a hotel that houses guests without changing its structure. This reversibility is why Li-ion achieves 10× more cycles than lead-acid.

Discharge — Following a Li⁺ Ion on Its Journey

Start: the cell is fully charged. The anode is a graphite crystal fully loaded with Li⁺ ions between its layers (LiC₆). The cathode is FePO₄ — fully depleted lithium iron phosphate, all Li⁺ slots empty.

You connect a load (a lamp). Here is what happens at the atomic scale in the next microseconds:

  • Oxidation at anode: A Li⁺ ion deintercalates from the graphite anode. The graphite simultaneously releases one electron. The Li⁺ enters the electrolyte. The electron enters the copper current collector, flows through the external circuit (through your lamp, doing work), arrives at the aluminum current collector.
  • Ion migration: The Li⁺ ion migrates through the electrolyte — pulled toward the cathode by the electric field gradient. This migration is rate-limited by ionic conductivity and the Li⁺ diffusion coefficient in the electrolyte (~10⁻⁶ cm²/s at 25°C). This is why charge rate has limits — you cannot push ions faster than diffusion allows.
  • Reduction at cathode: The Li⁺ ion reaches the LFP cathode and intercalates into an empty site in the FePO₄ crystal. Simultaneously, the electron arriving from the external circuit reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺, maintaining charge neutrality. The crystal site now holds LiFePO₄.
  • Result: One Li⁺ has moved from anode to cathode. One electron has flowed through the external circuit. The process repeats approximately 6×10²³ times per mole of lithium (Avogadro's number) to fully discharge the cell.
Complete LFP Discharge Reaction Anode: LiC₆ → C₆ + Li⁺ + e⁻ Cathode: FePO₄ + Li⁺ + e⁻ → LiFePO₄ Overall: LiC₆ + FePO₄ → C₆ + LiFePO₄ (ΔG° ≈ −309 kJ/mol) Negative ΔG° = spontaneous reaction = energy is released. Charge reaction is the exact reverse, requiring energy input equal to ΔG°.
Charge — Reversing the Process

During charging, an external power supply forces electrons backward — from the aluminum current collector (cathode) through the supply and into the copper collector (anode). This non-spontaneous electron flow drives the non-spontaneous chemistry: Li⁺ is pulled out of the LiFePO₄ cathode (Fe²⁺ oxidized back to Fe³⁺), travels through the electrolyte, and reintercalates into the graphite anode. The energy you put in (from the charger) is stored as chemical potential.

The efficiency of this round trip is the coulombic efficiency (η ≈ 99–99.9% for LFP) — almost all charge put in can be recovered. The small fraction lost (0.1–1%) goes to side reactions, primarily continued SEI layer growth on the anode. Over thousands of cycles, this tiny loss per cycle accumulates into measurable capacity fade — the subject of Section 2.7.

⚛
Why BMS Designers Must Understand Intercalation Kinetics

Intercalation is not instantaneous. Li⁺ diffusion into the electrode particle is governed by Fick's second law, with diffusion coefficients ranging from 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹¹ m²/s depending on material and temperature. At high charge rates (≥2C), Li⁺ cannot penetrate deep into the particle before more arrives — concentration polarization builds up at the particle surface, and local surface potential rises above the anode equilibrium potential, causing lithium plating instead of intercalation.

This is why the BMS must enforce charge rate limits — not as arbitrary conservative specs, but because exceeding them initiates a physically distinct, damaging reaction. At low temperature, diffusion slows further, dramatically lowering the safe charge rate threshold. A cell that accepts 0.5C safely at 25°C may plate lithium at just 0.05C at −10°C.

Section 2.2

LFP Cell Specifically

Chapter 1 made the case for LFP over other chemistries. Now we go deeper into the LFP cell itself — the exact reaction equations, the physical meaning of its voltage limits, and a precise electrochemical comparison against the competing chemistries every BMS engineer should understand.

The LFP Cathode Reaction — Step by Step

Lithium iron phosphate adopts the olivine crystal structure (orthorhombic, space group Pnma). The iron atoms occupy octahedral sites coordinated by six oxygen atoms. The phosphorus atoms sit in tetrahedral PO₄ groups. Lithium ions occupy one-dimensional channels (M1 sites) that run parallel to the b-axis — the highway along which Li⁺ ions must travel during intercalation.

Unlike NMC and NCA, where lithium extraction creates a continuous solid-solution (the Li content varies smoothly with SoC), LFP follows a two-phase mechanism. There are only two stable phases:

  • LiFePO₄ — the fully lithiated phase (discharged cathode). Iron is in the +2 oxidation state (Fe²⁺). Unit cell volume: ~291 ų.
  • FePO₄ — the delithiated phase (charged cathode). Iron is in the +3 oxidation state (Fe³⁺). Unit cell volume: ~272 ų.

During charging, a phase boundary moves through each LFP particle from the outside inward, converting LiFePO₄ → FePO₄. During discharge, the boundary moves outward, converting FePO₄ → LiFePO₄. While both phases coexist in a particle, the thermodynamic equilibrium potential is fixed at ~3.45V vs Li/Li⁺ — the potential of the FePO₄/LiFePO₄ phase boundary. This is what causes the flat OCV plateau.

LFP Full Cell Reaction (with graphite anode) Charge: LiFePO₄ + C₆ → FePO₄ + LiC₆ Discharge: FePO₄ + LiC₆ → LiFePO₄ + C₆ Volume change during full cycling: ~6.8% for the LFP cathode. Compare to NMC (high-Ni): 10–15%. Lower volume change = less mechanical stress = more cycles.
LFP Voltage Window — Physical Meaning of 2.5V to 3.65V

The BMS enforces a voltage window of 2.5 V – 3.65 V per cell. These are not arbitrary safety margins. Each limit corresponds to a specific physical event in the cell.

⬆
Upper Limit: 3.65 V — Why This Number

At 3.65V, the LFP cathode is fully delithiated (all sites converted to FePO₄). There are no more empty Li⁺ slots in the cathode crystal. Continued charging cannot do useful electrochemistry. Instead:

Above ~3.75V: Electrolyte begins oxidizing at the cathode surface, forming a growing CEI (cathode-electrolyte interphase) layer. This permanently increases impedance. Above ~4.0V: Iron dissolution begins — Fe³⁺ ions dissolve from the FePO₄ lattice into the electrolyte, migrate to the anode, and deposit as iron metal dendrites that can puncture the separator. Above ~4.2V on the anode side: lithium plating occurs — metallic lithium dendrites form.

The 3.65V cutoff provides ~100mV margin above the plateau, balancing full capacity utilization against electrolyte stability. Set it to 3.75V and you gain a few percent capacity but accelerate aging by 2–5× in the cells that chronically reach it.

⬇
Lower Limit: 2.5 V — Copper Dissolution Threshold

The anode copper current collector is electrochemically stable as long as the anode potential remains low. As the cell discharges deeply, the anode potential rises toward the standard potential of the Cu/Cu²⁺ couple (+0.34V vs SHE, or approximately +3.4V vs Li/Li⁺ at the anode). At cell terminal voltages below ~2.5V, the copper collector begins to oxidize — Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻.

Cu²⁺ ions dissolve into the electrolyte, migrate to the cathode, and deposit on the LFP surface, blocking Li⁺ intercalation sites. They also deposit on the separator, potentially nucleating dendritic shorts. Even a single 0V event can deposit enough copper to measurably increase impedance and reduce capacity. Repeated deep discharges below 2.5V are cumulative and irreversible.

Nominal voltage (3.2–3.3V) vs. operating range: The nominal voltage is the average voltage during a standard 0.2C discharge at 25°C — this is the voltage you use for energy calculations (Wh = Ah × V_nominal). It is not the open-circuit voltage at any particular SoC; it is the time-averaged value across the discharge curve. For LFP, the flat plateau means this average closely matches the plateau potential.

LFP vs. NMC — why nominal voltage differs: NMC uses a layered oxide cathode (LiNiₓMnᵧCoᵤO₂). The Ni²⁺/Ni⁴⁺ and Co²⁺/Co³⁺ redox couples operate at higher potential than Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺. This gives NMC a nominal voltage of ~3.6–3.7V vs LFP's 3.2V — a 12–15% higher energy per cell. Higher voltage is why NMC achieves higher energy density despite similar cathode mass.

Section 2.3

Cell Formats — Cylindrical, Prismatic, Pouch

The same electrode chemistry (LFP) can be packaged in three fundamentally different physical form factors. The choice of format is not cosmetic — it determines thermal management requirements, manufacturing cost, pack integration complexity, cycle life, and safety behaviour. For UPS and home ESS, the choice matters enormously.

Cylindrical Cells — 18650, 21700, 4680

Cylindrical cells are wound — a long strip of anode material, separator, cathode material, and another separator is wound around a mandrel like a roll of paper towel, then inserted into a steel can. The numbers in the name are the dimensions: an 18650 is 18 mm diameter × 65 mm length. A 21700 is 21 mm × 70 mm. A 4680 is 46 mm × 80 mm.

Why those numbers matter: Volume scales as r²×h, so going from 18650 to 4680 increases cell volume by a factor of ~(23/9)² × (80/65) ≈ 6.9×. Tesla's 4680 cell contains approximately 5–6× more energy than an 18650, but far fewer cells are needed per pack — reducing tab welding complexity and associated failure modes.

Cylindrical cells have inherent mechanical strength from the steel can. The wound structure means current must flow from the jelly roll core to the terminals through the winding — this creates moderate internal resistance compared to prismatic designs. The cylindrical geometry creates inefficient packing in rectangular module designs (air gaps between cylinders).

🌡
Cylindrical Thermal Profile

Heat is generated throughout the jelly roll but must escape radially through the steel can to reach the cooling surface. For small cells (18650), the thermal path is short. For large-format cylinders (4680), the center can run significantly hotter than the surface. At high C-rates, center-to-surface temperature gradients of 15–20°C are possible, accelerating degradation non-uniformly. BMS thermal management must account for this internal gradient, not just the surface temperature.

Prismatic Hard-Case Cells — The ESS Standard

Prismatic cells stack the electrode layers (rather than winding them) inside a rigid aluminum rectangular enclosure. The layers are either stacked flat or wound flat (a "flat wound" or "Z-fold" design). Typical prismatic LFP cells for ESS: 100Ah, 200Ah, 280Ah, 304Ah — sizes that have become de facto standards driven by Chinese manufacturers (CATL, EVE, CALB, Lishen).

The 280Ah prismatic LFP cell (~173 × 72 × 205 mm, ~620g) has become the dominant format for DIY and commercial home ESS globally. At 3.2V nominal, one cell stores 896 Wh. A 16-cell series string (48V nominal) stores approximately 14.3 kWh — enough for a typical home's evening energy consumption.

  • Low internal resistance due to large electrode area and short current path to terminals
  • Good thermal conductivity through flat aluminum case — efficient cooling with side plates
  • Efficient volumetric packing in rectangular modules — no air gaps
  • Flat external surfaces accommodate compression fixtures — critical for managing cell swelling
  • Large format = fewer cells per pack = fewer welds, connectors, and potential failure points
  • Standardized dimensions allow commodity pricing and interchangeable sourcing
  • Large format means a single cell failure can hold significant energy — fault management critical
  • Cell swelling (3–5% expansion over life) requires mechanical constraint in module design
  • Individual cell replacement in a module requires disassembly — field maintenance is harder
Pouch Cells — Maximum Energy Density, Minimum Rigidity

Pouch cells stack or layer electrodes inside a flexible aluminum-laminate polymer bag (the "pouch"). No metal can — the bag conforms to the cell contents. This eliminates ~15–20% of the weight of a cylindrical or prismatic format, delivering the highest gravimetric and volumetric energy density of the three formats.

Pouch cells are dominant in consumer electronics (smartphones, tablets) and some EV applications (GM Bolt, Nissan Leaf original). For ESS, they have significant disadvantages:

  • No structural rigidity — requires external compression frames that add weight and cost
  • Cell swelling is unconstrained without external pressure — pouch "pillowing" can rupture the laminate
  • The flexible pouch provides no mechanical protection against puncture or vibration
  • Tabs and bus bars connecting to external circuits are typically thin and fragile
  • Higher manufacturing complexity — sealing is sensitive to contamination

For UPS and home ESS: prismatic cells are the right choice. The combination of large format, high capacity, rigid construction, efficient thermal management, and commodity availability makes prismatic LFP the universal selection for stationary ESS applications.

Section 2.4

Vcell Behaviour — The Heart of BMS Design

The voltage of a lithium-ion cell is not a simple linear function of its charge state. It is a complex, temperature-dependent, rate-dependent, history-dependent signal that encodes multiple overlapping phenomena simultaneously. Understanding this in depth is not optional for BMS engineers — the entire architecture of SOC estimation, protection logic, and health monitoring depends on it.

Open-Circuit Voltage (OCV) — The Thermodynamic Truth

The open-circuit voltage is the cell terminal voltage measured when no current flows and the cell has been at rest long enough for all internal concentration gradients to equilibrate. It is the thermodynamic quantity — the true electrochemical potential difference between cathode and anode at the current state of lithiation.

OCV is the most reliable voltage measurement you can make. It is not affected by ohmic drop (no current → no IR drop), not affected by diffusion polarization (equilibrated), and highly reproducible at the same temperature and SoC. This is why OCV is the gold standard for SOC calibration — if you can measure OCV, you can look up SoC in a pre-characterized table.

The catch: you can only measure true OCV when no current is flowing and you have waited long enough. For LFP cells, "long enough" is surprisingly long — 30 minutes to 4+ hours, depending on temperature and how recently the cell was disturbed. The reason is slow Li⁺ diffusion equilibration within electrode particles after current stops.

THE FLAT CURVE PROBLEM — LFP's Central Challenge
📉
This Is the Most Important Topic in LFP BMS Design

The flat OCV curve is not a minor inconvenience. It is the defining technical challenge of LFP battery management. Every SOC estimation algorithm, every calibration strategy, every design decision in the BMS firmware is shaped by this single property. If you understand nothing else from Chapter 2, understand this.

Recall from the electrochemistry: LFP operates via a two-phase mechanism. While both FePO₄ and LiFePO₄ phases coexist in the electrode particles, the cell potential is pinned at the equilibrium potential of the phase boundary: ~3.35–3.38V. This pinning holds from approximately 20% SoC to 90% SoC — covering 70% of the cell's usable range.

The result: a real LFP cell's OCV varies by only 50 mV across the 20–80% SoC range where you actually operate the cell most of the time.

Terminal Voltage vs. OCV — The IR Drop Problem

The OCV is what the cell wants to be at rest. The terminal voltage is what you actually measure on the cell terminals during operation. The difference is the voltage drop across the cell's internal resistance, plus diffusion polarization:

Terminal Voltage Model V_terminal = OCV − I × R_internal − V_polarization(t) Discharge: V_terminal < OCV (voltage drops under load) Charge: V_terminal > OCV (voltage rises during charging) R_internal for a 280Ah LFP prismatic cell: typically 0.2–0.5 mΩ at 25°C. V_polarization rises over seconds-to-minutes as concentration gradients build.

Why this matters for BMS design: If you read terminal voltage during a discharge at 200A (0.7C for a 280Ah cell), and R_internal = 0.4 mΩ:

IR Drop Example — 200A Discharge ΔV_IR = I × R = 200 A × 0.0004 Ω = 0.08 V = 80 mV Terminal voltage reads 80 mV below OCV. Since the LFP plateau changes only 50 mV across 20–80% SoC, this IR drop completely masks SoC information. You cannot read SoC from terminal voltage under load — ever — for LFP.

At higher currents (2C = 560A for this cell), IR drop reaches 224 mV — larger than the entire useful OCV variation from 20% to 100% SoC. The cell appears to have a lower terminal voltage at high current purely from resistance, regardless of SoC. This is why some UPS systems falsely report "low battery" when high loads are applied to LFP packs — the BMS misinterprets IR-induced voltage sag as low SoC.

💡
BMS Design Requirement: Current-Compensated Voltage

Any BMS that uses voltage in its SOC algorithm must compensate for IR drop. This requires measuring the current simultaneously with voltage, knowing (or estimating) R_internal, and computing the estimated OCV as: OCV_est = V_terminal + I × R_internal. R_internal changes with temperature and aging — so the BMS must track it continuously using pulse characterization or model-based estimation.

C-Rate Effect on Voltage Depression

Even with IR compensation, terminal voltage is further depressed at high current by diffusion polarization — concentration gradients in the electrolyte and within electrode particles that take minutes to equilibrate. Higher C-rate → deeper diffusion polarization → lower terminal voltage at the same true SoC:

Hysteresis — Charge Curve vs. Discharge Curve

A subtlety of LFP that surprises many engineers: the OCV curve is not the same for charging and discharging. The OCV measured after charging (charging OCV) sits approximately 10–20 mV higher than the OCV measured after discharging to the same SoC (discharging OCV).

This is the hysteresis of LFP — a consequence of the thermodynamics of the two-phase transition. Moving the phase boundary in one direction (lithiating FePO₄ → LiFePO₄) costs slightly different energy than moving it in the other direction (delithiating LiFePO₄ → FePO₄), due to coherency strain and nucleation energy at the phase boundary. The cell does not trace the same equilibrium path in both directions.

BMS design implication: An SOC lookup table derived only from discharge curves will be incorrect for a cell that has recently been charged. The BMS must maintain two OCV tables (charge and discharge) or use a single mid-point curve with ±10mV uncertainty. This adds another ±3–5% SoC uncertainty on top of the already-problematic flat curve. For SOC estimation algorithms that use OCV correction, the history of the cell (last charging vs discharging direction) must be tracked.

⚛
Temperature Effect on LFP Cell Voltage

Temperature affects LFP terminal voltage through three mechanisms: (1) OCV temperature coefficient: LFP OCV decreases by ~0.1–0.3 mV/°C. A cell at 0°C reads ~3–7 mV lower than the same cell at 25°C at the same SoC. (2) Internal resistance increase: Ionic conductivity of electrolyte decreases dramatically at low temperature (~4× higher resistance at −20°C vs 25°C), increasing IR drop. A 200A load that creates 80 mV drop at 25°C may create 320 mV drop at −20°C. (3) Kinetic polarization: Slower diffusion at low temperature increases electrode polarization voltage.

Combined effect: at −20°C under moderate load, terminal voltage can be 500–800 mV lower than at 25°C at the same SoC. The BMS low-voltage cutoff must be temperature-compensated or the pack will falsely trip the discharge cutoff at 50–60% SoC in cold environments.

Section 2.5

Capacity, Energy & Coulomb Concepts

The BMS is, at its core, a charge accountant. It must track every electron that flows in and out of the battery, convert that count into meaningful units (Ah, Wh, percentage), and do so accurately over years of operation. This requires a thorough understanding of the underlying concepts and their engineering traps.

Capacity (Ah) — Physical Meaning

Battery capacity is measured in ampere-hours (Ah) — the product of current and time. A cell with a rated capacity of 280 Ah can deliver 1 ampere for 280 hours, or 280 amperes for 1 hour, or any combination of average current × hours = 280 Ah.

Physically, capacity represents the total amount of lithium that can shuttle between anode and cathode. For a 280Ah cell:

Moles of Li in a 280Ah LFP Cell Q = 280 Ah = 280 × 3600 = 1,008,000 C (coulombs) n_Li = Q / (F) = 1,008,000 / 96,485 = 10.45 mol Li Mass of Li = 10.45 × 6.941 g/mol = 72.5 g of lithium Faraday's constant F = 96,485 C/mol. Every 280Ah cycle moves 72.5 grams of lithium atoms between anode and cathode and back. Over 5000 cycles, each lithium atom makes the journey ~5000 times.
Energy (Wh) = Capacity × Average Voltage

Energy stored in the cell is the integral of power over the discharge:

Cell Energy E = ∫V(t) × I(t) dt ≈ Q_Ah × V_avg LFP 280Ah cell: E = 280 Ah × 3.20 V = 896 Wh = 0.896 kWh 16-cell 48V pack: E = 16 × 896 Wh = 14.3 kWh The average voltage for LFP at 0.2C is ~3.2V. The BMS reports energy in Wh or kWh to the user; it internally tracks Ah via current integration.
C-Rate — Normalizing Current to Capacity

The C-rate is a way to express charge or discharge current relative to a cell's capacity, making comparisons meaningful across cells of different sizes. 1C for a given cell means the current that would fully discharge it in exactly 1 hour:

C-Rate Definition I_rate = C_rated / hours For 280Ah: 1C = 280 A, 0.5C = 140 A, 0.2C = 56 A, 2C = 560 A C-rate is dimensionally hours⁻¹ but treated as a ratio. "2C" means twice the current, half the discharge time. Higher C-rate = more current = more heat = faster aging.

Why C-rate matters for BMS design: The BMS must configure protection thresholds in real current (Amperes), derived from C-rate specifications for the cell. Maximum continuous discharge current for most prismatic LFP cells is 0.5C–1C. Peak current (for ≤10 seconds) may be 2–3C. Short-circuit currents above 5C trigger hardware protection. These thresholds directly define the current sensor range, the MOSFET sizing, and the fuse specification.

The Peukert Effect — Why Available Capacity Drops at High Current

Wilhelm Peukert observed in 1897 (for lead-acid, but applies generally) that available capacity decreases at higher discharge rates. For LFP, the effect is smaller than for lead-acid but still measurable and must be accounted for in SOC algorithms.

Peukert's Equation Q_actual = Q_rated × (C_rated / I_actual)^(k-1) k = Peukert exponent: ~1.01–1.05 for LFP, ~1.1–1.3 for lead-acid At k=1.05 for LFP 280Ah: at 1C (280A), Q_actual = 280 × (1/1)^0.05 = 280 Ah. At 2C (560A): Q_actual = 280 × (0.5)^0.05 = 280 × 0.966 = 270.5 Ah — 3.4% less.

Physical mechanism: At high discharge rates, Li⁺ ions cannot diffuse deeply into electrode particles fast enough. The outer surface regions deplete and reach the voltage cutoff while the interior is still fully lithiated. The cell trips the low-voltage protection at a higher residual charge than it would at low rates. From a BMS perspective, the cell "ran out" of accessible capacity, even though some lithium is still trapped inside the particles.

BMS implication: SOC estimated from accumulated coulombs assumes a constant capacity (Q_nominal). If the cell is discharged at 2C, the actual accessible capacity is ~3–5% lower — the coulomb counter will think there is still 3–5% SoC remaining when the low-voltage cutoff triggers. This creates an expected inconsistency in SOC reports at end-of-discharge under high current. Advanced BMS algorithms apply a C-rate correction factor to Q_nominal dynamically.

Coulomb Counting — The Integral and Its Drift Problem

Coulomb counting (also called current integration or charge integration) is the primary method for SOC tracking in Li-ion BMS systems. The concept is simple:

Coulomb Counting — The Core Equation SOC(t) = SOC(t₀) + (1/Q_nominal) × ∫[t₀ to t] η(I) × I(τ) dτ Where: SOC(t₀) = initial SOC, η(I) = coulombic efficiency (~0.999 for LFP), Q_nominal = rated capacity (Ah) In discrete time (as the MCU does it): SOC[n] = SOC[n-1] + (η × I[n] × Δt) / Q_nominal. I[n] is current in amperes (positive=charge, negative=discharge). Δt = sampling interval in hours.

What the BMS actually executes in code: Every Δt seconds (typically 100–250 ms), the microcontroller reads the current sensor, multiplies current by Δt to get incremental charge (in ampere-seconds, converted to Ah by dividing by 3600), applies the coulombic efficiency correction, and adds this increment to the accumulated SOC. The result is updated to the SOC register and used for all reporting and protection decisions.

Why Coulombs Drift Over Time — The Accumulation Problem

Coulomb counting sounds simple but has a fundamental weakness: every error in current measurement accumulates. Unlike a voltage measurement that can be re-taken independently at any time, a current integration error from 3 hours ago is still in your SOC estimate right now. There is no self-correction.

Reset Strategy — When and How to Recalibrate

The standard strategy for managing coulomb counting drift is to reset the SOC estimate against a more accurate method at opportunistic moments: (1) End of charge reset: When the BMS detects charge cutoff conditions (all cells at OVP threshold, current drops to C/20 tail), set SOC = 100% with high confidence. (2) Rest OCV correction: After the cell has rested for ≥30 minutes (detected by current < 1A for LFP), measure OCV, look up the table, and correct the coulomb count. (3) NVRAM storage: Store the last-known SOC and timestamp to NVRAM before power-off. On restart, use stored SOC + estimate drift during off-time as initial condition.

Section 2.6

SOC Estimation — Five Techniques

State of Charge estimation is the most algorithmically demanding function of the BMS. No single technique is perfect. Each has a specific accuracy profile, computational cost, and failure mode. Knowing all five — and understanding why each exists — is essential for BMS architecture decisions.

🔑
Why We Need SOC Estimation at All — The Fundamental Problem

A lead-acid battery shows its state of charge directly in its terminal voltage: 12.6V ≈ 100%, 12.0V ≈ 50%, 11.8V ≈ 20%. The slope is steep enough to be useful. An LFP cell at 3.34V is anywhere from 30% to 70% SoC. You fundamentally cannot read SoC from LFP voltage during normal operation. This is not an engineering limitation to be designed around — it is a consequence of the two-phase thermodynamics described in Section 2.4. The BMS must estimate SoC by other means.

T1
OCV Lookup Table Method
LFP Accuracy: ±15–25% (mid-range)

The simplest conceptual approach: measure the cell's open-circuit voltage after a rest period, look it up in a pre-characterized OCV-vs-SoC table, and read off the corresponding SoC.

Step-by-Step Process
  1. Detect that cell is at rest (|I| < threshold for > T_rest minutes)
  2. Measure cell voltage (now approximately OCV since current ≈ 0)
  3. Select whether to use the charge OCV table or discharge OCV table based on last current direction (hysteresis correction)
  4. Look up nearest SoC values in the table, interpolate linearly between points
  5. Apply temperature correction (typically −0.2 mV/°C for LFP)
  6. Use result as SOC estimate, reset coulomb counter to this value
The Rest Time Problem

"After a rest period" is not trivial for LFP. After current stops, the cell voltage relaxes toward OCV — but the relaxation is slow and non-linear. The first 60 seconds: fast IR-drop recovery. The next 5–30 minutes: diffusion relaxation within electrode particles. The next 30 minutes to 4 hours: further slow equilibration of concentration gradients.

Equivalent Circuit Model (ECM)
LFP Accuracy: ±3–8%, better dynamics

The Equivalent Circuit Model replaces the electrochemical cell with an electrical circuit that has the same terminal voltage behaviour. The most common form is the Thevenin model, also called the RC model:

Thevenin ECM (1RC): OCV source (SOC-dependent) + R₀ (ohmic resistance) + R₁C₁ parallel network (diffusion dynamics). V_terminal = OCV(SoC) − I×R₀ − V_C₁

What each element represents physically:

  • OCV(SoC, T): The thermodynamic equilibrium voltage — the same OCV-SoC table as Method T1, but now used as a model input, not the sole SOC estimator. Updated as SoC is estimated.
  • R₀ (ohmic resistance): Immediate voltage drop when current changes. Combines contact resistance, electrolyte bulk resistance, and current collector resistance. Characterizable from current-pulse tests. Changes with temperature and aging.
  • R₁C₁ (RC network): The relaxation dynamics. When current stops, the voltage doesn't jump instantly to OCV — it relaxes exponentially with time constant τ = R₁×C₁ (~30–300 seconds for LFP). Models the diffusion polarization that builds and decays with current history.

SOC estimation using ECM: The model predicts V_terminal given current history and assumed SoC. The SoC is updated iteratively (usually via an optimization step) to minimize the error between predicted and measured terminal voltage. This is better than pure coulomb counting because it uses voltage as a correction signal — but for LFP, with its flat OCV curve, the voltage correction is weak and the model still relies heavily on current integration.

Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)
LFP Accuracy: ±1–4% (best available)

The Extended Kalman Filter is the gold standard for SOC estimation in high-accuracy BMS designs. It is an optimal state estimator that fuses the prediction from a dynamic model (like the ECM) with the correction from measurements (current and voltage), weighted by the relative uncertainty of each.

The name "Extended" Kalman Filter refers to the fact that it handles nonlinear systems (like battery models, where OCV is a nonlinear function of SoC) by linearizing around the current state estimate. A standard Kalman filter only handles linear systems.

What the Kalman Filter Does — Plain Language

Imagine you are trying to track the position of a car using two methods: (1) a GPS that gives noisy but absolute position readings, and (2) an odometer that measures distance traveled but accumulates errors. The Kalman filter is the optimal way to fuse both signals — weighting each based on its known uncertainty — to get a better estimate than either alone.

In the battery context:
• The "odometer" = coulomb counting (tracks charge, drifts)
• The "GPS" = voltage measurement compared to predicted voltage from the ECM
• The Kalman filter weights these based on process noise (model uncertainty) and measurement noise (sensor noise)

Why EKF Handles LFP Better Than Simple Methods — But Still Struggles

The Kalman gain K is proportional to ∂OCV/∂SoC — the slope of the OCV curve. For LFP in the plateau region (20–80% SoC), this slope is nearly zero (~0.5 mV/%SoC). This means K ≈ 0 and the voltage innovation y[k] contributes almost nothing to correcting SoC. The EKF is effectively running as a pure coulomb counter in the middle SoC range. It only gets strong voltage correction at the edges (<20% and >90% SoC) where the curve steepens.

This is not a failure of the EKF algorithm — it is a fundamental information-theoretic limit. There is simply insufficient voltage information in the LFP flat region. No algorithm can extract what is not there. The EKF does extract what little voltage information exists, and it handles transitions in and out of the plateau region optimally — which is why it outperforms simple methods.

Computational Cost on Embedded MCU

For a 2-state EKF (SoC + V_C1), the per-sample computation involves:

  • Matrix operations: 2×2 matrix multiply, invert (scalar for 1 measurement), add → approximately 20–40 floating point operations per cell per sample
  • OCV lookup: Table interpolation, or polynomial fit evaluation (~10 ops)
  • Total per cell per sample: ~50–80 FLOP
  • For a 16-cell pack at 4 Hz sample rate: ~3,200–5,120 FLOP/s
  • Cortex-M4F (FPU) at 168 MHz: ~84 MFLOP/s capacity — EKF for 16 cells consumes <0.006% of MCU time
  • Cortex-M0 (no FPU): Software FP is ~20–50× slower. EKF is feasible but consumes ~0.5–2% of MCU time. Still fine.

RAM requirement: Per cell: P matrix (4 floats) + x state (2 floats) + OCV table (100 points × 2 floats) + RC params (4 floats) ≈ 880 bytes per cell. 16-cell pack: ~14 KB total. Easily within even a small STM32F0 (4–16 KB RAM minimum).

SOC Initialisation on Power-Up — What Happens at Boot

Every BMS faces a critical question at power-up: what is the current SOC? The answer determines whether the first charge or discharge cycle is safe. A bad initial SOC can cause the pack to be over-discharged (BMS thinks it has 50% remaining when it actually has 20%) or over-charged (thinks it's at 80% when it's at 95%).

Strategy 1 — NVRAM stored SOC (preferred): The BMS writes the current SOC to non-volatile memory (NVM) before every power-off and at regular intervals during operation. On boot, it reads the stored value and uses it as the initial condition. If the time elapsed since last write is short, this is highly accurate. If the system was off for weeks, self-discharge must be estimated and subtracted (~1–3% per month for LFP at 25°C).

Strategy 2 — OCV measurement at boot: If NVRAM is unavailable or corrupted, wait for the pack to rest (no current flowing), measure OCV, and look up SoC. For LFP this gives ±5–15% accuracy in the plateau region, but is reliable at the edges. If OCV reads 3.60V or higher, SOC > 90% with certainty. If OCV reads 3.20V or lower, SOC < 15% with certainty. The uncertain zone is the plateau, where more sophisticated boot-time estimation is needed.

Strategy 3 — Conservative boot SOC: If OCV is in the plateau and NVRAM is unavailable, some BMS designs boot with a conservative estimate (e.g., 50% SOC) and rely on the first charge cycle to reset the coulomb counter to 100% at end-of-charge. This is safe for UPS applications where the pack is immediately float-charged.

⚠
NVRAM Corruption — The Boot Failure Mode

NVRAM can be corrupted by power loss during a write, electrostatic discharge, or flash endurance exhaustion (STM32 internal flash: ~10,000 write cycles). The BMS must always write with a validity checksum (CRC-16 or CRC-32) and verify on read. If the checksum fails, fall back to OCV measurement rather than trusting corrupted data. Overconfident SOC initialization from corrupted NVM is a documented root cause of over-discharge events in field-deployed BMS systems.

Section 2.7

SOH, Aging & Degradation

A new LFP cell rated at 280Ah will not deliver 280Ah forever. Electrochemical degradation processes, operating continuously both when the cell is in use and when it is at rest, slowly consume its usable capacity. Understanding these processes allows the BMS to track health, predict end-of-life, and optimize operation to extend service life.

State of Health (SOH) — Definition and Measurement

State of Health (SOH) is a dimensionless metric expressing how much of a cell's original capacity remains usable:

SOH Definition (Capacity-Based) SOH = Q_actual / Q_rated × 100% Q_actual = measured discharge capacity at 0.2C, 25°C, from 100% to cutoff voltage Industry standard end-of-life threshold: SOH = 80%. At 80% SOH, a 280Ah cell delivers 224Ah. For ESS applications, cells are often replaced at 70–75% SOH to maintain minimum runtime guarantees.

Alternative SOH definition (impedance-based): Some BMS designs use internal resistance as a proxy for SOH. As cells age, internal resistance increases due to SEI growth and electrode particle fragmentation. The relationship is not linear but monotonic:

SOH (Impedance-Based Approximation) SOH_R = R₀_initial / R₀_current × 100% Usable for trend monitoring but not for accurate capacity reporting. A cell may show 90% SOH_R while being at 80% capacity SOH, or vice versa, depending on the primary degradation mode.
How SOH is Measured in Practice by the BMS

The most accurate method requires a reference performance test (RPT): a full charge to 100% followed by a controlled 0.2C discharge to cutoff, with precise coulomb counting. This gives Q_actual directly. The BMS can trigger an RPT automatically once every 30–100 cycles, typically during a low-priority period (e.g., overnight when grid is available).

Between RPTs, the BMS tracks SOH incrementally: each full or near-full cycle, the actual discharged Ah is recorded. A rolling average of the last N full-cycle capacities gives a running estimate of Q_actual. This "forgetting factor" approach lets the BMS track gradual capacity fade without requiring a formal RPT.

The SEI Layer — Battery Aging's Silent Protagonist

The Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) is the most important phenomenon in lithium-ion battery aging. Understanding it in depth transforms the BMS from a reactive protector to a proactive life-extender.

What the SEI Is and Why It Forms

When a lithium-ion cell is first charged, the graphite anode is held at a potential of ~0.1–0.2 V vs Li/Li⁺. At this low potential, the organic electrolyte solvents (ethylene carbonate, dimethyl carbonate) are thermodynamically unstable — they react with the electrode surface. This reaction is:

SEI Formation Reaction (Ethylene Carbonate Example) 2EC + 2e⁻ + 2Li⁺ → Li₂CO₃ + CH₂=CH₂ or → (CH₂OCO₂Li)₂ (lithium ethylene dicarbonate) Multiple parallel reactions occur, producing a complex mixture of inorganic (Li₂CO₃, LiF, Li₂O) and organic (lithium alkyl carbonates) compounds. The exact composition depends on electrolyte formulation, temperature, and formation protocol.

The SEI forms primarily during the first charge cycle (the "formation" step in cell manufacturing). Once formed, it creates a passivation layer that electronically insulates the graphite surface from the electrolyte — stopping further electrolyte decomposition. Without SEI, the electrolyte would continuously react until it was consumed.

The SEI is both essential and costly:

  • Allows stable cell operation by passivating the anode
  • Provides a Li⁺ conducting pathway (only lithium ions can pass through)
  • Self-healing: if mechanically cracked, the fresh graphite surface exposed re-forms SEI
  • Consumes lithium permanently — each Li consumed in SEI formation is "dead" (no longer cyclable)
  • Adds ionic resistance — thicker SEI = higher cell impedance = more heat generation
  • Continues to grow slowly throughout the cell's life
Why SEI Continues Growing — The Aging Mechanism

After the initial formation cycle, the SEI is never truly stable. It continues to grow slowly throughout the cell's service life through two pathways:

1. Solvent diffusion through existing SEI: The SEI is not a perfect barrier. Electrolyte solvent molecules slowly diffuse through the SEI layer and react with the underlying lithiated graphite. The reaction is slow — the SEI provides ~10⁴–10⁵× reduction in reaction rate compared to bare graphite — but it never stops. The rate follows an Arrhenius dependence on temperature.

2. Volume change cracking: The graphite anode expands ~3.5% when lithiated (charging) and contracts when delithiated (discharging). Over thousands of cycles, this mechanical cycling cracks the SEI layer, exposing fresh graphite surface. The fresh surface immediately forms new SEI, consuming more lithium. This is the primary degradation mechanism at high cycle count.

SEI Growth Rate — Simplified Arrhenius Model dδ/dt = A × exp(−E_a / (R × T)) × (electrode stress factor) δ = SEI thickness, E_a ≈ 50–80 kJ/mol, R = 8.314 J/mol·K At 35°C vs 25°C: rate ≈ exp(E_a/R × (1/298 − 1/308)) ≈ 1.5× faster A 10°C temperature increase roughly doubles or more the SEI growth rate. This is why operating LFP packs at 25°C vs 45°C extends calendar life by 2–4×.
📊
Quantifying Lithium Inventory Loss from SEI Growth

A typical LFP cell loses approximately 0.01–0.05% of capacity per cycle from SEI growth. For a 280Ah cell:
• Per cycle: 280 × 0.02% = 0.056 Ah consumed as SEI growth
• After 1000 cycles: 56 Ah consumed = 20% capacity loss (→ SOH ~80%)
• After 5000 cycles: 280 Ah consumed — but LFP slows down significantly due to lower-quality SEI cracks being fewer (more stable SEI over time)

The capacity fade curve for LFP is often "knee-shaped" — approximately linear in the first 80% of life, then accelerating near end-of-life as the SEI becomes too thick and starts conducting electrons (losing its passivation quality).

Calendar Aging — Degradation Without Use

A cell stored on a shelf at full charge is aging. Calendar aging affects every LFP installation — UPS batteries that sit charged for months between discharge events are subject to calendar aging that can be the dominant life-limiting factor.

The three primary calendar aging mechanisms:

  • SEI growth (dominant): As described above — slow but continuous even without cycling. Rate approximately quadrupled for each 10°C temperature increase.
  • Electrolyte decomposition at high SoC: Holding LFP at 100% SoC (3.65V/cell) accelerates electrolyte oxidation at the cathode surface. Storage at 50% SoC significantly reduces this mechanism compared to 100% SoC storage.
  • Lithium plating self-discharge: In cells that have experienced lithium plating (see below), the plated lithium reacts with electrolyte, consuming lithium and electrolyte — accelerating degradation in already-stressed cells.

Lithium Plating — The Fast-Aging Failure Mode

Lithium plating occurs when Li⁺ ions arrive at the anode faster than they can intercalate into the graphite. Instead of inserting between graphene layers, the lithium deposits on the anode surface as metallic lithium (Li⁰). The plated lithium can:

  • React with electrolyte, forming "dead lithium" that is electrochemically isolated — permanent capacity loss and electrolyte consumption
  • Grow as dendrites — needle-like metallic structures that can pierce the separator and cause an internal short circuit
  • Partially strip back (reintercalate) during rest, recovering some capacity but leaving behind dead lithium and SEI debris

When lithium plating occurs: Any condition where the anode cannot accept Li⁺ fast enough:

  • Low temperature: Li⁺ diffusion coefficient drops dramatically below 10°C. Even gentle charging (0.1C) can cause plating below −10°C for some LFP formulations.
  • High charge rate: At 2C+, diffusion polarization builds faster than intercalation can clear it.
  • High SoC near cutoff: As the anode fills up, remaining empty sites are deep inside particles — diffusion path lengthens, effective rate decreases.
  • Aged anode: SEI-blocked particle surfaces reduce active area, effectively increasing local current density even at moderate pack-level current.
❄
Critical BMS Rule: Prohibit Charging Below 0°C

IEC 62619 and all major BMS reference designs prohibit charging LFP below 0°C. The BMS must read cell temperature before any charging begins, and must continuously monitor temperature during charging. If temperature drops below 0°C during a charge cycle, the BMS must immediately reduce charge current to zero or apply pre-heating.

A single charging event at −10°C at 0.5C rate can cause measurable lithium plating and accelerate capacity fade by the equivalent of 50–100 normal cycles. For a 5000-cycle LFP pack, a single cold-charging event represents 1–2% of total life.

LFP Aging Advantage vs NMC — Real Numbers

LFP's superior cycle life over NMC is not just a number — it comes from three distinct structural advantages:

  • Lower volume change (6.8% vs 10–15%): Less mechanical stress per cycle means slower SEI crack rate and lower particle fragmentation rate
  • No transition metal dissolution: In NMC, Ni and Mn ions dissolve into electrolyte and deposit on the anode, catalyzing additional SEI growth. The phosphate framework in LFP holds iron firmly in place at normal operating voltages.
  • Lower operating voltage: LFP operates up to 3.65V vs NMC's 4.2V. SEI growth rate increases exponentially with anode potential — even 0.5V higher anode potential in NMC creates substantially faster SEI growth.

ECM Parameter Identification — How to Characterise Your Cell

The ECM and EKF algorithms in Section 2.6 require accurate model parameters (R₀, R₁, C₁) to function correctly. These parameters are temperature-dependent, SoC-dependent, and age-dependent. This section explains the standard test procedure for identifying them and the BMS strategy for tracking their change over time.

Current Pulse Test (HPPC — Hybrid Pulse Power Characterization)

The standard method for characterizing battery ECM parameters is the Hybrid Pulse Power Characterization (HPPC) test, adapted from the USABC (United States Advanced Battery Consortium) protocol:

  1. Discharge cell to target SoC (e.g., 90%) at 0.2C, wait 30 min for OCV equilibration
  2. Apply a discharge pulse at 1C for exactly 10 seconds, record V(t) at 100ms intervals
  3. Remove pulse (current = 0), wait 40 seconds, record V(t) — relaxation transient
  4. Apply charge pulse at 0.75C for 10 seconds
  5. Remove charge pulse, wait 40 seconds
  6. Discharge 10% SoC at 0.2C, wait 30 min, repeat at next SoC
  7. Repeat across all SoC values (100%, 90%, 80% … 10%, 0%)
  8. Repeat full test at multiple temperatures: 0°C, 10°C, 25°C, 35°C, 45°C

From the pulse response, extract parameters:

ECM Parameter Extraction from HPPC Pulse R₀ = (V_before − V_at_t=0⁺) / I_pulse // instantaneous voltage drop (ohmic) ΔV_slow = V_at_10s − V_at_0⁺ − V_at_0s // voltage due to RC network R₁ = ΔV_slow / I_pulse τ = −t / ln(1 − V_C1(t)/V_C1_final) // from relaxation curve C₁ = τ / R₁ This gives R₀, R₁, C₁ at each (SoC, Temperature) point. The result is a 2D lookup table: [SoC][Temp] → {R₀, R₁, τ}. The BMS loads these tables at startup and selects parameters based on current SoC and temperature estimates.
📈
Typical LFP ECM Parameter Values (280Ah Prismatic, 25°C)

For orientation: R₀ ≈ 0.3–0.6 mΩ (very low — large area prismatic cells), increases to 1.0–2.0 mΩ at −20°C. R₁ ≈ 0.3–1.0 mΩ (diffusion resistance), τ = R₁×C₁ ≈ 30–150 seconds for LFP (slower diffusion kinetics than NMC). C₁ ≈ 50,000–300,000 F (Farads) — enormous value because the electrode area is huge. These values are for a single cell; multiply R by n_series for pack resistance.

Online Parameter Tracking — BMS as a Continuous Cell Characterizer

The HPPC test is performed once (during design/validation). But R₀ changes with temperature and aging throughout the product life. The BMS must track it online:

  • R₀ update from current interrupts: Every time current changes abruptly (load connect/disconnect, charge start/stop), the instantaneous voltage jump ΔV divided by the current change ΔI gives a real-time R₀ estimate. A rolling average of these estimates tracks slowly-changing R₀.
  • R₀ as a health indicator: R₀ typically doubles or triples at end-of-life compared to initial values. Tracking R₀ over time gives an independent SOH estimate (impedance-based SOH) that can be correlated with capacity-based SOH.
  • Temperature correction: Apply the temperature coefficient (approximately −0.5%/°C for LFP R₀) to compensate measurements taken at different temperatures before comparing to the aging baseline.

LFP vs. NMC vs. NCA — Deep Electrochemical Comparison

The BMS engineer who works exclusively with LFP should still understand NMC and NCA deeply. Customers compare chemistries. System integrators may specify either. Understanding the electrochemical differences explains why BMS algorithms and protection thresholds differ fundamentally between chemistries — not just numerically, but structurally.

NMC (LiNiₓMnᵧCoᵤO₂) — Layered Oxide Cathode

NMC uses a layered rock-salt structure (R-3m space group). Lithium, nickel, manganese, and cobalt ions occupy alternate layers between oxygen layers. During charging, Li⁺ is extracted from the Li layer, leaving vacancies that the transition metal layers expand into slightly.

The three transition metals serve distinct roles:

  • Nickel (Ni): Primary electroactive species. Ni²⁺/Ni⁴⁺ redox couple provides high capacity and high voltage (~3.7V). Higher nickel content → higher energy density → worse thermal stability (onset of O₂ release moves lower).
  • Manganese (Mn): Structural stabilizer. Mn⁴⁺ is electrochemically inactive but supports the layered structure, reducing volume change. Prevents collapse of layers during deep cycling.
  • Cobalt (Co): Enables good rate capability and reduces cation mixing (Ni²⁺ accidentally occupying Li sites — causes capacity loss). Also expensive and ethically problematic (see Chapter 1).

The trend in NMC is toward higher nickel content: NMC111 (equal Ni:Mn:Co) → NMC532 → NMC622 → NMC811. Each step increases energy density but decreases thermal stability and makes the cathode more sensitive to overcharge. NMC811 begins oxygen release at just ~180°C — only 60°C above typical cell operating temperature in abuse scenarios.

BMS difference vs LFP: NMC has a more strongly sloped OCV curve (~1.2V change from 0–100% SoC), making voltage-based SOC estimation more reliable. But NMC requires more conservative protection thresholds: upper cutoff 4.2V (vs LFP 3.65V), lower cutoff 2.8–3.0V. The higher upper voltage accelerates electrolyte oxidation — NMC battery management is more sensitive to overcharge margin than LFP.

NCA (LiNi₀.₈Co₀.₁₅Al₀.₀₅O₂) — High Energy Density, Tight Margins

NCA substitutes aluminum for manganese in the NMC layered structure. The result is the highest energy density cathode in commercial production (~200–280 Wh/kg at cell level). Tesla's original 18650-format cells (Panasonic NCA) demonstrated this chemistry's potential. Modern 4680 cells also use NCA-based formulations.

Aluminum in NCA plays the same structural stabilization role as manganese in NMC, but Al³⁺ is a smaller ion that increases the structural stability compared to Mn⁴⁺. This allows even higher nickel content (0.80 vs 0.60–0.80 in NMC) for maximum energy density.

  • Thermal onset temperature for O₂ release: ~160°C — worst of all major cathode chemistries
  • Very sensitive to moisture — requires dry room manufacturing (<0.1% RH)
  • Higher self-discharge than LFP or NMC at elevated temperatures
  • Requires very precise voltage management — tighter tolerance BMS hardware
  • Cobalt content (15%) creates same supply chain vulnerability as NMC

For a BMS engineer, NCA represents the highest-performance, highest-risk chemistry. Every protection margin needs to be tighter, every temperature threshold lower, and every SOC uncertainty smaller. The BMS complexity (and cost) scales accordingly. This is one reason why large NCA packs (EVs, aircraft) use EKF or more advanced SOC algorithms — the consequences of under- or over-charging are severe.

Capacity-Rate Tradeoff — Beyond Peukert

The Peukert equation gives a useful first approximation of capacity vs. rate, but the actual physics involves more nuance. For a BMS engineer designing a high-accuracy system, understanding the full picture prevents systematic errors in runtime prediction.

What Really Limits Capacity at High Current

When an LFP cell is discharged at high C-rate, multiple simultaneous mechanisms reduce accessible capacity:

Mechanism 1 — Concentration Polarization: Li⁺ ions migrate from anode to cathode through the electrolyte. At high current, the supply of Li⁺ at the cathode surface depletes faster than bulk diffusion can replenish it. The local Li⁺ concentration drops, raising the cathode overpotential. The cell terminal voltage sags below the cutoff threshold while lithium remains in the electrode interior.

Mechanism 2 — Solid-State Diffusion Limitation: Inside each electrode particle, Li⁺ must diffuse from the surface to the core. At high current, surface Li concentration reaches saturation (during charge) or depletion (during discharge) before the particle interior equilibrates. This creates a "utilization front" that doesn't reach the core — leaving lithium stranded in inaccessible interior sites.

Mechanism 3 — Thermal Effect: High current generates heat (I²R). Temperature rise during discharge increases ionic conductivity (reducing IR drop and polarization) — a partially compensating effect. At moderate C-rates for LFP, the thermal effect can nearly cancel the diffusion limitation for short bursts. This is why LFP pulse power can exceed the "expected" limit from steady-state Peukert.

Modified Peukert with Temperature Correction Q_actual(I, T) = Q_ref × (I_ref / I)^(k(T)-1) k(T) = k₀ + α × (T_ref − T) // k increases at low temperature For LFP: k₀ ≈ 1.03 at 25°C, α ≈ 0.003 /°C At −10°C: k = 1.03 + 0.003×35 = 1.135 → at 1C: Q_actual ≈ 280×(1)^0.135 = 254 Ah (−9%) Low temperature dramatically reduces accessible capacity even at moderate C-rates. A BMS must not claim runtime based on room-temperature capacity when the pack is cold.
Remaining Runtime Prediction — The BMS Calculation

Given current SOC, current discharge current I, temperature T, and cell age (SOH), the BMS can calculate predicted remaining runtime:

Remaining Runtime Calculation Q_remaining = SOC% × Q_nominal × SOH% // Ah remaining at current health Q_available = Q_remaining × (I_ref/I)^(k-1) // Peukert correction for current rate Q_temp_adj = Q_available × f_temp(T) // temperature derating factor t_remaining = Q_temp_adj / I_current // hours = Ah / A f_temp(T) ≈ 1.0 at 25°C, ~0.70 at −10°C, ~0.85 at 0°C, ~1.05 at 40°C. All factors combine multiplicatively. A 48V 280Ah pack at 60% SOC, 80% SOH, −10°C, 100A load: t = (0.60 × 280 × 0.80 × 0.70) / 100 = 0.94 hours.

This calculation is updated every second in the BMS firmware, giving a continuously updated "time to empty" display. The accuracy is limited by: SOC accuracy (±5–7% → ±5–7% in Q_remaining), SOH accuracy (±2–5% typically), and load predictability (if load changes, the runtime estimate changes immediately).

Managing Multi-Cell Packs — Series, Parallel, and Topology

Real LFP packs for UPS and home ESS are not single cells — they are assemblies of dozens to hundreds of cells in series and parallel configurations. The BMS architecture must account for the electrical and thermal interactions between cells, and the topology dramatically affects protection requirements.

Series Strings — Voltage Addition, Current Equality

When N cells are connected in series, their voltages add and they share the same current:

Series String V_pack = N × V_cell_avg (all cell voltages sum) I_pack = I_cell (all cells carry same current) A 16S LFP string (nominal): V = 16 × 3.2V = 51.2V. At full charge: V = 16 × 3.65V = 58.4V. At cutoff: V = 16 × 2.5V = 40V. This is the "48V nominal" system used in home ESS and telecom.

The weakest cell dominates: Because all cells carry the same current, the cell with the lowest capacity determines when the pack trips protection cutoffs. If cell #7 has 250Ah and all others have 280Ah, the pack effectively has 250Ah capacity even if 15 cells are not depleted. The BMS must monitor every cell individually and cut off discharge when any single cell reaches the undervoltage threshold.

⚡
Why Per-Cell Voltage Monitoring Is Not Optional

A common shortcut in budget BMS designs is to monitor only the total string voltage and divide by N to estimate average cell voltage. This fails because: (1) If cell #7 is at 2.3V (below cutoff) while others are at 3.5V, the string voltage = 16 × average ≈ 54.5V — appearing healthy. (2) Cell #7 is being driven below its cutoff, dissolving copper and accumulating damage, while the BMS reports "normal." (3) After a few such events, cell #7 fails catastrophically — typically with an internal short that then discharges all other cells through the fault. Per-cell monitoring with an analog front-end IC is not a premium feature — it is the minimum viable protection architecture.

Parallel Groups — Capacity Addition, Voltage Equality

When M cells are connected in parallel, their capacities add and they share the same voltage:

Parallel Group V_group = V_cell (all cells at same voltage) Q_group = M × Q_cell (capacities add) A 2P group of 280Ah cells: Q = 2 × 280Ah = 560Ah. Current splits between parallel cells proportionally to their impedance — lower impedance carries more current.

Parallel cells are inherently self-balancing — any voltage imbalance drives a circulating current that equalizes them. This is both an advantage (no active balancing within a parallel group needed) and a risk (if one parallel cell fails internally, it sucks charge from its neighbors at potentially destructive rates).

Common configurations for home ESS:

  • 16S1P (48V, 280Ah = 14.3 kWh): 16 cells in series, no parallel. Most common configuration for 5–15 kWh home ESS. Simple BMS topology.
  • 16S2P (48V, 560Ah = 28.7 kWh): Two parallel strings of 16 cells. Higher capacity. BMS can monitor per-string or per-cell — per-cell preferred.
  • 32S1P (100V, 280Ah = 28 kWh): Higher voltage for larger inverters. Requires 32 individual cell voltage measurements — more complex AFE configuration.
Thermal Management — Why Cell-to-Cell Uniformity Matters

In a module of 16 cells, the cells are not all at the same temperature. Inner cells in a stack can be 5–15°C hotter than outer cells, depending on cooling design. This creates differential aging rates within the same module:

  • Inner cells age faster (higher temperature → faster SEI growth → faster capacity loss)
  • Inner cells develop higher impedance faster → carry less of the total current
  • This creates a current imbalance within parallel groups — outer cells work harder as inner cells become higher impedance, further exacerbating the imbalance

BMS design implication: The BMS should monitor temperature at multiple positions in the module (not just the outer surface), and include the temperature gradient in calendar aging models. For high-accuracy SOH tracking, individual cell impedance measurements allow identification of hot-spot-accelerated degradation before it becomes a failure.

Cell Balancing — Algorithm Design and Implementation

Chapter 1 introduced the concept of cell balancing. Chapter 2 has established the electrochemical reasons why cells diverge. Now we go into the practical implementation of balancing algorithms in the BMS firmware.

When to Balance — Timing and Trigger Conditions

Balancing is not free. Passive balancing dissipates energy as heat in resistors — typically 50–200 mW per cell, which adds 0.8–3.2W of heat into the module during balancing. Active balancing costs charge efficiency (typically 90–95% efficient). Both approaches reduce available charge time if done improperly.

The optimal balancing strategy for LFP at top of charge:

  1. During charging, monitor all cell voltages continuously
  2. When the first cell reaches V_balance_threshold (typically OVP − 50mV = 3.60V for LFP), enable the balancing resistor for that cell
  3. Continue charging at the same current — the balancing resistor diverts excess current for the high-voltage cell, allowing other cells to catch up
  4. When all cells reach the balance threshold, reduce charge current to the termination C-rate (C/20)
  5. When all cells reach OVP or charge current drops below the termination threshold, end charging
  6. Post-charge balancing: continue balancing for 30–60 minutes after charge ends (at zero current), to equalize residual SoC differences that were masked by IR drop during charging
⚖
Why Balancing During Charging (Not Discharging) Is More Effective

During charging, all cells are approaching their upper voltage limit. The cell with the highest SoC (smallest capacity or previously overcharged) hits the OVP threshold first. Balancing at this point — bleeding charge off the high cell — is energy-efficient because you're only dissipating the excess that would have been wasted anyway in the charge termination logic.

During discharging, the weakest cell hits the UVP threshold first. "Balancing" by supplying additional current to the weak cell from stronger cells (active balancing) extends runtime, but requires more complex circuitry and only helps if the imbalance is SoC-based (not capacity-based). A cell with lower capacity will always reach UVP first regardless of SoC balancing.

Passive Balancing Design — Resistor Sizing

Each cell in the passive balancing design has a dedicated bypass path: a resistor (R_bal) and a switch (usually a small MOSFET controlled by the BMS AFE). When enabled:

Passive Balancing Resistor Sizing I_bal = (V_cell − V_threshold) / R_bal P_bal = V_cell × I_bal (heat dissipated in resistor) Example: V_cell=3.62V, V_threshold=3.60V, R_bal=30Ω I_bal = 0.02/30 = 0.67 mA, P_bal = 3.62 × 0.00067 = 2.4 mW Very gentle balancing — appropriate for minor imbalance maintenance. For fast balancing of significantly imbalanced cells, R_bal = 5Ω → I_bal = 100–200mA, P_bal = 300–700 mW. Thermal design must account for this.

Temperature Effects on LFP Cell Performance

Temperature is the single most influential external variable in Li-ion cell behaviour. It affects capacity, internal resistance, safe operating rates, and calendar life simultaneously. For a BMS engineer, understanding the temperature dependencies is not optional — every protection threshold, every SoC correction, and every charging algorithm must be temperature-aware.

The Arrhenius Foundation — Why Temperature Matters Exponentially

Electrochemical reaction rates in a Li-ion cell obey the Arrhenius equation. The rate constant k for any reaction scales as:

Arrhenius Rate Law k(T) = A × exp(−Eₐ / R×T) A = pre-exponential factor, Eₐ = activation energy (J/mol), R = 8.314 J/mol·K, T = temperature in Kelvin. For Li⁺ transport through SEI layer, Eₐ ≈ 50–80 kJ/mol. A 10°C rise doubles the reaction rate.

This exponential dependence means that both beneficial reactions (ion transport, faster charge kinetics at high temperature) and harmful reactions (SEI growth, electrolyte decomposition) accelerate together. The BMS cannot capture the benefit without accepting accelerated aging — temperature management is always a trade-off.

Low-Temperature Effects — The Lithium Plating Cliff

Below 0°C, the graphite anode's ability to intercalate Li⁺ ions during charging is severely impaired. The Li⁺ ions arrive at the anode surface faster than they can be absorbed into the graphite lattice. When this happens, lithium deposits as metallic lithium on the anode surface — a process called lithium plating.

🧊
Lithium Plating — The Irreversible Failure Mode

Metallic lithium deposited on the anode does not re-intercalate cleanly. Some of it reacts with electrolyte to form "dead lithium" — isolated metallic islands encapsulated in SEI material. This permanently reduces lithium inventory and capacity. Some deposited lithium forms dendritic structures that grow across the separator and cause an internal short circuit — the root cause of many Li-ion fires and failures. At −20°C, charging is absolutely prohibited. At 0°C to 5°C, charging must be derated to ≤0.2C. The BMS enforces these limits via a temperature-indexed charge rate lookup table that overrides all other charge commands.

Low temperature also dramatically increases internal resistance. The ohmic resistance R₀ and the RC diffusion impedance R₁C₁ both increase because:

  • Ion conductivity in the electrolyte solution drops (viscosity increases)
  • Li⁺ desolvation energy at the electrode surface increases
  • SEI ionic conductance decreases
  • Solid-state diffusion in graphite slows (lower D_Li)

At −20°C, the internal resistance of an LFP cell is typically 3–4× higher than at 25°C. With a 280Ah prismatic cell having R₀ = 0.4 mΩ at 25°C, at −20°C R₀ ≈ 1.5 mΩ. At 100A discharge, the IR drop is 150 mV — enough to falsely trigger undervoltage protection before the cell is actually depleted.

Temperature-Corrected IR Drop R₀(T) = R₀_ref × exp[Eₐ_R/R × (1/T − 1/T_ref)] Eₐ_R ≈ 20–30 kJ/mol for LFP ohmic resistance. At T_ref = 298K (25°C) and T = 253K (−20°C): R₀(−20°C) ≈ R₀_ref × exp[25000/8.314 × (1/253 − 1/298)] ≈ R₀_ref × 3.2

At high temperature, electrolyte solvents (EC, DMC) begin oxidising at the cathode surface above ~60°C, generating CO₂ and CO gas. This is detectable as a rise in cell internal pressure. Prismatic cells with pressure vents will vent above a threshold — the BMS should interpret a sudden drop in internal resistance (gas between electrodes reduces contact) as a sign of thermal stress, even before temperature sensors respond.

Capacity Derating — The Temperature Lookup Table

Production BMS firmware implements temperature-indexed capacity derating factors to ensure SoC estimates remain accurate across the operating temperature range. The nominal capacity Q_nom is measured at 25°C, 0.2C. At any other temperature, the actual usable capacity is:

Temperature-Derated Capacity Q_usable(T) = Q_nom × f_T(T) f_T lookup table (LFP typical): T (°C) | f_T | Notes --------|-------|---------------------------------- −20 | 0.60 | Very limited — only for discharge −10 | 0.78 | Emergency discharge only 0 | 0.88 | Limited charging permitted (≤0.2C) 10 | 0.94 | Normal operations begin 25 | 1.00 | Reference (rated capacity) 35 | 0.99 | Negligible derating 45 | 0.97 | Monitor aging rate 55 | 0.93 | Alarm threshold, reduce load 60 | 0.88 | Hard cutoff recommended

This table is stored in MCU flash. At each SoC calculation cycle, the firmware reads the battery temperature, interpolates f_T, and uses Q_usable(T) as the denominator in the SoC formula instead of the fixed Q_nom. Without this correction, a cold pack reading 88% capacity would display as 88% SoC when the actual usable energy relative to rated is only 88% × 88% ≈ 77%.

Temperature Sensor Placement — Where to Measure

Temperature measurement location matters significantly. A single sensor on the battery management board will not reflect the temperature inside the cell core. The thermal gradient across a 280Ah prismatic cell can be 5–15°C between the cell surface and the core under high-rate discharge.

🌡
Best Practice: Multi-Point Temperature Monitoring
  • NTC thermistor at cell surface midpoint — catches bulk thermal events
  • NTC at terminal tab — catches tab/busbar resistance heating
  • NTC at pack inlet and outlet — monitors airflow/cooling effectiveness
  • PCB ambient sensor — reference for BMS IC derating

The BMS uses the maximum temperature reading across all sensors for protection decisions, and the average reading for SoC correction. These two values will diverge during high-rate events — this divergence itself is diagnostic information.

Worked Numerical Examples — Full Runtime Calculation

Abstract formulas become real when you run numbers through them. This section walks through three complete engineering calculations: a UPS runtime prediction, a SoC drift budget for a 24-hour monitoring cycle, and a cell balancing energy analysis. Each example uses the real parameters of a commercial 16S1P LFP pack.

Example 1 — UPS Runtime Prediction at Variable Load

System: 48V LFP pack, 16S1P, 280Ah cells, SOH = 92%, T = 15°C, initial SoC = 95%. Load profile: Critical load 800W for first 10 min, then 400W until cutoff.

Step 1 — Available Energy Q_usable = Q_nom × SOH × f_T(15°C) = 280 × 0.92 × 0.94 = 242.1 Ah E_usable = Q_usable × V_nom = 242.1 × 51.2V = 12,395 Wh E_available = E_usable × SoC_initial = 12,395 × 0.95 = 11,775 Wh
Step 2 — First 10 Minutes (800W load) I_load = P / V_pack = 800W / 51.2V = 15.6A V_terminal = OCV − I × R_pack = 51.2 − 15.6 × 6.4mΩ = 51.1V (negligible sag at low rate) E_consumed = 800W × (10/60)h = 133.3 Wh SoC after 10 min = 95% − (133.3 / 12,395) × 100% = 93.9%
Step 3 — Remaining Runtime at 400W E_remaining = 11,775 − 133.3 = 11,642 Wh t_remaining = E_remaining / P_load = 11,642 / 400 = 29.1 hours Total predicted runtime = 10 min + 29.1 h ≈ 29.3 hours at the 400W phase. This assumes no Peukert correction (0.055C rate is negligible) and constant V_nom. For high-accuracy prediction, integrate V_OCV(SoC) over the discharge curve.
📐
Why V_nom is a Safe Approximation for Runtime

Using V_nom = 51.2V for energy calculation introduces ≤3% error for LFP packs in the 20–90% SoC range because the flat OCV curve maintains voltage close to nominal throughout. For NMC (sloped OCV), you would need to integrate the OCV-SoC curve to get accurate energy. This is one of the underappreciated practical advantages of LFP's flat curve.

Example 2 — SoC Drift Budget Over 24 Hours

System: Same 16S1P pack. BMS uses coulomb counting with a 16-bit ADC current sensor (±0.05% gain error, ±0.5mA offset error). Pack idles with 50mA self-discharge. How much SoC error accumulates in 24 hours?

Error Budget Breakdown 1. Gain error contribution: ±0.05% × 50mA × 24h = ±0.6 mAh → ±0.6/280000 × 100% ≈ ±0.0002% 2. Offset error: ±0.5mA × 24h = ±12 mAh → ±12/280000 × 100% ≈ ±0.004% 3. Unmodelled self-discharge: 50mA × 24h = 1200 mAh → 1200/280000 × 100% = 0.43% 4. Temperature drift (0°C → 25°C, 25 ppm/°C): ±25ppm × 25°C × 50mA × 24h = ±0.75 mAh ≈ 0.0003% Dominant error term: unmodelled self-discharge at 0.43%/day. If the BMS does not account for self-discharge, SoC drifts by ~0.43% per day even with a perfect current sensor. Over 30 days idle storage: ~13% cumulative error — rendering coulomb counting unreliable without periodic OCV recalibration.
Self-Discharge Compensation I_sd(T) = I_sd_ref × exp[k_sd × (T − T_ref)] For LFP: I_sd_ref ≈ 0.02–0.05% SoC/day at 25°C Modern BMS firmware adds a background self-discharge current term to the coulomb counter even when no external current is flowing. This constant subtraction keeps SoC accurate during long idle periods — critical for UPS systems that may sit on float charge for months.
Example 3 — Passive Balancing Energy and Time

Scenario: A 16S pack has completed charging. Cell voltages range from 3.62V (min) to 3.65V (max). The BMS uses passive balancing with R_bal = 10Ω resistors. How long to balance? How much energy is dissipated?

Balancing Current and Power ΔV = V_max − V_min = 3.65 − 3.62 = 30 mV I_bal = ΔV / R_bal = 30mV / 10Ω = 3 mA P_bal = I_bal² × R_bal = (0.003)² × 10 = 90 μW per cell
Balancing Time ΔQ = C_cell × ΔV / (dV/dSoC) In LFP plateau (dV/dSoC ≈ 0.5mV/% → 0.5mV per 2.8Ah), ΔV = 30mV → ΔQ ≈ 168 mAh t_bal = ΔQ / I_bal = 168 mAh / 3 mA = 56 hours This reveals a fundamental limitation of passive top balancing: 56 hours to correct a 30 mV imbalance. In practice, balancing occurs in parallel with charging (extending charge time) or during rest periods. The 10Ω resistor choice must balance heat dissipation (90 μW × 16 cells = 1.44 mW total — negligible) against balancing speed. Larger packs with 100mV imbalances may take several days — motivating active balancing for high-power applications.
2.10

BMS Commissioning — Practical First-Power Checklist

Theory becomes hardware when the pack is assembled on the bench for the first time. This section walks through the commissioning procedure that production engineers follow before a new BMS design is trusted with a live pack — covering pre-power checks, initial calibration, protection threshold verification, and SoC initialisation.

Phase 1 — Pre-Power Verification (No Cells Connected)
⚠
Always Verify Before Connecting Cells

LFP cells at full charge present ~3.65V per cell, or 58.4V for a 16S pack. This is a live energy source with fault energy sufficient to destroy PCB traces and start fires if the BMS has a wiring error. Pre-power checks on the bare PCB with a bench supply at reduced voltage (5V across the logic supply) catch most faults safely before the pack is committed.

  • Verify all cell-sense wire connections match the schematic — check with ohmmeter, all sense resistances should match expected topology
  • Verify thermistor resistance at room temperature matches NTC datasheet (e.g. 10kΩ NTC: ~10kΩ at 25°C)
  • Confirm current sensor orientation — positive current should read positive on shunt/Hall sensor
  • Inspect balancing resistor values with ohmmeter before first power
  • Verify protection FET drain-source leakage is within spec (typically <1 μA)
  • Check 5V and 3.3V logic rail regulation with bench supply before cell connection
Phase 2 — Initial SoC Calibration

The BMS's SoC estimate is only as good as its initial condition. On first power with cells at an unknown SoC, the BMS must perform an OCV-based SoC initialisation:

Boot SoC Initialisation Protocol 1. Apply I = 0 (open circuit) for t_rest ≥ 30 min 2. Read V_cell for all N cells 3. Check ΔV_max − ΔV_min < 50mV (cell balance acceptable) 4. Map V_OCV → SoC via lookup table for each cell 5. Set SoC_initial = average of all cell SoC values 6. Flag cells with SoC deviation >5% for investigation Why 30 minutes? After a discharge or charge event, surface concentration gradients persist in the electrodes. The OCV measured immediately after current stops includes a diffusion overpotential that decays with time constant τ ≈ R₁ × C₁ ≈ 100–300s. 30 minutes ensures the cell has fully relaxed to true thermodynamic OCV.
Phase 3 — Protection Threshold Verification

Every protection threshold must be verified by deliberately triggering it under controlled conditions. This cannot be done in simulation — the hardware path (AFE → MCU → FET gate driver) must be exercised end-to-end.

Protection Test Method Pass Criteria Typical Response Time
OVPOvervoltage Charge at C/10, let cell voltage reach threshold (typically 3.65V + 50mV) Charge FET opens within response time, alarm asserted <100 ms (hardware comparator path)
UVPUndervoltage Discharge at C/10, let lowest cell reach threshold (typically 2.8V) Discharge FET opens, alarm asserted, SoC ≤ 5% <100 ms
OCPOvercurrent Use a programmable load to ramp current to 1.2× OCP threshold Both FETs open within response time, latch set <5 ms (hardware latch path)
SCPShort Circuit Apply low-resistance load (0.1Ω) via relay with known closing time FET opens within SCP response time (typically <500 μs) <500 μs (dedicated SCP comparator)
OTPOvertemperature Use heat gun on thermistor, monitor BMS response Charge derated at 45°C warning, full cutoff at 60°C limit <1 s (temperature loop bandwidth)
UTPUndertemperature Place thermistor in freezer water, connect to BMS Charge blocked at 0°C, full cutoff at −20°C <1 s
⚠
SCP Test Safety Note

Short circuit protection testing involves intentionally creating a short circuit on a live pack. This must be done in a fire-safe enclosure with PPE, using a relay-switched known resistance (not a direct wire short). The test verifies that the BMS's hardware SCP path responds before the cells' own internal resistance limits current to a safe level — it does not verify that the cells survive an unlimited short.

Phase 4 — SoC Accuracy Verification via Reference Discharge

After protection thresholds are confirmed, perform a full Reference Performance Test (RPT) to calibrate the SoC estimator against a known ground truth:

RPT Protocol (IEC 62620 basis) 1. Charge to 100% SoC (CC-CV, 0.5C, CV until I < 0.05C) 2. Rest 30 minutes 3. Discharge at 0.2C to cutoff voltage (2.5V per cell) 4. Record Q_actual = ∫I dt (coulomb count with calibrated reference meter) 5. Q_actual is the ground-truth capacity. Compare to BMS-reported capacity. 6. Update Q_nominal in BMS firmware if error >2% The RPT should be repeated every 6 months as a scheduled SOH check. A steadily declining Q_actual tracks capacity fade — when Q_actual < 0.8 × Q_rated, the pack has reached end-of-life per IEC 62619.
