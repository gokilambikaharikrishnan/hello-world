Section 2.1

What Makes a Battery? First Principles

Every engineer who works with batteries benefits from a clear mental model of what is actually happening inside the cell. Not just "electrochemistry" as a black box, but the specific physical processes — electron flow, ion migration, crystal lattice changes — that determine every measurable parameter the BMS must track and protect.

The Electrochemical Cell — Four Essential Components

Every rechargeable battery cell is an electrochemical cell: a device that converts chemical potential energy into electrical energy through redox reactions at two electrodes separated by an ion-conducting electrolyte. Four components are essential, and each has a precise, non-negotiable role.

Anode (−)
LiC₆
Negative electrode. Oxidation occurs here on discharge — Li⁺ released, electrons emitted into external circuit. In Li-ion: graphite hosting lithium.
Electrolyte
LiPF₆ / EC:DMC
Ion highway. Carries Li⁺ between electrodes. Conducts ions but NOT electrons — forces electrons through external circuit. Liquid organic solvent.
Cathode (+)
LiFePO₄
Positive electrode. Reduction occurs here on discharge — Li⁺ absorbed, electrons received from external circuit. In LFP: lithium iron phosphate crystal.
Separator
PE/PP Membrane
Physical barrier. Prevents electronic short circuit while allowing Li⁺ ions through micropores. Typically 15–25 μm thick. Melts above 130°C (safety valve).
🔑
The Critical Distinction: Electrons vs. Ions

The electrolyte conducts lithium ions (Li⁺) but blocks electrons. This is the entire operating principle. Because electrons cannot flow through the electrolyte, they are forced through the external circuit (your load). That forced electron flow is the current you use. Remove the external circuit and the cell has no way to equilibrate — it holds its charge indefinitely (ignoring self-discharge). This is why "open-circuit voltage" is a stable, meaningful measurement.

Where voltage actually comes from: Cell voltage equals the electrochemical potential difference between cathode and anode — the Gibbs free energy released per mole of reaction, divided by the charge transferred. At standard conditions:

Cell Voltage — Origin in Reduction Potentials E_cell = E°_cathode − E°_anode LFP: E°_cathode (Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ in PO₄ lattice) ≈ +3.45 V vs Li/Li⁺ E°_anode (Li⁺/LiC₆ graphite) ≈ +0.15 V vs Li/Li⁺ → E_cell ≈ 3.45 − 0.15 = 3.30 V nominal The ~0.15V anode potential varies slightly with SoC. The Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ redox couple in the phosphate lattice pins the plateau. This is why LFP voltage barely changes with SoC.
Intercalation — What Physically Happens When a Li-ion Cell Charges

The word "intercalation" comes from the Latin intercalare — to insert between. In lithium-ion batteries, it describes the process by which lithium ions are inserted into (or extracted from) the crystal lattice of an electrode material, without permanently altering that material's structure.

Think of it like this: imagine a stack of pancakes. Each pancake is a graphene layer in the graphite anode. The gaps between pancakes are where Li⁺ ions park during charging. When you charge the battery, Li⁺ ions squeeze between the graphene layers and sit there, electrostatically held. When you discharge, they unsqueeze and leave. The pancakes (graphene layers) barely change shape — just expand slightly (3.5% for graphite) when the Li⁺ ions are inside.

This is fundamentally different from lead-acid, where the electrode is consumed in the reaction (lead converts to lead sulfate). In intercalation, the electrode hosts lithium reversibly — like a hotel that houses guests without changing its structure. This reversibility is why Li-ion achieves 10× more cycles than lead-acid.

Discharge — Following a Li⁺ Ion on Its Journey

Start: the cell is fully charged. The anode is a graphite crystal fully loaded with Li⁺ ions between its layers (LiC₆). The cathode is FePO₄ — fully depleted lithium iron phosphate, all Li⁺ slots empty.

You connect a load (a lamp). Here is what happens at the atomic scale in the next microseconds:

  • Oxidation at anode: A Li⁺ ion deintercalates from the graphite anode. The graphite simultaneously releases one electron. The Li⁺ enters the electrolyte. The electron enters the copper current collector, flows through the external circuit (through your lamp, doing work), arrives at the aluminum current collector.
  • Ion migration: The Li⁺ ion migrates through the electrolyte — pulled toward the cathode by the electric field gradient. This migration is rate-limited by ionic conductivity and the Li⁺ diffusion coefficient in the electrolyte (~10⁻⁶ cm²/s at 25°C). This is why charge rate has limits — you cannot push ions faster than diffusion allows.
  • Reduction at cathode: The Li⁺ ion reaches the LFP cathode and intercalates into an empty site in the FePO₄ crystal. Simultaneously, the electron arriving from the external circuit reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺, maintaining charge neutrality. The crystal site now holds LiFePO₄.
  • Result: One Li⁺ has moved from anode to cathode. One electron has flowed through the external circuit. The process repeats approximately 6×10²³ times per mole of lithium (Avogadro's number) to fully discharge the cell.
Complete LFP Discharge Reaction Anode: LiC₆ → C₆ + Li⁺ + e⁻ Cathode: FePO₄ + Li⁺ + e⁻ → LiFePO₄ Overall: LiC₆ + FePO₄ → C₆ + LiFePO₄ (ΔG° ≈ −309 kJ/mol) Negative ΔG° = spontaneous reaction = energy is released. Charge reaction is the exact reverse, requiring energy input equal to ΔG°.
Charge — Reversing the Process

During charging, an external power supply forces electrons backward — from the aluminum current collector (cathode) through the supply and into the copper collector (anode). This non-spontaneous electron flow drives the non-spontaneous chemistry: Li⁺ is pulled out of the LiFePO₄ cathode (Fe²⁺ oxidized back to Fe³⁺), travels through the electrolyte, and reintercalates into the graphite anode. The energy you put in (from the charger) is stored as chemical potential.

The efficiency of this round trip is the coulombic efficiency (η ≈ 99–99.9% for LFP) — almost all charge put in can be recovered. The small fraction lost (0.1–1%) goes to side reactions, primarily continued SEI layer growth on the anode. Over thousands of cycles, this tiny loss per cycle accumulates into measurable capacity fade — the subject of Section 2.7.

⚛
Why BMS Designers Must Understand Intercalation Kinetics

Intercalation is not instantaneous. Li⁺ diffusion into the electrode particle is governed by Fick's second law, with diffusion coefficients ranging from 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹¹ m²/s depending on material and temperature. At high charge rates (≥2C), Li⁺ cannot penetrate deep into the particle before more arrives — concentration polarization builds up at the particle surface, and local surface potential rises above the anode equilibrium potential, causing lithium plating instead of intercalation.

This is why the BMS must enforce charge rate limits — not as arbitrary conservative specs, but because exceeding them initiates a physically distinct, damaging reaction. At low temperature, diffusion slows further, dramatically lowering the safe charge rate threshold. A cell that accepts 0.5C safely at 25°C may plate lithium at just 0.05C at −10°C.

Section 2.2

LFP Cell Specifically

Chapter 1 made the case for LFP over other chemistries. Now we go deeper into the LFP cell itself — the exact reaction equations, the physical meaning of its voltage limits, and a precise electrochemical comparison against the competing chemistries every BMS engineer should understand.

The LFP Cathode Reaction — Step by Step

Lithium iron phosphate adopts the olivine crystal structure (orthorhombic, space group Pnma). The iron atoms occupy octahedral sites coordinated by six oxygen atoms. The phosphorus atoms sit in tetrahedral PO₄ groups. Lithium ions occupy one-dimensional channels (M1 sites) that run parallel to the b-axis — the highway along which Li⁺ ions must travel during intercalation.

Unlike NMC and NCA, where lithium extraction creates a continuous solid-solution (the Li content varies smoothly with SoC), LFP follows a two-phase mechanism. There are only two stable phases:

  • LiFePO₄ — the fully lithiated phase (discharged cathode). Iron is in the +2 oxidation state (Fe²⁺). Unit cell volume: ~291 ų.
  • FePO₄ — the delithiated phase (charged cathode). Iron is in the +3 oxidation state (Fe³⁺). Unit cell volume: ~272 ų.

During charging, a phase boundary moves through each LFP particle from the outside inward, converting LiFePO₄ → FePO₄. During discharge, the boundary moves outward, converting FePO₄ → LiFePO₄. While both phases coexist in a particle, the thermodynamic equilibrium potential is fixed at ~3.45V vs Li/Li⁺ — the potential of the FePO₄/LiFePO₄ phase boundary. This is what causes the flat OCV plateau.

LFP Cathode Half-Reaction Discharge: FePO₄ + Li⁺ + e⁻ → LiFePO₄ (Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺) Charge: LiFePO₄ → FePO₄ + Li⁺ + e⁻ (Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺) The Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ redox couple has E° ≈ +3.45V vs Li/Li⁺. This determines the plateau voltage. No oxygen atoms are ever released — PO₄ holds them covalently at 544 kJ/mol.
LFP Full Cell Reaction (with graphite anode) Charge: LiFePO₄ + C₆ → FePO₄ + LiC₆ Discharge: FePO₄ + LiC₆ → LiFePO₄ + C₆ Volume change during full cycling: ~6.8% for the LFP cathode. Compare to NMC (high-Ni): 10–15%. Lower volume change = less mechanical stress = more cycles.
LFP Voltage Window — Physical Meaning of 2.5V to 3.65V

The BMS enforces a voltage window of 2.5 V – 3.65 V per cell. These are not arbitrary safety margins. Each limit corresponds to a specific physical event in the cell.

⬆
Upper Limit: 3.65 V — Why This Number

At 3.65V, the LFP cathode is fully delithiated (all sites converted to FePO₄). There are no more empty Li⁺ slots in the cathode crystal. Continued charging cannot do useful electrochemistry. Instead:

Above ~3.75V: Electrolyte begins oxidizing at the cathode surface, forming a growing CEI (cathode-electrolyte interphase) layer. This permanently increases impedance. Above ~4.0V: Iron dissolution begins — Fe³⁺ ions dissolve from the FePO₄ lattice into the electrolyte, migrate to the anode, and deposit as iron metal dendrites that can puncture the separator. Above ~4.2V on the anode side: lithium plating occurs — metallic lithium dendrites form.

The 3.65V cutoff provides ~100mV margin above the plateau, balancing full capacity utilization against electrolyte stability. Set it to 3.75V and you gain a few percent capacity but accelerate aging by 2–5× in the cells that chronically reach it.

⬇
Lower Limit: 2.5 V — Copper Dissolution Threshold

The anode copper current collector is electrochemically stable as long as the anode potential remains low. As the cell discharges deeply, the anode potential rises toward the standard potential of the Cu/Cu²⁺ couple (+0.34V vs SHE, or approximately +3.4V vs Li/Li⁺ at the anode). At cell terminal voltages below ~2.5V, the copper collector begins to oxidize — Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻.

Cu²⁺ ions dissolve into the electrolyte, migrate to the cathode, and deposit on the LFP surface, blocking Li⁺ intercalation sites. They also deposit on the separator, potentially nucleating dendritic shorts. Even a single 0V event can deposit enough copper to measurably increase impedance and reduce capacity. Repeated deep discharges below 2.5V are cumulative and irreversible.

Nominal voltage (3.2–3.3V) vs. operating range: The nominal voltage is the average voltage during a standard 0.2C discharge at 25°C — this is the voltage you use for energy calculations (Wh = Ah × V_nominal). It is not the open-circuit voltage at any particular SoC; it is the time-averaged value across the discharge curve. For LFP, the flat plateau means this average closely matches the plateau potential.

LFP vs. NMC — why nominal voltage differs: NMC uses a layered oxide cathode (LiNiₓMnᵧCoᵤO₂). The Ni²⁺/Ni⁴⁺ and Co²⁺/Co³⁺ redox couples operate at higher potential than Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺. This gives NMC a nominal voltage of ~3.6–3.7V vs LFP's 3.2V — a 12–15% higher energy per cell. Higher voltage is why NMC achieves higher energy density despite similar cathode mass.

Section 2.3

Cell Formats — Cylindrical, Prismatic, Pouch

The same electrode chemistry (LFP) can be packaged in three fundamentally different physical form factors. The choice of format is not cosmetic — it determines thermal management requirements, manufacturing cost, pack integration complexity, cycle life, and safety behaviour. For UPS and home ESS, the choice matters enormously.

Cylindrical Cells — 18650, 21700, 4680

Cylindrical cells are wound — a long strip of anode material, separator, cathode material, and another separator is wound around a mandrel like a roll of paper towel, then inserted into a steel can. The numbers in the name are the dimensions: an 18650 is 18 mm diameter × 65 mm length. A 21700 is 21 mm × 70 mm. A 4680 is 46 mm × 80 mm.

Why those numbers matter: Volume scales as r²×h, so going from 18650 to 4680 increases cell volume by a factor of ~(23/9)² × (80/65) ≈ 6.9×. Tesla's 4680 cell contains approximately 5–6× more energy than an 18650, but far fewer cells are needed per pack — reducing tab welding complexity and associated failure modes.

Cylindrical cells have inherent mechanical strength from the steel can. The wound structure means current must flow from the jelly roll core to the terminals through the winding — this creates moderate internal resistance compared to prismatic designs. The cylindrical geometry creates inefficient packing in rectangular module designs (air gaps between cylinders).

🌡
Cylindrical Thermal Profile

Heat is generated throughout the jelly roll but must escape radially through the steel can to reach the cooling surface. For small cells (18650), the thermal path is short. For large-format cylinders (4680), the center can run significantly hotter than the surface. At high C-rates, center-to-surface temperature gradients of 15–20°C are possible, accelerating degradation non-uniformly. BMS thermal management must account for this internal gradient, not just the surface temperature.

Prismatic Hard-Case Cells — The ESS Standard

Prismatic cells stack the electrode layers (rather than winding them) inside a rigid aluminum rectangular enclosure. The layers are either stacked flat or wound flat (a "flat wound" or "Z-fold" design). Typical prismatic LFP cells for ESS: 100Ah, 200Ah, 280Ah, 304Ah — sizes that have become de facto standards driven by Chinese manufacturers (CATL, EVE, CALB, Lishen).

The 280Ah prismatic LFP cell (~173 × 72 × 205 mm, ~620g) has become the dominant format for DIY and commercial home ESS globally. At 3.2V nominal, one cell stores 896 Wh. A 16-cell series string (48V nominal) stores approximately 14.3 kWh — enough for a typical home's evening energy consumption.

  • Low internal resistance due to large electrode area and short current path to terminals
  • Good thermal conductivity through flat aluminum case — efficient cooling with side plates
  • Efficient volumetric packing in rectangular modules — no air gaps
  • Flat external surfaces accommodate compression fixtures — critical for managing cell swelling
  • Large format = fewer cells per pack = fewer welds, connectors, and potential failure points
  • Standardized dimensions allow commodity pricing and interchangeable sourcing
  • Large format means a single cell failure can hold significant energy — fault management critical
  • Cell swelling (3–5% expansion over life) requires mechanical constraint in module design
  • Individual cell replacement in a module requires disassembly — field maintenance is harder
Pouch Cells — Maximum Energy Density, Minimum Rigidity

Pouch cells stack or layer electrodes inside a flexible aluminum-laminate polymer bag (the "pouch"). No metal can — the bag conforms to the cell contents. This eliminates ~15–20% of the weight of a cylindrical or prismatic format, delivering the highest gravimetric and volumetric energy density of the three formats.

Pouch cells are dominant in consumer electronics (smartphones, tablets) and some EV applications (GM Bolt, Nissan Leaf original). For ESS, they have significant disadvantages:

  • No structural rigidity — requires external compression frames that add weight and cost
  • Cell swelling is unconstrained without external pressure — pouch "pillowing" can rupture the laminate
  • The flexible pouch provides no mechanical protection against puncture or vibration
  • Tabs and bus bars connecting to external circuits are typically thin and fragile
  • Higher manufacturing complexity — sealing is sensitive to contamination

For UPS and home ESS: prismatic cells are the right choice. The combination of large format, high capacity, rigid construction, efficient thermal management, and commodity availability makes prismatic LFP the universal selection for stationary ESS applications.

Section 2.4

Vcell Behaviour — The Heart of BMS Design

The voltage of a lithium-ion cell is not a simple linear function of its charge state. It is a complex, temperature-dependent, rate-dependent, history-dependent signal that encodes multiple overlapping phenomena simultaneously. Understanding this in depth is not optional for BMS engineers — the entire architecture of SOC estimation, protection logic, and health monitoring depends on it.

Open-Circuit Voltage (OCV) — The Thermodynamic Truth

The open-circuit voltage is the cell terminal voltage measured when no current flows and the cell has been at rest long enough for all internal concentration gradients to equilibrate. It is the thermodynamic quantity — the true electrochemical potential difference between cathode and anode at the current state of lithiation.

OCV is the most reliable voltage measurement you can make. It is not affected by ohmic drop (no current → no IR drop), not affected by diffusion polarization (equilibrated), and highly reproducible at the same temperature and SoC. This is why OCV is the gold standard for SOC calibration — if you can measure OCV, you can look up SoC in a pre-characterized table.

The catch: you can only measure true OCV when no current is flowing and you have waited long enough. For LFP cells, "long enough" is surprisingly long — 30 minutes to 4+ hours, depending on temperature and how recently the cell was disturbed. The reason is slow Li⁺ diffusion equilibration within electrode particles after current stops.

THE FLAT CURVE PROBLEM — LFP's Central Challenge
📉
This Is the Most Important Topic in LFP BMS Design

The flat OCV curve is not a minor inconvenience. It is the defining technical challenge of LFP battery management. Every SOC estimation algorithm, every calibration strategy, every design decision in the BMS firmware is shaped by this single property. If you understand nothing else from Chapter 2, understand this.

Recall from the electrochemistry: LFP operates via a two-phase mechanism. While both FePO₄ and LiFePO₄ phases coexist in the electrode particles, the cell potential is pinned at the equilibrium potential of the phase boundary: ~3.35–3.38V. This pinning holds from approximately 20% SoC to 90% SoC — covering 70% of the cell's usable range.

The result: a real LFP cell's OCV varies by only 50 mV across the 20–80% SoC range where you actually operate the cell most of the time.

🔢
Numerical Proof: 3 mV Voltage Error = 15–20% SOC Error in LFP

Suppose your BMS reads a rested LFP cell at 3.341 V. From the table above, this could be 40% SoC (3.338V) or 50% SoC (3.340V) or 60% SoC (3.345V). A 3 mV measurement error spans 30% of SoC — from 40% to 70%. For a 280Ah cell, that is a 84 Ah uncertainty (268 Wh). For a 16-cell 48V string, it is 4.3 kWh of unknown energy.

For a UPS claiming "4 hours of backup at 100W," this uncertainty means the actual runtime could be 1.6 to 5.6 hours — completely unacceptable. This is why a simple voltage-based SOC meter, adequate for lead-acid, fails completely for LFP.
