4.1

Voltage Protection

Cell voltage is the primary health signal in a Li-ion pack. Every protection decision — from OVP to UVP — is derived from measuring voltage accurately at the per-cell level. This section explains why per-cell monitoring is non-negotiable, what physics occurs at the threshold boundaries, and how the BMS enforces them.

Why Per-Cell Voltage Monitoring is Non-Negotiable

Pack voltage hides weak cells. This is the single most important lesson in BMS design.

⚡ The Hidden Weak Cell Problem — 14S Pack Example

Cell 9 (red, 2.3V) is below the 2.5V UVP threshold and being damaged by copper dissolution. The 13 healthy cells at 3.5V produce a pack total of 47.8V — well within the expected 14S range of 45.5–49V. A pack-level BMS never sees the fault. Only per-cell AFE monitoring catches it.

⚡
Pack Voltage Measurement Cannot Replace Per-Cell Monitoring

A 14S LFP pack has a nominal voltage range of 35V (empty) to 51.1V (full). One cell at 2.3V (deeply discharged, damaged) while others are at 3.5V gives a pack voltage of 47.8V — a "normal" reading. The damaged cell continues to be discharged, dissolving copper current collector material into the electrolyte. Copper deposits then plate onto the separator, creating internal shorts. This failure chain is invisible without per-cell voltage monitoring.

Overvoltage Protection (OVP) — The 3.65V Hard Wall

LFP cells must not be charged above 3.65V per cell. Above this threshold, the cathode chemistry begins to destabilise in ways that cause permanent, cumulative damage.

LFP OVP Threshold Physics Normal charge ceiling: V_cell ≤ 3.65V (CC-CV target) OVP alarm: V_cell > 3.65V → stop charge, log event OVP hard cutoff: V_cell > 3.70V → open charge FET immediately OVP recovery hysteresis: do not allow charge resume until V_cell < 3.60V Hysteresis prevents rapid FET cycling (chattering) near the threshold. Without hysteresis, the FET would open at 3.65V, voltage sags to 3.64V, FET closes, voltage rises to 3.65V again — thousands of cycles per second, destroying the gate driver. The 50mV hysteresis window gives the charger time to ramp down gracefully.

What physically happens above 3.65V:

  • 3.65–3.80V: Electrolyte oxidation at cathode surface accelerates — CO₂ generation begins, internal pressure builds, SEI growth rate increases 5–10×
  • 3.80–4.00V: Lithium plating begins on cathode (Li deposits as metal, not intercalated) — irreversible capacity loss begins
  • Above 4.00V: Rapid gas generation, possible venting; cathode structural degradation accelerates sharply
📊 OVP Event — Voltage, Current, and FET State Timeline

Cell voltage rises during charge (blue). At 3.65V the BMS asserts OVP and opens the charge FET within <1s (firmware path) or <100ms (hardware comparator path). Current drops to zero. Voltage relaxes toward OCV (~3.55–3.60V) as the surface concentration gradient dissipates.

Undervoltage Protection (UVP) — The 2.5V Floor

The UVP threshold of 2.5V is chosen because below this point, the copper current collector on the anode begins to dissolve — an irreversible failure mechanism with no recovery path.

⚠
Copper Dissolution — The UVP Physical Basis

At voltages below ~2.0V, Cu²⁺ ions dissolve from the copper anode current collector into the electrolyte. These ions migrate toward the cathode and plate as metallic copper dendrites. These dendrites penetrate the separator, creating a permanent internal short circuit. A cell that has been deeply discharged to 0V then recharged will exhibit dramatically increased self-discharge (the internal short slowly drains it) and may fail catastrophically on the next charge cycle. The UVP threshold of 2.5V provides a 500mV safety margin above the copper dissolution onset.

UVP Thresholds and Recovery Procedure UVP warning: V_cell < 2.8V → reduce load, alert host system UVP hard cutoff: V_cell < 2.5V → open discharge FET immediately Deep discharge: V_cell < 2.0V → flag for inspection, pre-charge required Recovery hysteresis: charge resume only after V_cell > 2.8V (post pre-charge) If a cell reaches UVP, the BMS must not allow normal CC charge to resume immediately. The pre-charge sequence (see Section 4.4) first applies a low current (C/20) until all cells are above 2.8V, then transitions to normal CC-CV charge. This safely brings deeply discharged cells back without triggering lithium plating from high-rate charge on a partially failed SEI.

Voltage Measurement Accuracy — Why 16-bit AFE Matters

The OVP and UVP thresholds are tight windows. OVP fires at 3.65V; normal full charge is 3.65V — only a few millivolts separate "charged" from "fault". The AFE must measure with enough resolution to avoid both false positives and missed faults.

AFE Resolution Calculation Full voltage range per cell: 2.0V to 4.0V = 2.0V span 12-bit ADC: 2.0V / 4096 = 0.49 mV/LSB → ±1 mV accuracy (2 LSB) 16-bit ADC: 2.0V / 65536 = 0.031 mV/LSB → ±0.1 mV accuracy A 12-bit measurement gives ±1 mV. In the LFP plateau where dOCV/dSoC ≈ 0.2 mV/%, this ±1 mV translates to ±5% SoC uncertainty. A 16-bit ADC reduces this to ±0.5% SoC — critical for accurate balancing decisions. Production AFE ICs (TI BQ76952, ADI LTC6813) use 16-bit delta-sigma ADCs with integrated calibration references to achieve ±1.0 mV absolute accuracy across temperature.
4.2

Current Protection

Overcurrent and short-circuit faults are the most energetically violent events a BMS must handle. The response time requirements span four orders of magnitude — from milliseconds for short-circuit detection to seconds for charge overcurrent — each tier matched to the physical threat it addresses.

The Response Time Hierarchy — Why Each Tier Exists
<500μs
HARDWARE PATH
Short-Circuit Discharge (SCD)
Current can reach 50–200× rated (50kA on a large pack). Cell temperature would reach runaway in under 1 second. Hardware comparator fires directly — MCU is bypassed entirely. Cannot rely on software interrupt latency.
<5ms
HW + SW PATH

High transient current (3–5× rated, <10s). Joule heating is significant but not immediately catastrophic. AFE hardware latch with MCU confirmation. Allows UPS inrush and motor-start bursts within the pulse window.
<100ms
SW PATH
Overcurrent Discharge — Continuous (OCD-C)
Sustained current above 1C. Thermal accumulation over seconds drives cell temperature up. MCU software loop detects, commands FET open. 100ms response is fast enough to prevent thermal damage.
<1s

Overcurrent Charge (OCC)
Charger delivering excess current. No immediate catastrophic risk — overcharge is slow. MCU detects, sends current-limit command to charger via CAN/PWM, or opens charge FET. 1s response is acceptable.
Short-Circuit Discharge — The 500μs Hard Requirement

A hard short circuit across a 16S LFP pack (58V nominal) through a low-resistance fault (e.g. dropped wrench, wiring insulation failure) produces current limited only by the pack's internal resistance. For a 16S pack with R_int = 8 mΩ total:

Short-Circuit Current Calculation I_sc = V_pack / R_fault_total R_fault_total = R_pack_int + R_wiring + R_fault Example: 58V / (8mΩ + 2mΩ + 0.5mΩ) = 58V / 10.5mΩ ≈ 5,500A 5,500A into a 280Ah pack: Joule heat = I²×R = 5500² × 0.008 = 242,000W = 242 kW. At this power, the cell temperature rises at roughly 44,000°C/s (adiabatic) — thermal runaway in under 10ms. The SCD circuit must fire in <500μs to interrupt current before FET and busbar damage occurs. Software loops running at 1kHz (1ms period) cannot achieve this — only a dedicated hardware comparator can.
⚡ SCD Hardware Path — Why Software Cannot Protect Fast Enough

Software path (top): ADC sample → MCU interrupt → FET command ≈ 1–2ms. Too slow — cell damage begins in <1ms at short-circuit current. Hardware path (bottom): dedicated comparator monitors shunt voltage continuously and latches the gate driver directly, bypassing the MCU entirely. Response <500μs. The MCU is notified afterwards via an interrupt flag.

💡
SCD Latch Behaviour — Manual Reset Required

After an SCD event, the BMS enters a latched fault state — both charge and discharge FETs stay open and will not close without a deliberate reset sequence (power cycle or CAN command from a authorised host). This is intentional: an SCD event indicates a potentially dangerous external condition (short circuit). Automatically reconnecting after a short-circuit without human inspection would re-expose a potentially live short fault. The latch also prevents thermal runaway from repeated reconnection attempts into a persistent fault.

Overcurrent Discharge — Continuous vs Pulse Ratings
LFP Overcurrent Limits (280Ah prismatic cell) Continuous discharge: I_max_cont = 1C = 280A (indefinite, T < 45°C) Pulse discharge: I_max_pulse = 3C = 840A (≤10s, T < 55°C) OCD-C threshold: I > 1.1 × I_max_cont for > 5s → open FET OCD-P threshold: I > 3.2 × I_max_cont for > 50ms → open FET The 10% headroom (1.1×) on continuous OCD prevents nuisance trips from measurement noise and normal load transients. The pulse threshold at 3.2C allows UPS switchover inrush, motor starting, and capacitor charging loads that last tens of milliseconds but are thermally benign due to their short duration.

Current Sensing — Shunt Resistor vs Hall Effect
⊕Shunt Resistor HIGH ACCURACY
Principle: V_shunt = I × R_shunt. Ohm's law — the simplest possible sensor.
Typical R: 0.5–2 mΩ (low to minimise power loss at high current)
Accuracy: ±0.1–0.5% with 16-bit differential ADC
Power loss at 280A: P = I²R = 280² × 0.001 = 78.4W (1mΩ shunt)
Pro: No zero-drift, DC accurate, fast response (<1μs)
Con: Not isolated from pack voltage — common-mode rejection needed. Power loss.
⊕Hall Effect Sensor ISOLATED
Principle: Current through conductor creates magnetic field → Hall voltage proportional to I
Isolation: Fully galvanic — no connection to high-voltage pack bus
Accuracy: ±1–3% (core saturation, offset drift, temperature sensitivity)
Power loss: Near zero — senses field, not current
Pro: Safe for high-voltage packs, no insertion loss, wide range
Con: DC offset drift over temperature (±2–5A at 25°C→85°C), slower response
📐
Production Choice: Shunt for BMS, Hall for System-Level Monitoring

Most production BMS designs use a shunt resistor (0.5–1 mΩ, ±0.1% tolerance, low TCR) for the primary current measurement feeding the SoC estimator and SCD comparator. The key advantage is DC accuracy — Hall sensors drift at 0°C and above 60°C, introducing exactly the kind of offset error that ruins coulomb counting. A secondary Hall-effect sensor is sometimes added for system-level (pack-to-inverter) monitoring where isolation from the high-voltage bus is required for safety reasons.

4.3

Cell Balancing

No two cells are identical. Manufacturing tolerances, temperature gradients, and differing self-discharge rates cause cell voltages to drift apart over hundreds of cycles. Without balancing, the weakest cell determines pack capacity while the strongest risks overcharge. The BMS must continuously correct this drift.

Why Cells Drift — Four Root Causes
±2%
Manufacturing Capacity Spread
Grade-B cells: ±2–5% capacity variation off the production line. Even "matched" cells diverge over 500+ cycles.
2×
Temperature Gradient Aging
10°C difference across pack → cells age at 2× different rates → progressive capacity imbalance growing each year.
0.05%
Self-Discharge Variation
Cell-to-cell self-discharge varies ±20%. Over 30 days idle, this produces ~1% SoC imbalance even with perfect initial balance.
∑
Compounding Over Cycles
Each cycle, imbalance compounds. A 1% initial spread can grow to 10–15% over 1000 cycles without active balancing.
📊 Impact of Cell Imbalance — Pack Capacity Loss Visualised

Left: balanced pack — all cells reach UVP simultaneously, using full capacity. Right: imbalanced pack — cell 4 (short bar) hits UVP cutoff while other cells have 30% charge remaining. That 30% energy is stranded and unusable. Balancing directly recovers this lost capacity.

Passive Balancing — Burn the Excess

Passive balancing bleeds energy from the highest-voltage cells through a resistor until all cells match the lowest. Simple, reliable, and the industry standard for ESS applications.

Passive Balancing Equations I_bal = (V_cell − V_threshold) / R_bal P_bal = I_bal² × R_bal = (V_cell − V_threshold)² / R_bal t_bal = ΔQ / I_bal where ΔQ = capacity mismatch in Ah Typical R_bal = 10–47Ω. At ΔV = 50mV and R_bal = 10Ω: I_bal = 50mV / 10Ω = 5 mA. P_bal = 0.25 mW per cell. For 16 cells balancing simultaneously: 4 mW total — negligible heat. But: to correct 100 mAh mismatch at 5 mA takes t = 0.1 Ah / 0.005 A = 20 hours. Passive balancing is slow — only practical at top-of-charge where the pack rests on CV.
🔥 Passive Balancing
Cost: Very low (resistor + FET)
Efficiency: Poor — excess energy wasted

⚡ Active Balancing

The LFP Balancing Challenge — 50mV of Signal, Nothing Else

LFP's flat OCV curve (Chapter 2) creates a fundamental balancing problem: the BMS cannot see voltage-based imbalance across 20–80% SoC because all cells sit within 50mV of each other regardless of their true SoC difference.

🔍 LFP OCV Plateau — The Balancing Blindspot

In the plateau (20–80% SoC), a cell at 30% SoC and a cell at 70% SoC differ by only 3mV — within the noise floor of any practical voltage measurement. Balancing must happen at top (CV charge phase, where voltage diverges) or bottom (near UVP, where OCV slopes steeply). LFP packs that never reach full charge will never be properly balanced.

⚠
Consequence for ESS Charge Management

Home ESS systems that never charge above 80% SoC (a common cycle-life optimisation) forfeit the balancing window. The cells will drift apart unnoticed across hundreds of cycles. To prevent this, the BMS should trigger a full charge-to-100% balancing cycle at minimum monthly, even if daily cycling only reaches 80%. Some BMS firmwares implement a "balance cycle scheduler" that forces a full CV phase weekly.

4.4

MOSFET Switching

The MOSFET is the BMS's actuator — the component that physically disconnects the pack from the load or charger when protection events fire. Understanding the switching topology, body diode behaviour, and the pre-charge circuit separates a safe design from a damaging one.

Charge FET and Discharge FET — Why Two Separate Switches

A BMS uses two back-to-back N-channel MOSFETs (common-source configuration) rather than a single switch. This allows independent control of charge and discharge current paths.

⚡ Back-to-Back MOSFET Topology — Common Source Configuration

Q1 (discharge FET) and Q2 (charge FET) share a common-source node. With sources tied together, each FET's body diode faces opposite directions — when Q1 is off but Q2 is on, Q1's body diode still allows discharge current to flow. This is why both FETs must be opened to fully isolate the pack. The shunt resistor on the negative bus measures pack current for both paths.

FET Selection — RDS(on) Power Loss P_FET = I² × RDS(on) × N_parallel Target: P_FET < 2W per FET at max continuous current Example: I = 280A, target P = 2W → RDS(on) max = 2 / 280² = 25 μΩ Solution: parallel 4× FETs at 100 μΩ each → effective RDS = 25 μΩ ✓ Modern N-channel power MOSFETs (e.g. IRFB7437, CSD18537) achieve 1–3 mΩ RDS(on) individually. For 280A continuous current a single FET would dissipate 78W — unacceptable. Paralleling 4–8 FETs distributes the thermal load. Each FET must have a small source-degeneration resistor (1–5 mΩ) to ensure equal current sharing during switching transients.
Pre-Charge Circuit — Protecting the Load Capacitors

When the discharge FET closes onto a discharged load with large input capacitors (e.g. an inverter with 1000μF of DC-link capacitance), the inrush current spike can reach thousands of amps — enough to weld FET contacts or blow fuses.

Inrush Current Without Pre-Charge I_peak = V_pack / R_esr_caps (at t=0, capacitor looks like short circuit) Example: 58V pack, R_esr = 5 mΩ → I_peak = 58 / 0.005 = 11,600A This exceeds SCD threshold → BMS trips immediately → FET never closes
🔌 Pre-Charge Sequence — Four-Phase Turn-On

Pre-charge sequence: (1) pack isolated, (2) pre-charge relay bypasses main FET through R_pchg (~100Ω), slowly charging load capacitors, (3) BMS monitors until V_load ≈ V_pack (ΔV < 2V), (4) closes main discharge FET and opens pre-charge relay. Total time ~600ms — imperceptible to users.

Pre-Charge Resistor Calculation R_pchg = V_pack / I_pchg_max (limit inrush to safe level) Time constant: τ = R_pchg × C_load V_cap(t) = V_pack × (1 − e^(−t/τ)) → 95% charge at t = 3τ Example: V_pack = 58V, C_load = 2000μF, I_max = 500mA safe inrush. R_pchg = 58V / 0.5A = 116Ω → use 100Ω 5W resistor. τ = 100 × 0.002 = 200ms. 95% charged at 3τ = 600ms. ✓ The pre-charge relay is typically a small signal relay (5V coil) rated 1A — it handles the limited pre-charge current easily while the main FETs handle the 280A+ load.

4.5

Analog Front-End (AFE) Architecture

The AFE IC is the BMS's sensory system — the silicon that translates raw cell voltages, pack current, and temperatures into digital data the MCU can act on. Without a precision AFE, every algorithm in this book is running on garbage data.

What the AFE Does — Full Picture
🔲 AFE IC Internal Architecture — TI BQ76952 / ADI LTC6813 Style

AFE IC internal blocks. Animated lines show active data paths. Cell voltages enter the multiplexer which feeds the 16-bit ΔΣ ADC. Hardware comparators fire the ALERT pin and gate driver directly for fast faults. All data aggregates in the digital core and is read by the MCU over SPI at 1–10 kHz.

Cell Voltage Measurement — Flying Capacitor and Delta-Sigma ADC

Measuring cell voltages in a series string presents a fundamental challenge: the cells sit at successively higher common-mode voltages (cell 1 at 0–3.65V, cell 16 at ~54–58V). The AFE must measure each cell's differential voltage on top of a high common-mode rail.

⚡
Flying Capacitor Measurement Technique

The AFE multiplexer connects a small sampling capacitor (the "flying capacitor", ~50 pF) across each cell in sequence. The capacitor charges to the cell's differential voltage, then the MUX disconnects it from the cell bus and connects it to the ADC input — which now sees only a low-voltage signal equal to V_cell, isolated from the high common-mode. The ΔΣ ADC then digitises this with 16-bit resolution over a conversion window of ~1ms. Total scan time for 16 cells: 16 × 1ms = 16ms — fast enough for 60Hz fault detection.

AFE-to-MCU Communication — SPI Daisy Chain for Multi-AFE Systems

A single AFE IC handles 12–16 cells. A 32S pack needs two AFEs. The daisy-chain topology lets the MCU communicate with all AFEs through a single SPI port:

/* SPI daisy chain read — two AFE ICs, 16 cells each MCU sends command to AFE1; AFE1 forwards to AFE2; response propagates back through chain */ void AFE_ReadAllCellVoltages(uint16_t *v_cells, uint8_t n_cells) { uint8_t cmd[4] = { AFE_CMD_RDCVA, 0x00, 0x00, 0x00 }; uint8_t resp[48]; /* 2 bytes/cell × 16 cells × 1.5 (overhead) */ SPI_CS_Assert(); SPI_Transmit(cmd, sizeof(cmd)); /* broadcast to chain */ SPI_Receive(resp, sizeof(resp)); /* all AFEs reply in sequence */ SPI_CS_Deassert(); for (int i = 0; i < n_cells; i++) { /* 2-byte raw reading → voltage in mV */ uint16_t raw = ((resp[i*2] << 8) | resp[i*2+1]); v_cells[i] = (raw * 100UL) / 3276; /* LSB = ~100μV for BQ76952 */ } } /* AFE watchdog — must be petted every 2s or AFE opens FETs This ensures MCU lockup results in safe pack disconnect */ void AFE_PetWatchdog(void) { AFE_WriteReg(AFE_REG_WDOG, 0xA5); /* magic byte resets timer */ }
⚠
Watchdog Is a Safety-Critical Feature — Do Not Disable

The AFE watchdog timer opens the main FETs if the MCU stops petting it within the timeout window (typically 1–2s). This is a critical safety feature: if the MCU crashes, hangs, or enters an infinite loop, the watchdog ensures the pack disconnects rather than remaining connected with no active protection. Never disable the watchdog during debug — instead, increase the timeout temporarily. In production firmware, the watchdog pet must be in the highest-priority task with a hardware timer, not a software delay loop that can be blocked.
