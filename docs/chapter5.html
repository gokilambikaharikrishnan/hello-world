5.1

Why Standards Exist

Battery standards are not bureaucratic paperwork — they are written in fire. Every requirement in IEC 62133 or UL 1973 was preceded by a real incident: a recalled product, a burned aircraft, an evacuated apartment block. Understanding where standards come from makes it easier to follow them correctly.

The Incidents That Created the Modern Standards Landscape
2006
Dell / Sony Laptop Battery Recall — 9.6 Million Units
Sony-manufactured 18650 cells had metallic contamination from the manufacturing process. Dendritic growth from contaminant particles caused internal short circuits in laptops worldwide. Multiple fires reported on aircraft.
→ Triggered: IEC 62133 cell-level manufacturing quality requirements; FAA battery fire suppression rules for cabins
2013
Boeing 787 Dreamliner — Two Pack Fires, Global Fleet Grounded
Two separate incidents (Boston and Tokyo) involving GS Yuasa Li-ion packs. Root cause: electrolyte venting and internal thermal runaway propagation. The packs met all then-current standards — yet still failed. The FAA grounded all 787s for 3 months.
→ Triggered: Revision of FAA Special Condition SC-BATM; new requirements for cell-to-cell propagation containment; reinforced IEC 62133 thermal propagation tests
2016
Samsung Galaxy Note 7 — 2.5 Million Units Recalled Globally
Two separate battery suppliers both produced packs with insufficient space for electrode expansion. During charging, electrode swelling caused internal short circuits. 35+ fires reported on aircraft; banned from all commercial flights worldwide.
→ Triggered: Samsung SDI and Amperex internal process overhaul; UN 38.3 transport test tightening; updated IEC 62368 consumer electronics power safety
2019
Arizona APS Grid ESS Fire — 4 Firefighters Injured
A 2 MWh grid storage installation using NMC chemistry experienced thermal runaway in one module. When firefighters opened the container, accumulated combustible gas (CO, H₂) caused a deflagration. The fire suppression system had been activated but gas had continued accumulating.
→ Triggered: NFPA 855 revision with stricter gas detection and ventilation requirements; UL 9540A large-scale fire testing protocol; EPRI Battery Storage Hazard Assessment
Now
Home ESS Residential Fires — Multiple Jurisdictions
As residential LFP storage deployments scale to millions of units, sporadic fire incidents continue — mostly from non-certified products sold through online channels without UL 1973 or IEC 62619 certification. Insurance and building codes are tightening rapidly.
→ Active: UL 1973 edition 3 in development; California Title 19 mandatory certification for all residential ESS; EU Battery Regulation 2023/1542 requiring Declaration of Conformity
The Standards Bodies and Their Jurisdictions
IEC
International Electrotechnical Commission
Global. Publishes IEC 62133, 61960, 62619, 62620, 62933. The primary technical standards body for batteries worldwide. EU, Asia, and most markets reference IEC directly.
UL
UL Solutions (formerly Underwriters Laboratories)
USA. Publishes UL 1973 (stationary ESS), UL 9540 (ESS systems), UL 9540A (fire test). Required for US market access. UL certification also accepted in Canada and increasingly globally.
UN
United Nations
Global transport. UN 38.3 (transport testing) is mandatory for all Li-ion cells/packs shipped by air, sea, or road. Published under UN Recommendations on the Transport of Dangerous Goods.
ISO
International Organization for Standardization
Global. ISO 12405 (EV battery testing), ISO 6469 (EV safety). Less directly relevant for ESS/UPS but sets testing methodologies referenced by IEC.
Cost of Non-Compliance — The Business Case for Certification
⚠
Non-Certified Products Cannot Be Legally Sold in Most Markets
  • USA: UL 1973 certification required for utility interconnection in all 50 states under NEC Article 706. Uncertified products cannot be connected to the grid or installed in occupied buildings.
  • EU: CE marking requires compliance with Low Voltage Directive and Battery Regulation. IEC 62619 is the harmonised standard — non-compliant products face market withdrawal.
  • Air freight: UN 38.3 certification required for all Li-ion shipments. Uncertified batteries are classified as prohibited dangerous goods — carrier liability exposure.
  • Insurance: Building and equipment insurers increasingly require ESS certification. Uncertified products void fire insurance coverage.
  • Liability: In a product fire, non-compliance is evidence of negligence in civil litigation — multiplying damages exposure.
5.2

IEC 62133 — Safety Requirements

IEC 62133 is the foundational cell-level safety standard. It defines abuse tests that every Li-ion cell must survive before entering a certified product. Understanding each test reveals the exact failure mode it guards against — and precisely how the BMS contributes to passing it.

IEC 62133-2:2017
Secondary cells and batteries containing alkaline or other non-acid electrolytes — Safety requirements for portable sealed secondary lithium cells, and for batteries made from them, for use in portable applications
Portable & Stationary Li-ion Cell-level + Pack-level
FOUNDATIONAL

IEC 62133 Part 2 specifically covers lithium cells and packs. It is the minimum safety gate every Li-ion product must clear. Six core abuse tests simulate the worst-case conditions a battery could encounter during normal use or foreseeable misuse.

The Six Core Safety Tests
Test 7.3.1
Continuous Low-Rate Charge
Cell charged at C/10 for 28 days continuously — simulating a faulty charger that never terminates. Tests BMS and cell robustness against chronic overcharge.
Pass: no fire, no explosion, no leakage
Test 7.3.2
Vibration
Sinusoidal sweep 7–50 Hz, 1g, three axes, 90 min each. Simulates transport and operational vibration. Tests mechanical integrity of cell construction and connections.
Pass: no fire, explosion, leakage, or capacity loss >5%
Test 7.3.3
Thermal Abuse
Cell stored at 130°C for 30 min. Simulates external heat source (adjacent cell TR, hot storage environment). Tests separator robustness at thermal extremes.
Pass: no fire or explosion (venting acceptable)
Test 7.3.4
Overcharge
Cell charged at 3× rated current to 10V (for Li-ion). Simulates complete BMS failure with runaway charger. Validates that cell vents safely without explosion.
Pass: no fire or explosion
Test 7.3.5
Forced Discharge
Fully discharged cell driven to −1V by a series load — polarity reversal. Simulates deep over-discharge in a series string where a weak cell reverses.
Pass: no fire or explosion
Test 7.3.6
External Short Circuit
Full charge cell shorted through <100 mΩ at 20°C and 55°C. Tests cell's intrinsic current interrupt device (CID) and PTC response. Simulates wiring fault.
Pass: no fire or explosion; surface T <170°C
🔬 How the BMS Contributes to IEC 62133 Compliance

Five of the six IEC 62133 tests are directly addressed by BMS protection features. Vibration (Test 7.3.2) is a mechanical design concern — the BMS ensures electrical connections remain intact but cannot help if the physical cell structure fails.

📋
IEC 62133 vs IEC 62619 — Know the Difference

IEC 62133 covers portable applications (cells and small packs up to 100Wh). IEC 62619:2022 is the sister standard for industrial and stationary applications (larger systems, higher voltages, ESS and UPS). For a home ESS product, you need IEC 62619, not 62133. Many manufacturers mistakenly cite 62133 when the applicable standard is 62619. Both share similar test philosophies but 62619 adds requirements for communication, SOC management, and installation.

5.3

IEC 61960 — Performance Characterisation

IEC 61960 defines how cell and battery performance is measured and reported. It is the reference standard behind every number on a cell datasheet — the rated capacity, the cycle life figure, the C-rate definitions. Understanding it lets you read datasheets critically.

IEC 61960-3:2017
Secondary cells and batteries containing alkaline or other non-acid electrolytes — Secondary lithium cells and batteries for portable applications — Part 3: Prismatic and cylindrical lithium secondary cells
Performance Measurement Datasheet Reference Standard
PERFORMANCE
Key Tests and What They Mean for ESS Design
TestProcedureWhat the Number MeansESS Design Implication
Rated capacityClause 7.3 0.2C discharge from full charge to cutoff at 20°C, rest after each step The Ah number on the label. The definitive measurement — all other capacity figures are relative to this. Your BMS Q_nominal must match this exact test condition. Using 1C for the RPT will give a lower number — not a defect, just Peukert effect.
C-rate capacityClause 7.4 Discharge at 1C, 2C, 5C from full charge at 20°C How much of rated capacity is available at different currents. LFP typically: 1C → 98%, 2C → 95%, 5C → 88%. BMS runtime prediction must use the C-rate adjusted capacity, not rated capacity, at high discharge rates. Peukert exponent k extracted from this data.
Cycle lifeClause 7.6 1C charge / 1C discharge, 100% DoD, 20°C. Count cycles until capacity < 80% of initial. The "3000 cycle" or "5000 cycle" claim. This is the cycle count to 80% SOH under standard conditions. Real-world cycle life depends heavily on DoD (80% DoD → ~1.5× more cycles than 100% DoD), temperature, and charge rate. Standard test is worst-case reference.
Self-dischargeClause 7.5 Fully charge, store at 20°C for 28 days, measure retained capacity % capacity remaining after 28 days idle. LFP typically 98–99% (i.e. 1–2% self-discharge per month). BMS coulomb counter must add a self-discharge current term to prevent SoC drift during long idle periods. Value from this test feeds the firmware constant.
Temperature performanceClause 7.7 Capacity at −20°C, 0°C, 20°C (reference), 60°C The f_T derating table for the BMS. Shows how much capacity is available at each temperature relative to 20°C reference. Directly populates the BMS temperature derating lookup table used in SoC calculation and runtime prediction.
Reading a Cell Datasheet with IEC 61960 in Mind
⚠
Five Red Flags on Cell Datasheets
  • Capacity measured at C/5 not C/10: C/5 gives higher apparent capacity than the IEC standard C/10 — inflated numbers. Verify test condition in footnotes.
  • Cycle life at partial DoD: "5000 cycles" claimed at 20% DoD is not comparable to "3000 cycles" at 100% DoD. Always compare at same DoD.
  • No temperature derating curves: Legitimate datasheets include capacity vs temperature graphs. Absence means the manufacturer hasn't tested, or is hiding poor cold performance.
  • No self-discharge figure: Required by IEC 61960. Absence is non-compliance.
  • Capacity measured at T=25°C vs T=20°C: IEC standard is 20°C. Measuring at 25°C gives ~1–2% higher capacity. Small but worth noting when comparing cells.
