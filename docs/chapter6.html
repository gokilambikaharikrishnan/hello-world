Chapter 6 — BMS Architecture

Building the Brain:
BMS Architecture

A BMS is not a single chip or a few protection FETs — it is a layered system of hardware and firmware modules, each solving a specific physical problem. This chapter traces every architectural decision from battery physics to silicon: AFE ICs, MCU peripherals, CAN bus framing, NVM wear leveling, RTC timestamping, state machines, and SOX algorithms. By the end, you will be able to design a production-grade BMS from a blank schematic.

Section 6.1

BMS Requirement Traceability

Every component in a BMS exists because of a specific battery physics problem — nothing is there by accident. The AFE chip exists because cell voltages must be measured precisely. The MCU exists because protection decisions need a programmable brain. The CAN bus exists because the battery needs to report its state to the inverter. Understanding this cause-and-effect chain — which weakness requires which hardware — is what separates a design that is understood from one that is just copied from a reference schematic. This section maps the full chain from battery limitation to silicon implementation.

Section 6.2

Hardware Architecture — Full Picture

A real BMS is built from six distinct hardware layers stacked on top of each other. The cells sit at the bottom — the physical battery. Above them, the AFE chip (Analog Front End) reads all the voltages and temperatures. Above that, the MCU (microcontroller) runs the protection logic and algorithms. The MCU connects outward through communication interfaces — CAN bus, SMBus — to talk to the inverter and charger. Data is saved to persistent storage so nothing is lost if power disappears. And the physical protection layer — the MOSFETs, fuse, and contactor — is what actually cuts the electrical connection when a fault fires. Each layer has one job, and they pass information between them through well-defined connections. Most BMS design bugs come from misunderstanding where one layer ends and the next begins.

AFE Layer — Deep Dive

The AFE IC is the most safety-critical chip in the entire BMS — it is the BMS's eyes and ears. It sits between the high-voltage battery stack (up to 58V) and the low-voltage microcontroller (3.3V), and it handles four jobs simultaneously: measuring each cell's individual voltage, reading all the temperature sensors, detecting short-circuit faults, and controlling the individual balancing resistors. It does all of this with galvanic isolation — meaning the high-voltage battery side and the low-voltage MCU side are electrically separated, so a wiring fault on the battery side cannot destroy the microcontroller or create a shock hazard on the logic side.

Flying Capacitor — Why It's Necessary
In a 16S pack, cell 16 sits at ~52V above ground while cell 1 sits at ~3.3V. Connecting both directly to a single ADC is impossible — the common-mode voltage would saturate or destroy the input. The flying capacitor multiplexer solves this by charging a small capacitor (C_fly) across each cell differentially, then transferring that charge to the ADC input. The ADC only ever sees a differential voltage of ≤3.65V — never the full stack voltage. This is why AFE ICs are expensive: the flying capacitor architecture requires 16 high-voltage switches inside the IC.
Delta-Sigma ADC — How It Works
The Δ-Σ (delta-sigma) ADC does not flash-convert the voltage directly. Instead, it oversamples at a very high rate (1–4 MHz) using a 1-bit comparator, then applies a digital decimation filter to produce a 16-bit result. This architecture is immune to thermal noise because averaging 1000s of 1-bit samples produces a statistically precise result. The tradeoff: each conversion takes ~1ms per cell. For 16 cells, the AFE needs ~16ms per full cycle — which is why the firmware measurement loop runs at 10ms (AFE pipelines the conversions).
AFE Watchdog — The True Fail-Safe
The AFE watchdog is the most important safety feature that most engineers overlook. The MCU must write 0xA5 to the AFE's watchdog register every ≤2 seconds. If the MCU crashes (stack overflow, firmware bug, power glitch) and stops writing, the AFE watchdog timer expires and the AFE independently opens all protection FETs. The battery is now safe even with a dead MCU. UL 1973 Phase 5 tests this explicitly: the tester freezes the firmware and watches for the watchdog response. Without this, no UL 1973 certification.
SPI Daisy Chain for Multi-Pack
A single BMS may manage 2–4 AFE ICs (for very large packs). TI BQ76952 uses standard SPI, requiring one CS line per IC. ADI LTC6813 uses isoSPI — a differential, isolated version that chains multiple AFEs in a ring topology with a single twisted pair. Each AFE receives commands, acts on its cells, appends its data, and passes the packet downstream. The MCU talks only to the first AFE; the last AFE returns the combined data. This allows 18 cells per AFE × N AFEs = practically unlimited cell count from one MCU SPI port.

MCU Layer

The microcontroller is the brain of the BMS — it runs every software module, makes all the protection decisions, and coordinates data flowing between the AFE, the storage chip, and the CAN bus. Picking the wrong MCU is one of the most expensive mistakes you can make in BMS design, because you often only discover the problem after the PCB is manufactured. Not enough RAM means the state-of-charge algorithm doesn't fit in memory. Not enough Flash means the full OCV lookup table can't be stored. No CAN peripheral on the chip means you have to add an extra SPI-to-CAN converter IC, adding cost, complexity, and failure modes. Pick the right MCU once, up front.

Why Dedicated MCU for BMS
Some designs try to share one microcontroller between the BMS and the inverter controller to save money. This is dangerous: if the inverter's control loop briefly takes over the CPU for a heavy calculation — for example, recalculating the solar MPPT point — the BMS measurement loop is paused. A 100ms gap in cell voltage monitoring during a fault can allow real cell damage. Production BMS designs always use a dedicated MCU just for the BMS. A second MCU costs about $2. A missed protection event costs your company far more than that in liability. A dedicated MCU also makes UL 1973 certification simpler, because the fault injection test only needs to simulate crashing the BMS firmware, not the whole shared system.
Core Clock Speed Requirements
A processor running at 48–168 MHz (Cortex-M0+ to M4 family) is enough for most BMS applications. If you're implementing the advanced Extended Kalman Filter SoC algorithm covered in chapter 2, you need a chip with a hardware floating-point unit (FPU) — a Cortex-M4F or M7F. Without an FPU, every floating-point calculation goes through slow software emulation, making the EKF about ten times slower. The practical rule: when you measure your system's peak CPU usage — all BMS tasks running simultaneously — it should use less than 60% of the available CPU time. The remaining 40% is breathing room for balancing, logging, and future software updates. Never design at 95% CPU — you will regret it when you're debugging a timing problem at 3am before a customer deadline.
RAM Requirements
RAM is the working memory where the BMS keeps all its live data. The minimum for a 16-cell LFP BMS is 32KB. Here's where it goes: storing the sixteen cell voltages takes 32 bytes; six temperature readings take 24 bytes; the EKF state matrix takes 64 bytes; the OCV-SoC lookup table (if kept in RAM) takes 400 bytes; the circular fault log buffer takes 4KB; CAN message buffers take 128 bytes; and the RTOS task stacks take about 16KB across four tasks. That all adds up to roughly 21KB just for the basics. The recommendation is 64KB or more — tight 32KB designs can actually crash in very cold temperatures (−40°C) because slower code execution causes deeper interrupt call stacks, using more RAM than at room temperature.
Flash Requirements
Flash is the permanent storage where the firmware code itself lives. A complete BMS firmware is typically 64–128KB. The breakdown: the FreeRTOS operating system kernel takes about 12KB; the main BMS application code takes about 40KB; the OCV lookup table is 400 bytes; the NVM storage driver is about 4KB; the CAN communication stack is about 8KB; calibration data takes 2KB. Always leave at least 25% of Flash empty for future firmware updates — both over-the-air updates and bench programming need that buffer space. Data storage (NVM) must use separate Flash pages from the code, because writing to Flash while code is running can corrupt it. Minimum recommended size: 256KB Flash.
Required Peripherals
Must-have peripherals: SPI port to talk to the AFE chip; ADC channels to read NTC temperature sensors, the shunt resistor voltage, and the total pack voltage; CAN or CAN-FD for communicating with the inverter and charger; UART for a debug and service interface; hardware timers to drive the measurement loop, balancing timing, and watchdog; a hardware watchdog timer that is separate from the software tick (so a software freeze can't also freeze the watchdog); I²C or SPI to read and write the NVM storage chip; an RTC for timestamping fault events; and backup power domain so the RTC and backup RAM survive a main power loss. Nice-to-have: DMA (lets SPI transfers run in background without tying up the CPU), hardware CRC (checks data integrity in NVM without software overhead), and an independent watchdog (IWDG) clocked from a separate low-speed oscillator so even a main clock failure doesn't disable the watchdog.
Watchdog Timer Design
A BMS needs two separate watchdog layers, not one. The first is the AFE hardware watchdog — built into the AFE chip itself. The MCU must write a "keep-alive" value to the AFE's watchdog register regularly. If the MCU stops doing this (because it crashed), the AFE independently opens the main power FETs and disconnects the pack, timeout is 2 seconds. The second is the MCU's own independent watchdog (IWDG) — this one resets the MCU itself if the firmware hangs. It runs off a separate low-speed oscillator at 32kHz, so even if the main clock fails, the watchdog still works. Both watchdogs must be fed by the measurement loop task — not from a background task. This way, if the measurement loop freezes (the most critical part of the firmware), the watchdog fires. Set the IWDG timeout at 100ms to allow one measurement cycle to slip; set the AFE watchdog at 2 seconds to allow time for the MCU to reset and restart.

CAN Bus — Deep Dive

CAN — Controller Area Network — is the communication protocol the BMS uses to talk to the inverter and charger. It was invented by Bosch in 1986 for use in cars, where dozens of electronic control units need to share a single cable without stepping on each other's messages. The reason it became the standard for battery systems too is its robustness: CAN uses two wires carrying opposite signals (called differential signalling), which means electrical noise hits both wires equally and cancels out — a huge advantage in an environment with switching power electronics generating noise constantly. It also has hardware-level error detection built in (CRC checksum and bit-stuffing rules), and a clever arbitration system that lets multiple devices share the bus without any of them needing to be designated as "master". If two devices try to send at the same time, the one with the lower message ID automatically wins and the other waits — no collision, no lost data.

CAN Error Handling and Bus-Off

Error Counters (TEC / REC)
Every CAN device keeps two error counters: a Transmit Error Counter (TEC) and a Receive Error Counter (REC). Every time a device detects an error — a garbled message, a timing violation, a CRC mismatch — its counter goes up. Every successful message brings it back down. Three states exist: Error Active (TEC/REC below 96) is normal operation. Error Passive (96–127) means the device has seen repeated errors and is being more cautious — it can still communicate but signals errors differently. Bus-Off (TEC at 128 or above) means the device completely disconnects from the bus and waits for 128 × 11 clean bits before trying to reconnect. This self-isolation mechanism prevents one broken device from flooding the bus with error frames and taking down every other device connected to it.
BMS Silent Timeout
UL 1973 certification requires that the inverter (the host system) must notice within 5 seconds if the BMS stops talking. If the inverter hasn't received a CAN message from BMS ID 0x355 in more than 5 seconds, it must stop all charging and discharging and raise an alarm — because a silent BMS means no active protection. The BMS also watches in the other direction: if CAN transmission has been failing continuously (the bus-off state), it logs a communication fault internally. If the bus stays dead for longer than a configurable timeout (default 30 seconds, to allow bus recovery), the BMS opens the FETs and isolates the pack as a precaution.
Baud Rate: 250 vs 500 kbps
250 kilobits per second is the industry standard speed for stationary ESS systems — every major inverter brand (PYLON, SMA, Victron, Growatt) defaults to 250 kbps. At this speed the CAN bus can reliably carry signals over cable runs up to about 250 metres. Running at 500 kbps doubles the speed but cuts the maximum cable length to about 100 metres. For a home ESS where the BMS and inverter sit in the same wall cabinet connected by a 1-metre cable, 500 kbps works fine. For a UPS installation with a remote monitoring terminal at the end of a long cable run, use 250 kbps. One critical rule: both ends of the bus must be set to exactly the same baud rate. A mismatch — even by a small amount — immediately causes bus errors from the first message onwards.

NVM & Backup SRAM

When the BMS loses power — a normal shutdown, a fault disconnect, or an unexpected blackout — it must remember what it knew. That means the state of charge, state of health, total cycle count, fault history, and calibration data all need to survive a power loss. Without this, every time the system powers back up the BMS would have to guess the state of charge from scratch using the open-circuit voltage — which, as we know from chapter 2, is almost useless in the middle of LFP's flat voltage plateau. And cycle count would reset to zero, losing years of accurate battery health tracking. Two storage technologies solve this problem — non-volatile Flash memory (NVM) and battery-backed SRAM — and they each have different tradeoffs around how fast they write, how many times they can be written, and how reliably data survives.

NVM Wear Leveling
EEPROM endurance: 100,000–1,000,000 write cycles. If SoC is written every 60s for 10 years: 10yr × 365d × 24h × 60 = 5,256,000 writes. This would destroy a single EEPROM address in ~5 years. Wear leveling rotates writes across a sector of N pages. With 16 pages and 1M endurance, the effective life extends to 16M writes — over 30 years. Implement by maintaining a write pointer that advances to the next page on each write, wrapping at N. A valid-byte header identifies the current page on startup.
CRC Protection of NVM Data
A power loss during an NVM write leaves a partially written page — the data may appear valid but contain bit errors. Every NVM record must include a CRC-16 or CRC-32 checksum over the data payload. On startup, the MCU reads each NVM record and validates the CRC. If the CRC fails, the record is marked invalid and the previous valid record is used. Never boot from corrupted NVM data — this has caused field failures where a BMS reported 0% SoC and refused to discharge from a fully charged pack.
EEPROM vs Internal Flash
External EEPROM (I²C AT24C256, 32KB): simple byte-level writes, 100K cycles per byte, no erase-before-write required. Best for SoC/SoH records. Internal Flash (STM32 dual-bank): must erase full page (2KB) before writing, 10K cycles per page, fast read. Best for large OCV tables and firmware. The professional solution uses both: EEPROM for high-write-frequency data (SoC checkpoints, fault log), internal Flash for low-write configuration (calibration, OCV tables, firmware parameters).
Backup SRAM Architecture
STM32 backup SRAM (BKP_SRAM) is a 4KB SRAM powered from VBAT when main VDD is absent. A CR2032 coin cell (220mAh) can power 4KB SRAM + RTC for 10+ years at 1–2μA standby. On power loss, the MCU writes the current SoC, SoF, and critical fault flags to BKP_SRAM. On startup, the MCU reads BKP_SRAM and validates integrity (magic number + checksum). If valid, SoC is restored immediately — no OCV wait. This gives sub-millisecond SoC recovery after power interruptions — critical for UPS applications where 10ms switchover is expected.

NVM Write Pattern with CRC and Wear Leveling

#define NVM_SECTOR_PAGES 16 #define NVM_PAGE_SIZE 64 /* bytes per page */ #define NVM_MAGIC 0xBDA5 typedef struct { uint16_t magic; /* 0xBDA5 = valid */ uint16_t soc_x100; /* SoC * 100 (0–10000) */ uint16_t soh_x100; /* SoH * 100 (0–10000) */ uint32_t cycle_count; /* full cycles */ uint32_t timestamp; /* Unix seconds from RTC */ uint16_t crc16; /* CRC-16 of above fields */ } NVM_Record_t; static uint8_t nvm_write_ptr = 0; /* current write page */ void NVM_SaveState(BMS_State_t *bms) { NVM_Record_t rec; rec.magic = NVM_MAGIC; rec.soc_x100 = (uint16_t)(bms->soc * 100.0f); rec.soh_x100 = (uint16_t)(bms->soh * 100.0f); rec.cycle_count = bms->cycle_count; rec.timestamp = RTC_GetUnixTimestamp(); rec.crc16 = CRC16_Compute((uint8_t*)&rec, offsetof(NVM_Record_t, crc16)); /* Write to current wear-level page */ EEPROM_Write(nvm_write_ptr * NVM_PAGE_SIZE, (uint8_t*)&rec, sizeof(rec)); /* Advance pointer (ring buffer) */ nvm_write_ptr = (nvm_write_ptr + 1) % NVM_SECTOR_PAGES; } bool NVM_LoadState(BMS_State_t *bms) { /* Scan all pages, find newest valid record */ uint32_t newest_ts = 0; int8_t best_page = -1; NVM_Record_t rec; for (uint8_t i = 0; i < NVM_SECTOR_PAGES; i++) { EEPROM_Read(i * NVM_PAGE_SIZE, (uint8_t*)&rec, sizeof(rec)); uint16_t expected = CRC16_Compute((uint8_t*)&rec, offsetof(NVM_Record_t, crc16)); if (rec.magic == NVM_MAGIC && rec.crc16 == expected && rec.timestamp > newest_ts) { newest_ts = rec.timestamp; best_page = i; } } if (best_page < 0) return false; /* No valid record */ EEPROM_Read(best_page * NVM_PAGE_SIZE, (uint8_t*)&rec, sizeof(rec)); bms->soc = rec.soc_x100 / 100.0f; bms->soh = rec.soh_x100 / 100.0f; bms->cycle_count = rec.cycle_count; nvm_write_ptr = (best_page + 1) % NVM_SECTOR_PAGES; return true; }

RTC & Timestamping

Every production BMS needs a Real-Time Clock (RTC) — a tiny chip that keeps track of the actual date and time, even when the battery is off. Why does that matter? Because when something goes wrong, you need to know exactly when it happened and what the battery was doing before the fault. An RTC also lets the BMS track how the battery ages over months and years, not just how many times it has been charged.

Why RTC in BMS?
Calendar aging: Heat ages batteries even when you are not using them. A pack sitting at 40°C for 6 months ages twice as fast as one kept at 25°C. Without an RTC, the BMS can only count charge cycles — it cannot see this invisible time-based wear. With an RTC, it logs how many hours were spent at high temperature and factors that into the battery's health estimate.

Fault timestamps: "Cell 8 hit 3.67V" tells you something failed. "Cell 8 hit 3.67V on 2025-03-15 at 14:32:07" tells you exactly when — which is what you need to diagnose the problem, honour a warranty claim, or check what the battery was doing in the minutes before.
RTC Backup Power
The RTC runs off a tiny CR1225 coin cell (the same kind in watches) connected to the STM32's VBAT pin. That coin cell holds 48mAh and the RTC only needs 0.5μA — so the math gives 96,000 hours, or about 11 years on one battery. Even when the main pack is completely dead and unplugged, the BMS clock keeps ticking and remembers the last known charge level. The same coin cell also powers the backup SRAM, bringing total draw to ~1.5μA — still 7+ years.
Unix Timestamp Format
The BMS stores time as a single big number — Unix seconds — which counts how many seconds have passed since 1 January 1970. This makes time math trivial: to find out how long ago something happened, you just subtract two numbers. No messy date calculations needed. For event logs, each entry takes just 6 bytes (4 for the timestamp, 1 for the event type, 1 for data), so a 256-event log only uses 1,536 bytes — a tiny slice of the EEPROM.
Calendar Aging via RTC
Every 60 seconds the BMS logs the temperature. Once an hour it uses those temperature readings to calculate how much the battery aged due to heat — using a chemistry formula called the Arrhenius equation: k_age = A × exp(−Ea / R·T). Think of k_age as a "speed of aging" — hotter temperature means the number gets bigger and the battery degrades faster. Without an RTC, the BMS can only count charge cycles. Calendar aging (heat over time when not in use) stays completely hidden — and for batteries kept in hot environments, that can be the bigger threat.

Section 6.3

Firmware Architecture

BMS firmware is not a simple program that reads sensors and reacts — it is a real-time system where timing is everything. Different tasks must run at very different speeds, set by physics. Voltage must be checked every 10ms because a runaway charge can push a cell into danger in milliseconds. Charge level (SoC) updates every 100ms to stay accurate during sudden load changes. Battery health (SoH) only needs to update once an hour because aging is slow. Build the timing wrong and you get a BMS that passes lab tests but fails in the field.

Module Architecture Overview

Protection Response Time Hierarchy

< 500μs
Short Circuit Detection (SCD) — Hardware path
This is the fastest protection layer — and it has to be, because a short circuit can dump thousands of amps in milliseconds. The AFE chip has a dedicated comparator that watches the shunt resistor voltage. The moment it sees too much current (e.g., 300A on a 1mΩ shunt), it slams the discharge FET shut — no MCU needed. The microcontroller only finds out afterward via an interrupt. This hardware speed is required by UL 1973 and UN 38.3 Test T5.
< 10ms
Over-Current Detection (OCD) — Fast firmware path
SCD catches sudden massive spikes. OCD handles the slower but still dangerous case: sustained overcurrent. Every 10ms the MCU reads the pack current. If it stays above the OCD threshold (typically 1.5× the continuous rating, e.g., 150A) for more than 10ms, the firmware opens the FET. The 10ms filter prevents false shutdowns from brief capacitor inrush spikes when a device first powers on.
< 100ms
Over-Voltage Protection (OVP) — Firmware path
The MCU reads all 16 cell voltages every 10ms. If any cell climbs above 3.65V (the LFP upper limit) and stays there for more than 100ms, the firmware shuts off the charge FET. The 100ms debounce window filters out brief ADC noise spikes. Acting quickly matters: lithium plating (metallic lithium depositing on the anode) accelerates above 3.65V and becomes permanent above 3.80V — that damage cannot be reversed.
< 1s
Under-Voltage / Temperature Protection — Firmware path
Under-voltage (too-empty cell) and temperature faults are allowed a full second to respond because the underlying chemistry moves slower. A cell going below 2.5V won't dissolve its copper current collector instantly — it takes seconds. Likewise, thermal runaway doesn't start the moment a cell touches 55°C. Using a faster response here (like 100ms) would cause false shutdowns every time a large load causes a brief voltage dip. The 1s window filters those out while still protecting against real damage.
> 2°C/s
Thermal Runaway Detection (dT/dt) — Emergency path
This is not a normal temperature alarm — it watches how fast the temperature is rising. If the rate exceeds 2°C per second, thermal runaway may already be starting. The BMS does not wait: it opens all FETs immediately, latches the fault (the pack cannot restart until someone power-cycles it), logs the event with a timestamp, and can trigger an external fire suppression system via a GPIO pin. Catching the temperature rate rather than the absolute value means the BMS can react before a cell has even reached dangerous temperatures.

Timing Architecture

< 1ms
Hardware Interrupt Layer — SCD, AFE FAULT pin
The AFE detects a short circuit in hardware and instantly fires an interrupt to the MCU — bypassing all software loops entirely. The MCU's interrupt handler latches the fault in under 1ms. Meanwhile the AFE has already opened the FET on its own, without waiting for any code to run. The interrupt just tells the 10ms loop "log this and broadcast the alarm."
10ms
Measurement + Protection Loop — Core safety loop
Every 10ms the MCU reads all 16 cell voltages, 6 temperatures, and pack current. It runs those numbers through a noise filter, then checks them against every protection threshold. If anything is out of range, it triggers the FETs. It also "pets" the watchdog timer to prove it is still alive. This loop is the BMS heartbeat — missing even one cycle is a failure.
100ms
SOX Computation + State Machine — Algorithm layer
Every 100ms the MCU updates the charge level (SoC) by integrating how much current has flowed since the last update. If the pack is resting, it corrects against the open-circuit voltage curve. It also calculates how much power the pack can safely deliver right now (SoP), accounting for temperature effects. The state machine checks whether any conditions have changed enough to move to a different state. If the SoC shifted by more than 0.5%, a CAN message is sent to the inverter.
1s
Balancing + CAN TX + Backup SRAM — Secondary loop
Once per second the BMS handles its housekeeping tasks. It checks which cells are highest in voltage and turns on balancing resistors to drain them slightly. It transmits the full set of CAN status messages to the inverter. It saves the current SoC to backup SRAM (takes just 2 microseconds). It checks whether the inverter has gone silent for too long. And it logs daily peak readings to the event log.
60s
NVM SoC Checkpoint — Persistence layer
Every 60 seconds the BMS saves the SoC, timestamp, and fault count to EEPROM (the slower but longer-term storage). This is the fallback recovery point in case both power and backup SRAM are lost. Worst case: if power fails right before a 60s write, the BMS wakes up with a SoC reading that is at most 60 seconds out of date. At a 1C discharge rate on a 280Ah pack, that is only 0.36% error — acceptable for almost any application.
1 hour
SoH Update + Calendar Aging — Long-term tracking
Once an hour the BMS runs its long-term health calculations. It adds up today's partial charges into a full-cycle-equivalent count. It applies the temperature-based Arrhenius aging factor accumulated since the last hour. It then updates the SoH estimate, saves it to EEPROM, and sends a CAN message with the updated cycle count. This hourly record is what a service engineer looks at to decide whether the battery needs replacement.
Section 6.4

How World-Class BMS Companies Design Their Systems

Tesla, BYD, and CATL together manage more lithium battery energy than the rest of the world combined. Every design decision in their BMS systems was shaped by real failures — fires, recalls, field replacements, warranty claims. Their architectures carry the lessons of millions of battery-hours in the real world. What they do differently is exactly what separates a battery that lasts 10 years from one that degrades in two.

Tesla — Distributed Cell-Level Intelligence

Cell-Level Fusing (Invar Wire)
Tesla solders a hair-thin invar wire fuse directly to each individual cell — one wire per cell. If a cell develops an internal short and starts pulling too much current, its wire fuse melts and disconnects just that one cell. The rest of the pack keeps working. In a 4680-format pack with 960 cells, losing 10 bad cells is less than 1% capacity loss — the pack degrades gracefully instead of failing catastrophically. This protection happens at the hardware level, below even the BMS.
Thermal Propagation Barrier
Between rows of cells in Tesla's structural battery pack sits an intumescent foam — a material that expands when it gets hot. If one cell goes into thermal runaway, the foam swells and physically blocks heat from reaching the neighbouring row of cells. Meanwhile the BMS detects the rising temperature via the dT/dt alarm, opens all FETs, and signals venting. The foam barrier buys time; the BMS contains the damage. Together they prevent a single bad cell from turning into a pack fire.
Multi-Node Distributed BMS
Rather than one central BMS chip monitoring the whole pack, Tesla uses a two-tier structure. Each battery module (~46 cells) has its own Battery Module Controller (BMC) with its own AFE. The BMCs all report via CAN to a central Battery Management Unit (BMU), which runs the SoX algorithms and talks to the vehicle. If one AFE fails, it takes down only that module — not the entire pack. Fault isolation through distributed architecture.
Coulomb Counting Precision
Most BMS designs use a 1mΩ shunt with a 16-bit ADC to measure current. Tesla uses a 500μΩ shunt with a 24-bit ADC — twice the hardware precision, 256× more resolution in the digitiser. At 500A peak, the 500μΩ shunt produces 250mV — a large, easy-to-measure signal. The 24-bit ADC resolves that to about 30mA steps. Over hundreds of charge-discharge cycles, this precision accumulates into SoC accuracy below ±1% — the reason Tesla can tell a driver "you have 3 miles of range left" and be right.
OTA Firmware Updates
Tesla pushes BMS firmware updates over the air — no service visit needed. To do this safely, four design rules are mandatory: (1) the MCU Flash is split into two banks — new firmware loads into Bank B while Bank A runs; (2) the CAN bootloader lives in a protected Flash region that application code can never overwrite; (3) every update is cryptographically signed — a bad actor cannot inject malicious firmware; (4) if the new firmware fails health checks in the first 100 cycles, the BMS automatically rolls back to the old version. Tesla has used OTA to improve SoH algorithms, adjust OVP thresholds, and tune thermal management — years after the pack left the factory.

BYD — Blade Battery Architecture

Cell-to-Pack (CTP) — No Module Layer
BYD's Blade battery skips the module step entirely. Instead of grouping cells into modules and stacking modules into a pack, the long flat blade cells go directly into the pack housing side-by-side, like books on a shelf. Their shape gives the structure mechanical strength without a separate frame. This also simplifies the BMS: only one AFE layer is needed instead of Tesla's two-tier BMC + BMU structure, which reduces both cost and the number of things that can fail.
Nail Penetration Safety
BYD filmed a nail being driven through a Blade LFP cell live on stage — no fire, no smoke. This is possible because LFP's P-O bond (explained in Chapter 1) holds so tightly that the cathode cannot release oxygen even when mechanically punctured. Without oxygen, there is no combustion. The BMS contribution is to watch the dT/dt signal during such an event: the temperature rises sharply when the nail shorts the cell, and the BMS logs it as a safety event. The cell survives; the log proves it happened.
Temperature Uniformity Focus
Most BMS designs put one temperature sensor every 10 cells or so. BYD uses one sensor per 4 cells — significantly denser coverage. But sensors only measure at their exact location. BYD's BMS goes further: it runs a temperature model that uses cell impedance data to estimate the internal temperature of every cell, not just the ones next to a sensor. The model is updated live using the thermal resistance calculated from voltage behaviour under varying loads, giving a more accurate picture than sensors alone can provide.
Electrolyte Leak Detection
Battery electrolyte is a conductive liquid. If it leaks and pools inside the pack enclosure, it can bridge the gap between the high-voltage terminals and the metal chassis — creating a shock and fire hazard. BYD's BMS constantly measures the insulation resistance between the pack terminals and chassis using a simple high-impedance resistor network. If that resistance drops below 100Ω per volt, the BMS logs a fault and alerts the operator before anyone is at risk. This meets both NFPA 855 and UL 1973 insulation requirements.

CATL — System-Level Intelligence

Cloud-Based Fleet SoH Calibration
CATL's products send health data to a central cloud platform at the end of every charge cycle — cell voltages, temperatures, current profiles, and SoH estimates. The cloud compares data across the entire fleet. If it notices that cells in one region are fading faster than the on-device model predicted, it pushes a calibration update to all 50,000 packs in the fleet within 24 hours, no service visits needed. A SoH error found in one customer's pack gets fixed for everyone.
Multi-Frequency Impedance Sensing
When the pack is resting, CATL's BMS injects tiny alternating current signals at different frequencies (0.1 to 1000 Hz) and measures how the cell voltage responds. This is a simplified form of Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) — a technique normally done only in labs. The BMS analyses the response to calculate the internal resistance at different timescales: ohmic resistance (R₀), SEI layer resistance (R₁), and diffusion resistance. From those numbers it can detect the early signs of lithium plating and SEI growth — catching failures 50–100 charge cycles before they would show up in normal SoH tracking.
Adaptive Charging Protocol
Most chargers use a fixed CC/CV profile — the same charge current regardless of how old the cell is. CATL's BMS adjusts the charge current in real time based on the cell's measured impedance. If the SEI layer has thickened (R₁ has grown), the BMS reduces the charge current to avoid lithium plating on the anode. As the cell ages over thousands of cycles, the BMS keeps adapting. This is why CATL cells in products with adaptive charging often exceed 3,000 cycles, while the same cells charged with fixed protocols typically reach only 2,200 — the difference is the algorithm, not the chemistry.
What They All Have in Common
Every world-class BMS shares four properties:
1. Hardware fail-safe — watchdog + fuse + cell-level fusing independent of firmware
2. Persistent state — SoC never lost on power cycle
3. Timestamped events — every fault has a calendar timestamp
4. Adaptive algorithms — SoC/SoH improve as data accumulates
The gap between a $200 BMS module and a $2000 one is almost entirely in points 3 and 4.

Communication Stack Deep Dive

A production BMS usually speaks three different communication languages. CAN is the primary link to the inverter and charger — fast, noise-immune, and the industry standard for energy storage. SMBus is an older, simpler interface used by laptop-style chargers and UPS systems. UART is for engineers in the field — plugging in a laptop to read logs, run diagnostics, or update firmware. No single protocol handles all three jobs well, which is why all three coexist.

SMBus — Smart Battery Data Specification

SMBus (Smart Battery Data Specification, SBDS v1.1) is a simple I²C-based protocol that batteries use to talk to chargers. It pre-dates CAN for BMS use and is still widely found in UPS and laptop chargers. The clever part: a charger that supports SMBus can plug into any SBDS-compatible battery and automatically learn the chemistry, capacity, and the exact voltage and current it should charge at — no manual setup needed.

Section 6.6

SOX State Estimation — Complete Reference

SOX is the shorthand name for the four numbers a BMS computes and reports: SoC (how full is the battery right now?), SoH (how degraded is the battery compared to when it was new?), SoF (is the battery fit to deliver what is being asked of it?), and SoP (how much power can the battery safely deliver at this moment?). Each one uses a different algorithm, targets a different accuracy level, and is used by different parts of the system. This section covers all four in full.

SoC — State of Charge

Primary: Coulomb Counting
SoC(t) = SoC₀ − ∫(I·dt) / C_full
Works like a water flow meter — count how many amps flowed in and out, divide by the total capacity, and you get the SoC. Simple, but the error accumulates over time if not corrected. On startup, the BMS seeds this from the last saved value in NVM or from an OCV reading. A temperature factor η(T) adjusts because cold batteries deliver less usable capacity than warm ones.
Correction: OCV Lookup (at rest)
SoC_ocv = f(V_ocv) from table
When the battery has been resting (current less than C/20) for more than 30 minutes, the terminal voltage equals the true open-circuit voltage (OCV). The BMS looks up that voltage in a 50-point table to get SoC. The big problem with LFP: its voltage is almost flat between 20% and 80% SoC, so a small voltage error becomes a huge SoC error in that range.
Advanced: Extended Kalman Filter
x̂ₖ = Aₖx̂ₖ₋₁ + Bₖuₖ + Kₖ(yₖ − Cₖx̂ₖ)
The EKF treats the battery as a mathematical model with hidden internal states — mainly SoC and the voltage across the RC circuit. Every 100ms it compares the model's predicted terminal voltage against the measured one and uses the error to correct its SoC estimate. The Kalman gain K decides how much to trust the new measurement vs the previous prediction. The result: drift-free SoC even during active charging and discharging.
Temperature Compensation
SoC_T = SoC_25 × η(T)
Cold batteries shrink. At −20°C an LFP cell delivers only about 60% of what it would at room temperature. The η(T) factor corrects for this: η(−20°C)=0.60, η(0°C)=0.82, η(25°C)=1.00, η(45°C)=1.02. It is applied to both the coulomb counter and the OCV lookup, so the SoC reading reflects actual usable capacity, not just nominal capacity.

SoH — State of Health

Capacity SoH
SoH_C = C_full_now / C_design × 100%
The only accurate way to measure SoH_C is a calibration cycle: fully charge, then fully discharge, and measure exactly how many amp-hours came out. The ratio of that number to the original design capacity gives SoH. Industry standard: replace the battery when SoH drops below 80%. For our 280Ah reference pack, that means replacing at 224Ah effective capacity.
Resistance SoH
SoH_R = R₀_initial / R₀_now × 100%
Internal resistance (R₀) rises as a battery ages — typically doubling by end of life. The BMS measures it by watching the voltage drop when a large current step occurs: R₀ = ΔV / ΔI at the 10ms mark. A battery with high R₀ gets hot faster, wastes more energy as heat, and can deliver less peak power — even if its capacity is still acceptable. Resistance SoH catches this degradation separately from capacity SoH.
Cycle Aging
SoH_cyc = 1 − (N_equiv × k_cyc)
Every partial charge or discharge is counted as a fraction of a full cycle — the BMS accumulates these into a "full-cycle-equivalent" count. Each full equivalent cycle degrades LFP capacity by about 0.02%. After 3,000 full-equivalent cycles, cycling alone has cost 60% of the original capacity. (Thankfully, LFP's flat degradation curve means it degrades slowly and predictably all the way to that point.)
Calendar Aging (Arrhenius)
SoH_cal = 1 − A·√t·exp(−Ea/RT)
Even a battery sitting on a shelf is aging — the SEI layer slowly grows over time regardless of cycling. This formula captures that: the √t term comes from the physics of SEI growth (it slows down as the layer thickens). The Arrhenius exponential captures the temperature dependence — calendar aging doubles for every 10°C increase. A battery stored at 45°C ages twice as fast as one stored at 25°C, even if neither is ever used.

SoF and SoP — Operational Limits

SoF — State of Function
SoF asks: "Can this battery actually do its job right now?" It multiplies three factors together: how full it is (SoC), how cold it is (η(T)), and how degraded it is (SoH) — combining them into a single readiness percentage.

SoF = SoC × η(T) × SoH_C

Example: SoC=75%, cold at −5°C (η=0.75), slightly aged SoH=0.90: SoF = 0.75 × 0.75 × 0.90 = 50.6%

The inverter uses this to calculate real backup time. On a 5kWh pack with a 1kW load at 50.6% SoF: runtime = 5 × 0.506 / 1 = 2.53 hours. Without SoF, the inverter might report 3.75 hours based on SoC alone — and be dangerously wrong.
SoP — State of Power
SoP asks: "How much power can this battery safely push right now?" It is the answer the BMS sends to the inverter via CAN 0x351 every second, telling it the current ceiling for both charging and discharging.

P_chg_max = min(I_cc, I_cv, I_T_derate) × V_pack

Temperature squeezes the limit hard: at −20°C the BMS allows only 0.2C, at 0°C only 0.5C, at 25°C the full 1C, and at 45°C it backs off to 0.8C to avoid heat damage.

SoC also clamps the limits: below 10% SoC the discharge limit tapers linearly to zero at 5% (to avoid reversing a cell and destroying it). Above 95% SoC the charge limit drops to 0.2C to prevent lithium plating on an almost-full cell.

SOX Engine — Integration Code

/* SOX Engine — runs every 100ms */ void BMS_SOX_Update(BMS_State_t *bms, float dt_s) { /* ── 1. Coulomb Counting ── */ float eta_T = TempEfficiency(bms->temp_avg); /* temperature factor */ bms->soc -= (bms->pack_current * dt_s) / (bms->cap_full_ah * 3600.0f); bms->soc = clampf(bms->soc, 0.0f, 1.0f); /* ── 2. OCV Correction (at rest) ── */ if (fabsf(bms->pack_current) < (bms->cap_full_ah * 0.05f)) { /* <C/20 */ bms->rest_timer_s += dt_s; if (bms->rest_timer_s > 1800.0f) { /* 30min settled */ float ocv = bms->pack_voltage / 16.0f; /* average cell OCV */ float soc_ocv = OCV_to_SoC(ocv); /* lookup table */ bms->soc = bms->soc * 0.7f + soc_ocv * 0.3f; /* weighted blend */ bms->rest_timer_s = 0; } } else { bms->rest_timer_s = 0; } /* ── 3. SoF ── */ bms->sof = bms->soc * eta_T * bms->soh; /* ── 4. SoP — charge and discharge limits ── */ float i_derate = TemperatureDerating(bms->temp_avg); /* 0–1 factor */ float soc_derate_chg = (bms->soc > 0.95f) ? (1.0f - bms->soc) / 0.05f * 0.8f + 0.2f : 1.0f; float soc_derate_dsg = (bms->soc < 0.10f) ? bms->soc / 0.10f : 1.0f; bms->sop_charge_w = BMS_MAX_CHARGE_AMPS * i_derate * soc_derate_chg * bms->pack_voltage; bms->sop_discharge_w = BMS_MAX_DISCHARGE_AMPS * i_derate * soc_derate_dsg * bms->pack_voltage; /* ── 5. Cycle counting (increments on charge cycle end) ── */ bms->coulomb_accum += fabsf(bms->pack_current) * dt_s / 3600.0f; /* Ah */ if (bms->pack_current > 0 && bms->prev_current <= 0) { /* charge started */ bms->cycle_count += bms->coulomb_accum / (bms->cap_full_ah * 2); bms->coulomb_accum = 0; } bms->prev_current = bms->pack_current; }
Chapter Summary

Twelve Principles of BMS Architecture

① Physics Drives Architecture
Every BMS module exists because a specific battery physics problem requires it. The AFE exists because cells need per-cell voltage monitoring. The NVM exists because SoC must survive power cuts. Once you understand the physics, the architecture becomes obvious. Start with the traceability matrix and let the hardware follow.
② The AFE is the Safety Core
The AFE chip — not the microcontroller — is the last line of defence. Its short-circuit comparator reacts in microseconds, without waiting for any software. Its watchdog can shut down the system even if firmware is frozen. If the MCU is the only protection path and it crashes, nothing catches the fault. Always keep hardware protection independent of software.
③ Dedicated MCU, Always
When the BMS shares a microcontroller with the inverter, the inverter's code can hog CPU time and delay the 10ms safety measurement loop — the loop that catches overvoltage before a cell is damaged. A dedicated BMS MCU costs about $2. The warranty claim it prevents can cost $200 or more. Use a dedicated MCU.
④ CAN is the Language of ESS
250 kbps CAN with the PYLON-compatible message set (IDs 0x351–0x380) is what every major home energy storage inverter brand understands. Implement it correctly — including bus-off recovery and the 5-second silence timeout — and your BMS will plug into the whole ecosystem. Skip it and you are writing custom drivers for every inverter forever.
⑤ SoC Must Never Be Lost
Three-tier persistence: backup SRAM (1s resolution, instantaneous recovery), NVM checkpoint (60s resolution), OCV re-init (fallback, inaccurate on LFP plateau). Without BSRAM, every power cycle degrades SoC accuracy.
⑥ NVM Requires Wear Leveling
60s SoC writes × 10 years = 5.25M writes. Without wear leveling, a single EEPROM address wears out in 5 years. Rotate across 16+ pages, protect every record with CRC-16, always validate on startup.
⑦ RTC Enables Aging Intelligence
Calendar aging (Arrhenius) doubles every 10°C. Without an RTC, you cannot track time-at-temperature. Calendar aging is the dominant SoH degradation mechanism for storage applications. A coin cell keeps the RTC running for 11+ years.
⑧ Log Everything, Always
A 256-entry circular event log with RTC timestamps costs 2.5KB of EEPROM. It enables remote fault diagnosis, warranty root-cause analysis, and field upgrade planning. Without logs, failed packs are undiagnosable. Always log first, question storage size second.
⑨ State Machine Prevents Undefined Behaviour
A BMS without a formal state machine will eventually reach an undefined state — charge and discharge FETs both open, or both closed, under race conditions. The state machine with formal transition guards is the only way to guarantee deterministic behaviour.
⑩ SOX is a System, Not Four Numbers
SoC, SoH, SoF, SoP feed each other. SoP depends on SoC × T-derating. SoF depends on SoC × SoH × η(T). The host inverter adapts its power strategy every 100ms based on SoP — getting this wrong costs energy efficiency and causes premature cell damage.
⑪ World-Class = Hardware Fail-Safe + Persistent State + Adaptive Algorithms
Tesla, BYD, CATL all converge on the same architecture. Cell-level fusing. Distributed BMS. OTA-updatable firmware. Cloud-calibrated SoH. The $2000 gap between budget and world-class BMS is almost entirely firmware intelligence, not hardware cost.
⑫ Timing is Physics
<500μs for SCD (copper melts in ms). 10ms for OVP (Li plating starts in seconds). 100ms for SoC (load step response). 1s for balancing. 60s for NVM. 1hr for SoH. Each timing layer is set by the physics of the failure it prevents.

Quick Reference

BMS Architecture Reference Index

Key Equations

Coulomb counting SoC
SoC(t) = SoC₀ − ∫I·dt / C_full
Drift: ~0.5% per hour at 1C. Correct with OCV every rest period.
SoH capacity definition
SoH_C = C_full_now / C_design
Replace battery at SoH <0.80 (80%). Measure with calibration cycle.
Calendar aging (Arrhenius)
k_age = exp(−Ea/kT) / exp(−Ea/kT_ref)
Ea≈0.35eV (LFP). Doubles every 10°C. Integrate over time for total age.
SoF (functional readiness)
SoF = SoC × η(T) × SoH_C
Used for UPS runtime: t = C_design × SoF / I_load
SoP (max charge power)
P_chg = I_cc × derate(T, SoC) × V_pack
Sent in CAN 0x351 as CCL/CVL. Inverter must not exceed these limits.
NVM write life with leveling
N_life = N_endurance × N_pages
16 pages × 1M cycles = 16M writes = 30+ years at 60s interval.
AFE measurement time
t_meas = N_cells × t_convert
t_convert ≈ 1ms per cell (Δ-Σ). 16 cells = 16ms. Pipeline for 10ms loop.
NTC temperature (Steinhart-Hart)
1/T = A + B·ln(R) + C·ln(R)³
A,B,C from NTC datasheet. R = V_ref×R_pull/(V_adc−1) − R_pull simplified.

R&D Thinking: Why Super-Loop Over RTOS?

RTOS seems right but for a single-core BMS doing one job, a preemptive scheduler can interrupt your OVP check mid-execution to handle a UART event. Worst-case OVP response time becomes undefined.

Super-loop with SysTick gate gives determinism: WCET is measurable, certifiable, MISRA-C compatible. OVP checked at t=0, t=10ms, t=20ms - always. No priority inversion, no heap, no stack overflow risk.
