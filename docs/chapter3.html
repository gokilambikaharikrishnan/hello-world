Heat Generation in Li-ion Cells

Every Li-ion cell generates heat during charge and discharge. Understanding exactly where that heat comes from — and how much — is the first step in designing thermal management and setting BMS protection thresholds. There are three physically distinct heat sources, and the BMS must know all three.

🔥 Three Sources of Heat in a Li-ion Cell

Three independent heat generation mechanisms in a Li-ion cell. Joule heating dominates at high current; reaction heat depends on the difference between terminal voltage and OCV; entropic heat is reversible and small but measurable.

Source 1: Joule Heating — The Dominant Term

Joule heating is ohmic dissipation — electrical energy converted to heat as current flows through the cell's internal resistance. It is the dominant heat source in almost all practical conditions.

Joule Heating Power Q_J = I² × R_internal (Watts) Q_J = I² × (R₀ + R₁) (simplified Thevenin model) Critical insight: power scales with I², not I. Doubling the current quadruples the heat. This is the physical reason why high-C-rate operation is thermally dangerous. A 280Ah cell with R₀ = 0.4 mΩ at 1C (280A) dissipates: Q = 280² × 0.4×10⁻³ = 31.4 W. At 2C (560A): Q = 560² × 0.4×10⁻³ = 125.4 W — 4× more heat, not 2×.

The I² relationship has a profound implication for pack design: parallel cells share current, so a 1P pack at 2C and a 2P pack at 1C carry the same energy throughput, but the 2P pack generates one quarter of the heat. This is why high-power applications favor more parallel cells — the thermal load scales inversely with P².

⚛
Why R_internal Is Not a Single Resistor

The internal resistance seen during Joule heating is the sum of multiple contributions:
• Electrolyte resistance — ionic conductivity of LiPF₆ in EC/DMC solvent
• SEI resistance — Li⁺ transport through the anode passivation layer
• Charge transfer resistance — activation energy for intercalation reactions
• Contact resistance — cell tab, busbar, and terminal joints
• Current collector resistance — copper foil (anode) and aluminium foil (cathode)

Of these, SEI resistance grows over the cell's life, and contact resistance varies with temperature and assembly quality. The BMS's measured R₀ (from HPPC) is the sum of all these.

Real Numbers — Heat at Different C-Rates for LFP 280Ah

Using R_internal = 0.5 mΩ (R₀ + R₁, 25°C), nominal capacity 280Ah:

C-Rate Current (A) Joule Heat (W) Heat in 1h (Wh) Typical Use Case BMS Action
0.1C 28 A 0.39 W 0.39 Wh Float charge, idle No action needed
0.5C 140 A 9.8 W 9.8 Wh Normal home ESS charge/discharge Monitor temperature
1C 280 A 39.2 W 39.2 Wh UPS discharge, peak demand Active monitoring, derate if >40°C
2C 560 A 156.8 W 156.8 Wh High-power UPS switchover burst Time-limited, OTP armed at 45°C
3C 840 A 352.8 W 352.8 Wh Short circuit / fault condition SCP must fire within <500 μs

Notice that at 3C, the cell generates 352.8 W of heat — roughly equivalent to a clothes iron. In a 16-cell series pack all dissipating simultaneously, that is 5.6 kW of heat into the enclosure. Without short-circuit protection firing in microseconds, the pack would reach thermal runaway onset temperatures in minutes.

LFP 280Ah Cell — Heat Generation vs C-Rate
Source 2: Electrochemical Reaction Heat

When current flows and the terminal voltage deviates from the OCV, additional heat is generated by the irreversibility of the electrochemical reactions. This is sometimes called the polarisation heat or overpotential dissipation.

Reaction Heat Power Q_rxn = I × η_total where η_total = V_terminal − OCV During discharge (I > 0): V_terminal < OCV → η < 0 → heat generated During charge (I < 0): V_terminal > OCV → η > 0 → heat generated The overpotential η includes activation overpotential (charge transfer at electrode surface) and concentration overpotential (diffusion limitation). For LFP at moderate C-rates, this term is approximately 10–20 mV per cell, contributing 3–6 W at 280A — small but not negligible.
Source 3: Entropic Heat — The Reversible Component

Entropy changes during intercalation reactions produce a reversible heat exchange between the cell and its environment. Unlike Joule and reaction heat (always heat out), entropic heat can either heat or cool the cell depending on SoC:

Entropic Heat Q_S = I × T × (dU/dT) dU/dT = entropic coefficient (∂OCV/∂T), measured in V/K. For LFP: dU/dT ≈ +0.1 to −0.3 mV/K depending on SoC — changes sign across the plateau. At T = 298K and I = 280A, Q_S ≈ 280 × 298 × 0.0002 ≈ 16.7 W — comparable to reaction heat. Positive dU/dT means cell absorbs heat during discharge (endothermic — self-cooling). Negative dU/dT means cell releases additional heat (adds to Joule heating). Advanced thermal models in BMS firmware include the entropic term for precise temperature prediction.
🧊
LFP Self-Cooling Effect at Low SoC

Between 20–50% SoC, LFP has a slightly positive dU/dT, meaning the cell is slightly endothermic during discharge — it absorbs heat from its surroundings, slightly offsetting Joule heating. This is one reason why LFP cells run cooler than NMC at moderate rates in the mid-SoC range. The effect is small (a few watts) but measurable in precision calorimetry and is why some BMS firmware implementations include an SoC-dependent entropic offset in their thermal model.

Thermal Gradient Inside the Cell — Surface vs Core

Even with perfect external heat removal, a temperature gradient exists between the cell core and its surface during high-rate operation. This gradient is governed by the cell's thermal conductivity and geometry.

🌡 Temperature Profile Across a 280Ah Prismatic Cell at 1C Discharge

At 1C discharge, the prismatic cell core is approximately 5–10°C hotter than its surface. The NTC thermistor mounted on the surface reads T_ref while the active electrode stack runs hotter. The BMS must add a thermal lag correction: T_core ≈ T_surface + ΔT_lag(I, thermal_R).

Core-to-Surface Temperature Gradient ΔT = Q_gen × R_thermal_cell R_thermal ≈ thickness / (2 × λ × Area) for a slab geometry For a 280Ah prismatic cell: thickness ≈ 45mm, λ_radial ≈ 0.5 W/m·K, face area ≈ 0.04 m². R_thermal ≈ 0.045 / (2 × 0.5 × 0.04) ≈ 1.125 K/W. At 1C (Q_gen ≈ 39 W): ΔT ≈ 39 × 1.125 ≈ 44°C (across full thickness — actual is half this for symmetric cooling → ~22°C). This is why surface NTC readings underestimate core temperature. BMS over-temperature cutoff must be set conservatively to account for this lag.
