3.1

Heat Generation in Li-ion Cells

Every Li-ion cell produces heat when it charges or discharges. To design a safe battery system and set the right protection limits, you need to know where that heat comes from and how much of it to expect. There are three separate sources of heat inside the cell, and all three matter to the BMS.

🔥 Three Sources of Heat in a Li-ion Cell

Three independent heat generation mechanisms in a Li-ion cell. Joule heating dominates at high current; reaction heat depends on the difference between terminal voltage and OCV; entropic heat is reversible and small but measurable.

Source 1: Joule Heating — The Dominant Term

Joule heating is the heat produced when current flows through the cell's internal resistance — the same reason a wire gets warm when electricity runs through it. It is the biggest source of heat in almost all normal operating conditions.

Joule Heating Power Q_J = I² × R_internal (Watts) Q_J = I² × (R₀ + R₁) (simplified Thevenin model) The key point: heat grows with the square of the current, not the current itself. Double the current → four times the heat. Our 280Ah cell at 1C (280 A) produces 31.4 W of heat. At 2C (560 A) it produces 125.4 W — four times more, not two times.

This squared relationship matters for pack design too. If you add a second parallel cell, the current splits between them — each cell carries half. Half the current means one quarter the heat per cell. That is why high-power packs use more parallel cells: it cuts the thermal load dramatically without changing the total energy delivered.

⚛
Why Internal Resistance Is Made Up of Several Parts

The cell's internal resistance is not one simple resistor — it is the combined effect of several things happening at once:
• Electrolyte resistance — how easily lithium ions move through the liquid between the electrodes
• SEI resistance — how easily ions pass through the protective layer on the anode surface
• Charge transfer resistance — the energy needed to push lithium in and out of the crystal
• Contact resistance — friction at joints between the cell tabs, busbars, and terminals
• Current collector resistance — the copper foil at the anode and aluminium foil at the cathode

The SEI portion grows as the cell ages. Contact resistance varies with temperature and how well the pack was assembled. When the BMS measures R₀ using a pulse test, it sees all of these lumped together as one number.

Real Numbers — Heat at Different C-Rates for LFP 280Ah

Using R_internal = 0.5 mΩ (R₀ + R₁, 25°C), nominal capacity 280Ah:

Without short-circuit protection firing in microseconds, the pack would reach thermal runaway onset temperatures in minutes.

LFP 280Ah Cell — Heat Generation vs C-Rate
Source 2: Electrochemical Reaction Heat

The second heat source comes from the electrochemical reactions themselves. No chemical reaction is perfectly efficient — some of the energy that goes in (or comes out) gets lost as heat. You can measure this by comparing the actual terminal voltage to the resting OCV: the gap between them represents energy being wasted as heat.

Reaction Heat Power Q_rxn = I × η_total where η_total = V_terminal − OCV During discharge (I > 0): V_terminal < OCV → heat generated During charge (I < 0): V_terminal > OCV → heat generated For LFP at moderate charge rates, this gap is about 10–20 mV per cell — contributing roughly 3–6 W at 280 A. Small compared to Joule heating, but adds up across 16 cells.
Source 3: Entropic Heat — The Reversible Component

The third heat source is caused by entropy changes as lithium moves in and out of the crystal. Unlike the first two sources — which always produce heat — this one can go either way: it can add heat or actually absorb heat from the surroundings, depending on the cell's charge level.

Entropic Heat Q_S = I × T × (dU/dT) dU/dT is how much the resting voltage changes with temperature — about +0.1 to −0.3 mV per degree Kelvin for LFP, and it changes sign across the charge range. At room temperature (298 K) and 280 A: Q_S ≈ 16.7 W. Positive means the cell absorbs heat (self-cooling). Negative means it adds heat on top of Joule heating. Advanced BMS thermal models include this term for accurate temperature prediction.
🧊
LFP's Mild Self-Cooling at Mid Charge Levels

Between 20–50% state of charge, LFP actually absorbs a small amount of heat from its surroundings during discharge — partially cancelling the Joule heating. This is one reason LFP runs slightly cooler than NMC at moderate rates in the mid-range. The effect is only a few watts but is measurable, and is why some BMS firmware includes a state-of-charge-dependent thermal correction in its temperature model.

Thermal Gradient Inside the Cell — Surface vs Core

Even if you cool the outside of a cell perfectly, the inside still gets hotter than the surface. Heat is generated deep in the electrode stack and has to travel outward through the cell material — but that material is a poor heat conductor, so a temperature gap builds up between the centre (core) and the outer casing (surface).

🌡 Temperature Profile Across a 280Ah Prismatic Cell at 1C Discharge

At a 1C discharge rate, the inside of the cell is roughly 5–10°C hotter than the outside. Your temperature sensor (NTC) is glued to the surface, so it reads the cool side — not the hot centre. The BMS has to add a correction to estimate how much hotter the core actually is.

Core-to-Surface Temperature Gradient ΔT = Q_gen × R_thermal_cell R_thermal ≈ thickness / (2 × λ × Area) for a slab geometry For our 280Ah reference cell (45mm thick), the thermal resistance from core to surface is about 1.125 K/W. At 1C it generates ~39W of heat, giving a core-to-surface gap of up to ~22°C with cooling on both faces. This is why the BMS sets its over-temperature cutoff conservatively — the sensor on the outside always reads lower than the actual hottest point inside.
3.2

Thermal Runaway

Thermal runaway is the worst thing that can happen to a lithium battery. It is not a slow decline — it is a runaway chain reaction. Heat makes the cell hotter; the hotter the cell gets, the faster it generates even more heat; which makes it hotter still. Once this loop starts, it cannot be stopped. The battery burns itself up from the inside. The entire job of the BMS is to make sure this chain reaction never begins.

⚡
Definition: Thermal Runaway

Thermal runaway happens when the cell produces heat faster than it can lose it — and the extra heat speeds up the very reactions causing the heat. It's a vicious circle: more heat → faster reactions → even more heat. Once it starts, nothing outside the cell can stop it. The only winning move is to prevent it from starting in the first place, which is exactly what the BMS is designed to do.

What Triggers Thermal Runaway
⚡
Overcharge
Push a cell above 3.65V and lithium starts depositing as metal rather than inserting cleanly — the cathode also becomes unstable. The BMS over-voltage protection must cut the charge before this point.
🔥
External Heat
A neighbouring cell going into runaway, a nearby fire, or a stuck heater can bake a healthy cell hot enough to trigger its own chain reaction. Physical spacing and firewall barriers between cells are the main defence.
🔩
Internal Short
Dendrites (metallic spikes that grow during repeated overcharge or fast charge) can punch through the separator and connect the two electrodes directly inside the cell. All stored energy dumps as heat in seconds — with no external warning.
💥
Mechanical Damage
Crushing, a nail puncture, or a heavy drop can physically collapse the electrode layers inside the cell, creating the same kind of internal short as a dendrite. The BMS may see a sudden voltage drop or resistance change as the first warning.
The Three-Stage Thermal Runaway Cascade

Thermal runaway doesn't happen all at once — it unfolds in three stages, each one hotter than the last. Each stage can trigger the next. Knowing where you are in the cascade tells the BMS engineer whether there is still a chance to stop it — or whether it's already too late.

🔥 Thermal Runaway Cascade — Temperature Timeline

Thermal runaway progression in LFP vs NMC. NMC reaches catastrophic oxygen release at ~180°C; LFP requires ~270°C due to iron-phosphate bond stability. The animated curves show how rapidly temperature escalates once Stage 1 begins. BMS intervention window is Stage 0 → pre-Stage 1.

🟢
Stage 0 — Normal Operation
25–60°C
Safe Operating Zone — BMS Monitoring Active
The cell is working normally. Heat generated by current flow and chemistry is balanced by the cooling system. The BMS is watching temperature, voltage, and current the whole time. You have plenty of room to act here. Any sign of overheating should be caught and dealt with at this stage — that's the whole point of continuous thermal monitoring.
⚠
Stage 1 — SEI Decomposition (Last BMS Window)
90–120°C
SEI Layer Begins to Break Down
The protective SEI layer (the thin film that keeps the graphite anode stable) starts to break apart in an exothermic reaction — meaning it releases heat as it decomposes. This creates CO and CO₂ gas inside the cell, raising internal pressure. This is the last moment the BMS can stop escalation — by cutting the load, activating cooling, and alerting the host system. The temperature is rising at only 0.5–2°C per minute at this stage, which is fast enough to detect but slow enough to act on.
🔥
Stage 2 — Separator Failure (Unrecoverable)
130–160°C
Polyolefin Separator Melts — Internal Short Circuit Forms
The thin plastic sheet (separator) that keeps the positive and negative electrodes apart melts at 130–160°C. Once it collapses, the two electrodes touch — creating a permanent short circuit inside the cell. All the stored energy now dumps as heat, right inside the cell stack. For our 280Ah reference cell at 90% charge that's over 800 Wh — released in seconds to minutes. At this point, thermal runaway is irreversible. There is nothing the BMS can do — it's physically over.
💥
Stage 3A — Cathode Oxygen Release (NMC/NCA)
180–250°C
Layered Oxide Cathode Releases Oxygen — Fire and Explosion Risk
In NMC and NCA chemistries, the cathode crystal falls apart at high temperatures and releases pure oxygen. That oxygen then reacts explosively with the liquid electrolyte (which is flammable) — creating a self-feeding fire inside the cell. The result is venting, sustained fire, and potentially an explosion that can jump to neighbouring cells in seconds. This is why NMC packs need flame-retardant separators, ceramic coatings, and dedicated gas venting channels — none of which are needed in LFP packs.
🛡
Stage 3B — LFP Thermal Limit (Superior Safety)
270°C+
Olivine Structure Prevents Oxygen Release — No Combustion Chain
LFP's olivine crystal structure holds oxygen so tightly (bond energy: 544 kJ/mol) that even at 270°C the bonds don't break — so oxygen is never released. Without oxygen, there is no combustion chain reaction. If an LFP cell does reach full runaway, you get cell venting and electrolyte vapour — unpleasant, but not a sustained fire. This physical difference is why LFP is the standard chemistry for indoor UPS and home energy storage. Many building codes and insurance policies specifically allow LFP but require additional fire suppression for NMC in residential settings.
Cell-to-Cell Thermal Propagation in a Pack

One cell going into thermal runaway doesn't have to destroy the whole pack — but without careful design, it usually does. Heat from the failed cell bakes the cells next to it, pushing them toward their own runaway threshold, which then bakes the next cells, and so on.

🔥 Thermal Propagation in a 16-Cell Pack

Cell 7 (red) has gone into thermal runaway. Heat is radiating into cells 6 and 8 (orange/yellow), pushing them toward their own danger threshold. The cells further away are still cool (blue/green). Proper cell spacing, insulating gap-filler pads, and pyro fuses at each cell can stop the spread at cells 6 and 8 before it turns into a full pack failure.

🛡
Propagation Mitigation Strategies
  • Cell spacing ≥ 3 mm — reduces thermal coupling (air gap is an insulator)
  • Low-conductivity gap fillers — vermiculite or ceramic pads at 0.3–0.5 W/m·K between cells
  • Pyro fuses at each cell terminal — disconnect failed cell electrically within microseconds
  • BMS early detection — dT/dt monitoring triggers pack disconnect before Stage 2
  • Pack venting paths — allow gas to escape without pressurising the enclosure
  • Fire barrier plates — stainless steel or mica sheets between cells in high-density packs
⚗ R&D Thinking — Why Thermal Runaway Is Unstoppable Once Started

The core problem isn't just heat — it's self-acceleration. Every bit of heat released speeds up the chemical reactions, which release even more heat. Reaction rates roughly double for every 10°C rise (Arrhenius law). So by the time you detect 80°C inside the cell, it's already committed to destroying itself in 200–400 milliseconds — far too fast for any firmware to respond.

This is why the BMS can't "stop" thermal runaway once it's started. Its only tools are: prevent the conditions that start it (cut off on over-voltage, over-temperature, or short circuit) and contain propagation by opening the pack contactor before neighbouring cells get too hot.

The 130°C separator melt is the point of no return. After that, the internal short is physical — the electrodes are literally touching. No software can undo that.

3.3

Thermal Management in ESS and UPS Systems

Thermal management means keeping every cell in the pack at the right temperature — ideally between 15°C and 35°C — all the time. This isn't just about preventing fires. Temperature is the single biggest factor in how long a battery lasts. Run your pack 10°C hotter than necessary, and it ages twice as fast. Get the thermal management right and you can double the usable life of the pack.

Why Thermal Management Directly Determines Cycle Life
Arrhenius Aging Acceleration k(T) = k_ref × exp[Eₐ/R × (1/T_ref − 1/T)] Rule of thumb: aging rate doubles every +10°C A pack running at 35°C instead of 25°C ages about twice as fast. At 45°C, it ages four times faster. Over ten years, this is the difference between replacing the pack once or replacing it four times. For a home ESS pack costing $5,000–$8,000 to replace, keeping it just 5°C cooler on average can pay for the entire thermal management system many times over.
Passive Cooling — Natural Convection

Passive cooling uses no fans, pumps, or any powered components. Instead, heat travels through the cell casing and pack structure by conduction, then the warm air naturally rises and carries the heat away (natural convection). It's the simplest and most reliable approach — nothing to break or maintain.

✓
When Passive Cooling Is Sufficient

For a home ESS with LFP cells, if the average charge/discharge rate is below 0.5C and the room temperature stays below 30°C, passive cooling is generally enough to keep cells below 35°C. LFP has low internal resistance (≈ 0.5 mΩ), so at 0.5C it generates relatively little heat — typically 5–10 W per cell — and natural air movement can carry that heat away without the cell rising more than 10°C above room temperature.

⚠
BMS Must Control the Cooling System — Not Just Monitor

A common design mistake is to treat the BMS and the cooling system as two completely separate things with their own controllers. This creates a dangerous race condition: the BMS sees the pack overheating and cuts the load — but the cooling system is still blasting cold air and can push the cells below their minimum charge temperature limit. The BMS must control the cooling directly (fan speed, coolant valve position) via CAN or a dedicated interface. There should be one authority over the pack's thermal state: the BMS.

Temperature Gradients — The Hidden Threat to Pack Longevity

Even if the average temperature across the pack looks fine, a large difference between the hottest and coolest cells is still damaging. Cells age faster when they're hotter — a 10°C difference means the hot cells age twice as fast as the cool ones. Over time, this causes the cells to drift apart in capacity, even if they all started identical and the BMS has been balancing them perfectly.

🌡 Pack Temperature Gradient — Acceptable vs Problematic

Left: a well-cooled pack with less than 5°C temperature spread — cells age at the same rate. Right: a poorly designed pack with a 22°C spread — the hot (red) cells age four times faster, gradually drifting out of balance even if the BMS is actively balancing them. Design goal: keep the spread below 5°C across all cells at all times.

Pack Temperature Uniformity Target ΔT_pack = T_max − T_min across all cell temperature sensors Target: ΔT_pack < 5°C during normal operation Warning threshold: ΔT_pack > 8°C → reduce load, increase cooling Alarm threshold: ΔT_pack > 12°C → investigate thermal path failure The BMS calculates ΔT_pack every measurement cycle and logs it as a health indicator. If ΔT_pack slowly grows over months (with the same load), that's a warning sign of a deteriorating thermal path — maybe the gap-filler pad dried out, a vent got blocked, or a fan is starting to fail — caught early, before any single cell crosses its temperature limit.
NTC Placement Strategy — Where to Measure and Why

The BMS's thermal model is only as good as where its sensors are placed. A single temperature sensor on the circuit board tells you almost nothing about what's happening inside the cells in a 16-cell pack. Placement matters enormously.

Critical
Cell Midpoint Surface
Pressed flat against the large face of the cell at mid-height — this is where the surface temperature peaks. You need at least one sensor for every two cells.
Important
Positive Tab Junction
Placed at or near where the busbar is welded to the cell tab. The tab carries the full pack current, making it the hottest electrical connection point. This spot must stay below 80°C.
Important
Pack Inlet Air / Coolant
Measures how warm the incoming air or coolant already is. The BMS uses this to figure out how much cooling headroom is available and to pre-adjust fan or pump speed.
Important
Pack Outlet Air / Coolant
Comparing outlet temperature to inlet temperature (multiplied by flow rate) tells you exactly how much heat the cooling system is actually removing. This is how the BMS verifies that cooling is working as expected.
Recommended
BMS PCB Ambient
A reference for the BMS chip itself — the AFE ICs have their own temperature limits (typically −40°C to +85°C) and this sensor tells the firmware whether the BMS electronics are getting too hot.
Optional
Cell Bottom Centre
The best spot to catch a developing hot spot inside the cell. The core heats up 30–120 seconds before the surface at 1C — bottom sensors pick this up faster than side sensors on prismatic cells.
⚠
Surface Temperature Underestimates Core Temperature

At 1C discharge, the cell's interior is typically 5–15°C hotter than what the surface sensor reads. If your surface sensor shows 50°C, the core might already be at 60–65°C — dangerously close to the temperature where the SEI layer starts to break down. The BMS must correct for this lag using a simple formula based on current and cell thermal resistance:

T_core_est = T_surface + k_lag × I² × R_thermal_cell

k_lag is a correction factor you measure once during cell testing — by inserting a thermocouple into the centre of a test cell and comparing it with the surface sensor at different currents. For a 280Ah prismatic LFP cell, k_lag ≈ 0.08 K/A².

3.4

Mechanical Considerations — Swelling, Compression, and Vibration

Lithium-ion cells aren't just chemical devices — they're also mechanical ones. Every time you charge or discharge, the electrodes physically swell and shrink. Do this thousands of times and the accumulated mechanical stress damages cell health, loosens electrical contacts between cells, and can crack the pack structure. The BMS can't measure swelling directly, but the pack's physical design has to be built around it.

LFP Electrode Swelling — The Lithiation Expansion

When the cell charges, lithium ions squeeze into the graphite anode — and this forces the carbon crystal to expand, like packing more people into a crowd. When the cell discharges, the lithium leaves and the graphite contracts again. This happens every single cycle:

Volume Change During Intercalation LFP cathode: LiFePO₄ ↔ FePO₄ + Li⁺ + e⁻ LFP volume change: ΔV/V ≈ 6.8% (a-axis contracts, b-axis expands) Graphite anode: Li_x C₆ — expands ~10% at full lithiation (x=1) Net prismatic cell thickness change: ~2% (LFP) vs ~4–5% (NMC) Our 280Ah reference cell (45mm thick when empty) swells by about 0.9mm when fully charged. In a 16-cell pack, all cells expand together — that's 16 × 0.9mm = 14.4mm of total movement, nearly 1.5 cm. The pack's mounting frame and end-plates must be designed to flex with this, or they'll crack or crush the cells.

⚠
Irreversible Swelling — The Gassing Indicator

The swelling that happens every charge/discharge cycle is completely normal — the cell returns to the same thickness each time. The problem is irreversible swelling, where the cell gets permanently thicker over time. This means gas is being generated inside the cell, most commonly from: (1) overcharging above 3.65V (electrolyte breaks down at the cathode), (2) the SEI layer continuing to grow on the anode (producing CO₂), or (3) water contamination in the electrolyte (which generates corrosive HF gas). Permanent swelling is an early warning that the cell is approaching end of life. Advanced packs fit a strain gauge or load cell into the compression frame to track this as a health metric — a slowly growing force reading means the cells are gassing.

Compression Fixtures — Controlled Pressure for Better Performance

It sounds counterintuitive, but squeezing prismatic LFP cells with a controlled, steady pressure actually improves their performance and helps them last longer. Here's why:

  • Keeps the electrode layers pressed tightly together, reducing the resistance between them
  • Stops the layers peeling apart as the cell expands and contracts through each cycle
  • Leaves less free space inside for gas bubbles to collect in the electrode stack
  • Slows SEI layer growth by limiting how much the anode can expand each cycle
Optimal Compression Pressure P_optimal = 10–30 kPa (1.5–4.4 PSI) for prismatic LFP 280Ah P_max = 100 kPa — above this, electrolyte expulsion and capacity loss The pressure is applied using end-plates and tie-rods, or an aluminium channel that wraps around the cell stack. Critically, the fixture must include springs (Belleville washers or spring plates) so that pressure stays roughly constant even as the cells swell. A rigid, non-spring fixture lets pressure build as the cells expand — eventually crushing them beyond P_max. Design target: 15 kPa preload at 50% SoC (the midpoint), with a ±10 kPa range as cells breathe.

Vibration and Shock — UPS and Home ESS Requirements

UPS and home ESS packs go through physical stress before they even arrive at the installation site — vibration during shipping, rough handling during installation, and (for floor-mounted systems) constant low-level building vibration once in service. Industry standards specify exactly what tests a pack must survive:

From the BMS's perspective, vibration and shock testing mostly stress the physical connections rather than the firmware. The typical failure points are crimped cell-sense wires, balancing lead connections, and NTC mounts coming loose. Use proper vibration-rated connectors (such as Molex MX150) — not the hobby-grade JST or Dupont headers — for any wiring inside the BMS assembly.

3.5

BMS Thermal Features — From Physics to Firmware

Every temperature number in BMS firmware has a real physical reason behind it. This section traces each protection limit and control action back to the electrochemistry and physics that justify it — so you understand not just what the thresholds are, but why they are set where they are.

Overtemperature Charge Cutoff — Why 45°C

Most LFP BMS designs use 45°C as a charge warning and 55°C as a hard cutoff. These numbers aren't guesses — they come directly from how fast the SEI layer grows at different temperatures.

SEI Growth Rate vs Temperature d(SEI)/dt ∝ exp(−Eₐ_SEI / RT) [Arrhenius] At 35°C (308K): relative rate = exp(−50000 / 8.314 / 308) = 1.0 (reference) At 45°C (318K): relative rate = exp(−50000 / 8.314 / 318) ≈ 2.1 × reference At 55°C (328K): relative rate = exp(−50000 / 8.314 / 328) ≈ 4.3 × reference Charging is what drives SEI growth, because it pushes lithium ions into the graphite surface where the SEI forms. Above 45°C, this growth rate doubles — which means cycle life is halved if you keep charging at that temperature. The 45°C warning gives the BMS time to slow down before hitting the hard cutoff. Above 55°C, SEI is growing four times faster than baseline — continued charging is unacceptable. Importantly, discharge is allowed up to 60°C, because discharging pulls lithium out of the graphite (no SEI formation opportunity) — the only concern then is electrolyte stability, which has a higher limit.
Undertemperature Charge Cutoff — Why 0°C is Absolute

Below 0°C, the BMS must block all charging — not reduce it, not slow it down, but stop it completely. This is the single most important temperature rule in the entire firmware.

🧊
Charging Below 0°C = Lithium Plating = Dendritic Short Circuit Risk

Below 0°C, the graphite anode can't absorb lithium ions fast enough during charging. The ions arrive at the surface but can't squeeze into the crystal lattice in time — so instead they deposit as solid metallic lithium on the surface. This metallic lithium grows as thin needle-like spikes called dendrites. If a dendrite grows long enough to punch through the separator and touch the cathode, you have a sudden internal short circuit — all the stored energy dumps as heat in seconds, with thermal runaway a real possibility.

The BMS rule is unconditional: charge current = 0 when any cell is below 0°C. No exceptions. Not "just 0.1C". Not "gentle warm-up charge". Any charge at sub-zero temperatures risks dendrite formation.

This table lives in the BMS firmware as a lookup, indexed by the coldest cell temperature across all sensors. The maximum allowed charge current is whichever is smaller: what the charger is asking for, or what the table allows at the current temperature. The BMS enforces this limit — the charger doesn't get a vote.

Between the warning and hard cutoff temperatures, the BMS doesn't just switch off abruptly — it smoothly reduces the allowed charge current using a linear derating curve:

Thermal Runaway Detection in BMS Firmware — dT/dt Monitoring

The best early warning sign for thermal runaway is how fast the temperature is rising — the dT/dt (rate of temperature rise). A cell entering Stage 1 decomposition will get hotter faster than the current level alone can explain — the temperature climbs even without any increase in load. That's the signature the BMS is looking for.

dT/dt Detection Thresholds Normal operation: dT/dt < 0.1°C/s (6°C/min) Level 1 alarm: dT/dt > 0.5°C/s (30°C/min) → reduce load, increase cooling Level 2 alarm: dT/dt > 1.0°C/s (60°C/min) → disconnect pack immediately Thermal runaway: dT/dt > 2.0°C/s (120°C/min) → alert fire suppression These thresholds apply to the fastest-rising sensor in the pack. A healthy cell running at 2C generates heat slowly enough to produce only about 0.1°C/s of temperature rise. When the SEI layer starts decomposing (Stage 1), you see 0.5–1°C/s — without any matching increase in current. That disconnect between temperature rise and current is the unmistakable thermal runaway signature the BMS is trained to catch.
/* ============================================================ BMS Thermal Runaway Detection — Embedded C Runs in 100ms task (10 Hz sampling) ============================================================ */ typedef struct { float T_prev[MAX_SENSORS]; /* Previous temperature readings */ float dTdt[MAX_SENSORS]; /* Rate of temperature rise (°C/s) */ float dTdt_max; /* Maximum dT/dt across all sensors */ uint8_t alarm_level; /* 0=OK 1=warn 2=critical 3=runaway */ } ThermalMonitor_t; void BMS_ThermalRunawayCheck(ThermalMonitor_t *tm, float *T_now) { const float DT_SAMPLE = 0.1f; /* 100ms sample period (seconds) */ const float L1_THRESH = 0.5f; /* dT/dt Level 1 alarm threshold °C/s */ const float L2_THRESH = 1.0f; /* dT/dt Level 2 cutoff threshold */ const float TR_THRESH = 2.0f; /* Thermal runaway threshold */ tm->dTdt_max = 0.0f; for (int i = 0; i < MAX_SENSORS; i++) { /* Low-pass filter to reject noise */ tm->dTdt[i] = 0.8f * tm->dTdt[i] + 0.2f * (T_now[i] - tm->T_prev[i]) / DT_SAMPLE; tm->T_prev[i] = T_now[i]; if (tm->dTdt[i] > tm->dTdt_max) tm->dTdt_max = tm->dTdt[i]; } /* Classify alarm level */ if (tm->dTdt_max >= TR_THRESH) tm->alarm_level = 3; /* THERMAL RUNAWAY */ else if (tm->dTdt_max >= L2_THRESH) tm->alarm_level = 2; /* CRITICAL — cutoff */ else if (tm->dTdt_max >= L1_THRESH) tm->alarm_level = 1; /* WARNING — derate */ else tm->alarm_level = 0; /* NORMAL */ /* Level 2+: open main FETs immediately */ if (tm->alarm_level >= 2) { BMS_OpenAllFETs(); /* Disconnect pack from load */ BMS_SetFaultLatch(FAULT_THERMAL); /* Latch — requires manual reset */ CAN_SendAlert(ALERT_THERMAL_RUNAWAY_RISK); } /* Level 3: fire suppression alert if connected */ if (tm->alarm_level >= 3) { GPIO_Assert(PIN_FIRE_SUPPRESSION_TRIGGER); BMS_LogEvent("THERMAL_RUNAWAY", T_now, tm->dTdt); } }
💡
Low-Pass Filter Is Essential for dT/dt Measurement

Calculating dT/dt directly from raw sensor readings is noisy — tiny electrical noise on the 12-bit ADC creates phantom temperature spikes every 100ms that look like rapid heating. The exponential moving average filter (α = 0.2 in the code) smooths these out while still responding within about 1 second to a real thermal event. Getting α right matters: set it too low (too much smoothing) and the BMS responds too slowly to real runaway; too high (too little smoothing) and electrical noise causes false disconnections.

Multi-Sensor Voting Logic — Preventing False Alarms

A single faulty temperature sensor — shorted, open, or just drifted — can generate a fake overtemperature reading and shut the pack down unnecessarily. In a UPS, that's the worst possible outcome: the power fails exactly when the backup is needed. To guard against this, production BMS firmware uses voting logic — it requires multiple sensors to agree before triggering a protection action:

/* Multi-sensor voting — require 2 of N sensors to agree on alarm Prevents single-sensor failures from taking the pack offline */ uint8_t ThermalVote(float *T, uint8_t n_sensors, float threshold) { uint8_t votes = 0; for (int i = 0; i < n_sensors; i++) { if (T[i] > threshold) votes++; } return (votes >= 2) ? 1 : 0; /* Require 2+ sensors to confirm */ }

For thermal runaway detection specifically, drop to a 1-of-N vote — any single sensor showing runaway-level dT/dt should immediately disconnect the pack. A false positive (UPS briefly offline) is a minor inconvenience; a missed true positive (cell fire) is catastrophic. Use 2-of-N voting only for the lower warning thresholds, where false positives are common but consequences are manageable.

Summary

Chapter 3 Summary — The Thermal Physics Behind Every BMS Threshold

Thermal and mechanical behaviour is not a peripheral concern — it is the direct physical basis for the most critical safety features in the BMS. The following traces each key concept to its firmware manifestation.

01
Q_J = I² × R
→
Heat scales with I² not I — the reason high C-rates are thermally dangerous and why parallel cells dramatically reduce thermal load per cell.
02
Surface ≠ Core Temp
→
NTC reads 5–15°C below actual cell core temperature at 1C. BMS OT thresholds must be set conservatively (45°C surface = ~55°C core). Firmware applies T_core_est correction.
03
Stage 1 = Last Window
→
SEI decomposition at 90–120°C is the last point where BMS intervention can prevent thermal runaway. dT/dt > 0.5°C/s detected while T < 90°C means Stage 1 is starting. Disconnect immediately.
04
LFP — P–O Bond
→
544 kJ/mol P–O bond prevents oxygen release below 270°C. LFP thermal runaway is less catastrophic than NMC/NCA. No self-fuelling combustion chain. The physical basis for indoor ESS safety.
05
Arrhenius Aging
→
Every 10°C over optimal doubles aging rate. Thermal management that reduces average operating temp by 5°C increases calendar life by ~40%. The economic argument for good thermal design.
06
ΔT_pack < 5°C
→
Temperature gradient causes differential aging — hot cells age 2–4× faster. Balancing cannot compensate for aging-driven imbalance. Uniform temperature is the only solution.
07
I_charge = 0 at <0°C
→
Lithium plating below freezing forms dendrites → separator puncture → internal short → thermal runaway. This is an unconditional BMS rule — no exceptions for "gentle charge".
08
Cell Swelling ~2%
→
LFP 280Ah expands ~0.9 mm per cycle. Pack fixtures need spring compliance. Irreversible swelling indicates gassing — early EOL indicator detectable by thickness measurement or strain gauge.
09
dT/dt Monitoring
→
Rate of temperature rise, not absolute temperature, is the early thermal runaway signal. dT/dt > 1°C/s with no proportional current = exothermic reaction in progress. Requires 10Hz sampling minimum.
10
2-of-N Sensor Vote
→
Single failed NTC causes false alarm → UPS goes offline when needed most. Voting logic requires 2+ sensors to agree on warning threshold. Use 1-of-N for thermal runaway detection — no false negatives allowed.
