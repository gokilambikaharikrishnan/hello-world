3.1

Heat Generation in Li-ion Cells

Every Li-ion cell generates heat during charge and discharge. Understanding exactly where that heat comes from — and how much — is the first step in designing thermal management and setting BMS protection thresholds. There are three physically distinct heat sources, and the BMS must know all three.

🔥 Three Sources of Heat in a Li-ion Cell

Three independent heat generation mechanisms in a Li-ion cell. Joule heating dominates at high current; reaction heat depends on the difference between terminal voltage and OCV; entropic heat is reversible and small but measurable.

Source 1: Joule Heating — The Dominant Term

Joule heating is ohmic dissipation — electrical energy converted to heat as current flows through the cell's internal resistance. It is the dominant heat source in almost all practical conditions.

Joule Heating Power Q_J = I² × R_internal (Watts) Q_J = I² × (R₀ + R₁) (simplified Thevenin model) Critical insight: power scales with I², not I. Doubling the current quadruples the heat. This is the physical reason why high-C-rate operation is thermally dangerous. A 280Ah cell with R₀ = 0.4 mΩ at 1C (280A) dissipates: Q = 280² × 0.4×10⁻³ = 31.4 W. At 2C (560A): Q = 560² × 0.4×10⁻³ = 125.4 W — 4× more heat, not 2×.

The I² relationship has a profound implication for pack design: parallel cells share current, so a 1P pack at 2C and a 2P pack at 1C carry the same energy throughput, but the 2P pack generates one quarter of the heat. This is why high-power applications favor more parallel cells — the thermal load scales inversely with P².

⚛
Why R_internal Is Not a Single Resistor

The internal resistance seen during Joule heating is the sum of multiple contributions:
• Electrolyte resistance — ionic conductivity of LiPF₆ in EC/DMC solvent
• SEI resistance — Li⁺ transport through the anode passivation layer
• Charge transfer resistance — activation energy for intercalation reactions
• Contact resistance — cell tab, busbar, and terminal joints
• Current collector resistance — copper foil (anode) and aluminium foil (cathode)

Of these, SEI resistance grows over the cell's life, and contact resistance varies with temperature and assembly quality. The BMS's measured R₀ (from HPPC) is the sum of all these.

Real Numbers — Heat at Different C-Rates for LFP 280Ah

Using R_internal = 0.5 mΩ (R₀ + R₁, 25°C), nominal capacity 280Ah:

C-Rate Current (A) Joule Heat (W) Heat in 1h (Wh) Typical Use Case BMS Action
0.1C 28 A 0.39 W 0.39 Wh Float charge, idle No action needed
0.5C 140 A 9.8 W 9.8 Wh Normal home ESS charge/discharge Monitor temperature
1C 280 A 39.2 W 39.2 Wh UPS discharge, peak demand Active monitoring, derate if >40°C
2C 560 A 156.8 W 156.8 Wh High-power UPS switchover burst Time-limited, OTP armed at 45°C

Notice that at 3C, the cell generates 352.8 W of heat — roughly equivalent to a clothes iron. In a 16-cell series pack all dissipating simultaneously, that is 5.6 kW of heat into the enclosure. Without short-circuit protection firing in microseconds, the pack would reach thermal runaway onset temperatures in minutes.

LFP 280Ah Cell — Heat Generation vs C-Rate
Source 2: Electrochemical Reaction Heat

When current flows and the terminal voltage deviates from the OCV, additional heat is generated by the irreversibility of the electrochemical reactions. This is sometimes called the polarisation heat or overpotential dissipation.

Reaction Heat Power Q_rxn = I × η_total where η_total = V_terminal − OCV During discharge (I > 0): V_terminal < OCV → η < 0 → heat generated During charge (I < 0): V_terminal > OCV → η > 0 → heat generated The overpotential η includes activation overpotential (charge transfer at electrode surface) and concentration overpotential (diffusion limitation). For LFP at moderate C-rates, this term is approximately 10–20 mV per cell, contributing 3–6 W at 280A — small but not negligible.
Source 3: Entropic Heat — The Reversible Component

Entropy changes during intercalation reactions produce a reversible heat exchange between the cell and its environment. Unlike Joule and reaction heat (always heat out), entropic heat can either heat or cool the cell depending on SoC:

Entropic Heat Q_S = I × T × (dU/dT) dU/dT = entropic coefficient (∂OCV/∂T), measured in V/K. For LFP: dU/dT ≈ +0.1 to −0.3 mV/K depending on SoC — changes sign across the plateau. At T = 298K and I = 280A, Q_S ≈ 280 × 298 × 0.0002 ≈ 16.7 W — comparable to reaction heat. Positive dU/dT means cell absorbs heat during discharge (endothermic — self-cooling). Negative dU/dT means cell releases additional heat (adds to Joule heating). Advanced thermal models in BMS firmware include the entropic term for precise temperature prediction.
🧊
LFP Self-Cooling Effect at Low SoC

Between 20–50% SoC, LFP has a slightly positive dU/dT, meaning the cell is slightly endothermic during discharge — it absorbs heat from its surroundings, slightly offsetting Joule heating. This is one reason why LFP cells run cooler than NMC at moderate rates in the mid-SoC range. The effect is small (a few watts) but measurable in precision calorimetry and is why some BMS firmware implementations include an SoC-dependent entropic offset in their thermal model.

Thermal Gradient Inside the Cell — Surface vs Core

Even with perfect external heat removal, a temperature gradient exists between the cell core and its surface during high-rate operation. This gradient is governed by the cell's thermal conductivity and geometry.

🌡 Temperature Profile Across a 280Ah Prismatic Cell at 1C Discharge

At 1C discharge, the prismatic cell core is approximately 5–10°C hotter than its surface. The NTC thermistor mounted on the surface reads T_ref while the active electrode stack runs hotter. The BMS must add a thermal lag correction: T_core ≈ T_surface + ΔT_lag(I, thermal_R).

Core-to-Surface Temperature Gradient ΔT = Q_gen × R_thermal_cell R_thermal ≈ thickness / (2 × λ × Area) for a slab geometry For a 280Ah prismatic cell: thickness ≈ 45mm, λ_radial ≈ 0.5 W/m·K, face area ≈ 0.04 m². R_thermal ≈ 0.045 / (2 × 0.5 × 0.04) ≈ 1.125 K/W. At 1C (Q_gen ≈ 39 W): ΔT ≈ 39 × 1.125 ≈ 44°C (across full thickness — actual is half this for symmetric cooling → ~22°C). This is why surface NTC readings underestimate core temperature. BMS over-temperature cutoff must be set conservatively to account for this lag.
3.2

Thermal Runaway

Thermal runaway is the defining failure mode of Li-ion batteries. It is not a gradual degradation — it is a self-accelerating catastrophic event where exothermic reactions generate more heat than the cell can dissipate, driving temperature higher, which accelerates more reactions, in a feedback loop that cannot be stopped once triggered. The BMS exists, in part, to ensure this loop never starts.

⚡
Definition: Thermal Runaway

Thermal runaway occurs when the heat generation rate within a cell exceeds the heat dissipation rate, and the resulting temperature rise further accelerates exothermic reactions — creating a positive feedback loop (dQ_gen/dT > dQ_diss/dT) that is thermodynamically self-sustaining. Once initiated, no external intervention can prevent the cell from consuming itself. Prevention — through the BMS — is the only strategy.

What Triggers Thermal Runaway
⚡
Overcharge
Above 3.65V/cell, lithium metal plates on anode, cathode becomes unstable. BMS OVP must fire before this.
🔥
External Heat
Adjacent cell TR, fire exposure, or failed heater above 60°C. Pack spacing and thermal barriers are the defense.
🔩
Internal Short
Dendrite penetrates separator, metallic short releases all stored energy internally. No external trigger — the most dangerous.
💥
Mechanical Damage
Crushing, nail penetration, or drop damage collapses cell layers, creating internal short. Detected by sudden IR drop.
The Three-Stage Thermal Runaway Cascade

Thermal runaway does not happen in one step. It progresses through three distinct stages, each driving temperature higher and triggering the next. Understanding each stage tells the BMS engineer exactly where in the cascade an intervention is possible.

🔥 Thermal Runaway Cascade — Temperature Timeline

Thermal runaway progression in LFP vs NMC. NMC reaches catastrophic oxygen release at ~180°C; LFP requires ~270°C due to iron-phosphate bond stability. The animated curves show how rapidly temperature escalates once Stage 1 begins. BMS intervention window is Stage 0 → pre-Stage 1.

🟢
Stage 0 — Normal Operation
25–60°C
Safe Operating Zone — BMS Monitoring Active
Cell operates normally. Joule and reaction heat balanced by thermal dissipation. BMS monitors temperature, voltage, and current continuously. Intervention window is wide. Any approaching thermal event should be detected and arrested here — this is the entire purpose of the thermal monitoring subsystem.
⚠
Stage 1 — SEI Decomposition (Last BMS Window)
90–120°C
SEI Layer Begins to Break Down
The Solid Electrolyte Interphase on the graphite anode begins to decompose exothermically. Reactions: Li₂CO₃ + C → Li₂O + CO + C (ΔH ≈ −300 kJ/mol, exothermic). CO and CO₂ gas generation begins, causing internal pressure rise. This is the last point where BMS intervention can prevent escalation — by cutting load, triggering cooling, and alerting the host system. dT/dt at Stage 1 onset: typically 0.5–2°C/min — easily detectable by the BMS.
🔥
Stage 2 — Separator Failure (Unrecoverable)
130–160°C
Polyolefin Separator Melts — Internal Short Circuit Forms
The PE/PP separator (melting point 130–160°C) collapses, allowing direct contact between anode and cathode. This creates a permanent internal short circuit that discharges all remaining stored energy as heat in the electrode stack. At this point, thermal runaway is irreversible. The energy release is approximately: E = Q_stored × V_nom = e.g. 280Ah × 0.9 SoC × 3.2V = 806 Wh — all released as heat in seconds to minutes. No BMS action can stop this stage.
💥
Stage 3A — Cathode Oxygen Release (NMC/NCA)
180–250°C
Layered Oxide Cathode Releases Oxygen — Fire and Explosion Risk
In NMC and NCA, the layered oxide cathode (LiNiMnCoO₂) undergoes oxygen release: LiNiO₂ → LiNiO₂₋ₓ + x/2 O₂. This free oxygen reacts violently with organic electrolyte solvents (EC, DMC), creating a self-fuelling combustion reaction. Result: venting, fire, and potentially explosion. Cell-to-cell propagation in a pack occurs in seconds. This is why NMC packs require flame-retardant separators, ceramic coatings, and gas venting channels — all absent from LFP designs.
🛡
Stage 3B — LFP Thermal Limit (Superior Safety)
270°C+
Olivine Structure Prevents Oxygen Release — No Combustion Chain
LiFePO₄ has an olivine crystal structure where PO₄³⁻ polyanions are covalently bonded to Fe²⁺ with a P–O bond dissociation energy of 544 kJ/mol. Even at 270°C, this bond does not break — oxygen is never released from the crystal lattice. Without oxygen release, there is no combustion chain reaction. LFP thermal runaway at Stage 3 results in cell venting and electrolyte vapour release, but not sustained fire. This is the physical reason LFP is the chemistry of choice for indoor UPS and home ESS. Insurance and building codes in many jurisdictions specifically permit LFP but not NMC for residential installations without additional fire suppression.
Cell-to-Cell Thermal Propagation in a Pack

Even if one cell undergoes thermal runaway, the pack does not have to. But without design measures, it almost always does. Heat from the failed cell transfers to adjacent cells, raising them toward their own Stage 1 threshold — creating a cascade.

🔥 Thermal Propagation in a 16-Cell Pack

Cell 7 (red) has entered thermal runaway. Heat radiates to cells 6 and 8 (orange/yellow), which are being driven toward their own Stage 1 threshold. Cells farther away are still cool (blue/green). Cell spacing ≥ 3mm, thermal gap-filler pads with low conductivity, and pyro fuses at each cell can arrest propagation at cell 6/8 before the cascade goes pack-wide.

🛡
Propagation Mitigation Strategies
  • Cell spacing ≥ 3 mm — reduces thermal coupling (air gap is an insulator)
  • Low-conductivity gap fillers — vermiculite or ceramic pads at 0.3–0.5 W/m·K between cells
  • Pyro fuses at each cell terminal — disconnect failed cell electrically within microseconds
  • BMS early detection — dT/dt monitoring triggers pack disconnect before Stage 2
  • Pack venting paths — allow gas to escape without pressurising the enclosure
  • Fire barrier plates — stainless steel or mica sheets between cells in high-density packs
3.3

Thermal Management in ESS and UPS Systems

Thermal management is the engineering discipline of keeping every cell in the pack within its optimal temperature window (15–35°C) at all times. This is not just about preventing thermal runaway — it is about maximising cycle life. Arrhenius law guarantees that every 10°C of unnecessary heat cuts calendar life in half.

Why Thermal Management Directly Determines Cycle Life
Arrhenius Aging Acceleration k(T) = k_ref × exp[Eₐ/R × (1/T_ref − 1/T)] Rule of thumb: aging rate doubles every +10°C A pack that runs at 35°C instead of 25°C ages ~2× faster. A pack at 45°C ages ~4× faster. Over 10 years of operation, this is the difference between replacing the pack once and replacing it four times. For a 15 kWh home ESS pack at $5,000–$8,000 replacement cost, thermal management that saves 5°C average operating temperature pays for itself many times over in extended pack life.
Passive Cooling — Natural Convection

Passive cooling relies on natural convection (air currents driven by buoyancy of warm air) and conduction through the cell housing and pack structure. No fans, pumps, or active control — the simplest and most reliable thermal design.

✓
When Passive Cooling Is Sufficient

For LFP home ESS systems with average C-rates below 0.5C and ambient below 30°C, passive cooling with ≥5 mm inter-cell spacing and adequate pack-level ventilation is generally sufficient to keep cells below 35°C. The low internal resistance of LFP (R ≈ 0.5 mΩ) generates relatively little heat at 0.5C, and natural convection can remove 5–10 W/cell without temperature rise above ambient +10°C.

Passive Cooling Element Function Design Requirement Failure Mode
Cell spacing (air gap) Convective heat transfer path between cells ≥ 3–5 mm between adjacent cell faces Cells touching → zero convection → hot spot
Aluminium end plates Conduct heat from cell tabs to pack shell λ_Al = 200 W/m·K — use ≥ 3mm thickness Corrosion at tab contact → high thermal resistance
Thermal gap filler Fill air gaps between cell and heat spreader λ ≥ 3 W/m·K, compressible to fill voids Dry-out or delamination over thermal cycles

Active Cooling — Forced Air and Liquid

When passive cooling is insufficient — typically above 1C continuous discharge or ambient temperatures above 35°C — active cooling is required.

💨 Forced Air
Heat transfer coefficient: 20–80 W/m²·K
Suitable for: <5 kW continuous, ambient <40°C
Fan power: 5–30 W per kW of pack output
Pro: Simple, no liquid risk, easy maintenance
Con: Dust ingress, noise, limited at high power
BMS integration: Fan speed proportional to T_cell − T_setpoint via PID
💧 Liquid Cooling
Heat transfer coefficient: 500–5000 W/m²·K
Suitable for: >10 kW, all ambient conditions
Coolant: Water/glycol or dielectric fluid
Pro: Best thermal uniformity, scalable
Con: Leak risk, pump maintenance, cost
BMS integration: Coolant flow rate controlled by BMS via CAN to thermal management ECU
⚠
BMS Must Control the Cooling System — Not Just Monitor

A common design mistake is to treat the BMS and the thermal management system as separate subsystems with separate controllers. This creates a race condition: the BMS detects overtemperature and cuts off the load, but the cooling system is still running at full speed and may cool the pack below its charge temperature limit. The BMS must send cooling commands (fan speed setpoint, coolant valve position) via CAN or a dedicated thermal control interface. The BMS is the single authoritative source of thermal state for the entire system.

Temperature Gradients — The Hidden Threat to Pack Longevity

Even if the average pack temperature is within the optimal range, a large temperature gradient between cells is damaging. Cells at different temperatures age at different rates — a 10°C gradient means the hot cells age 2× faster than the cool cells, causing progressive capacity imbalance even in a perfectly cell-balanced pack.

🌡 Pack Temperature Gradient — Acceptable vs Problematic

Left: well-cooled pack with <5°C gradient — cells age uniformly. Right: poor thermal design with 22°C gradient — red cells age 4× faster, creating increasing voltage imbalance that balancing circuits cannot fully compensate. Target: ΔT_pack < 5°C across all cells.

Pack Temperature Uniformity Target ΔT_pack = T_max − T_min across all cell temperature sensors Target: ΔT_pack < 5°C during normal operation Warning threshold: ΔT_pack > 8°C → reduce load, increase cooling Alarm threshold: ΔT_pack > 12°C → investigate thermal path failure BMS firmware computes ΔT_pack every measurement cycle and logs it as a health indicator. A gradual increase in ΔT_pack over months (at the same load profile) indicates a deteriorating thermal path — gap filler dry-out, blocked vent, or failing fan — before any cell temperature exceeds the protection threshold.
NTC Placement Strategy — Where to Measure and Why

The accuracy of the BMS's thermal model depends entirely on where the temperature sensors are placed. A single NTC on the PCB tells you almost nothing about cell temperatures in a 16S pack.

Critical
Cell Midpoint Surface
Mounted flush against the large face of each cell, at mid-height. Captures the hottest surface point. Minimum 1 per every 2 cells.
Important
Positive Tab Junction
At or near the aluminium busbar-to-tab weld. Tab area carries full pack current — highest I²R heating site. Must not exceed 80°C.
Important
Pack Inlet Air / Coolant
Measures incoming thermal environment. Used to compute delta-T available for cooling and to adjust fan/pump speed feed-forward.
Important
Pack Outlet Air / Coolant
Measures heat rejection effectiveness. T_outlet − T_inlet × flow rate = actual heat removal rate. Used to verify cooling system performance.
Recommended
BMS PCB Ambient
On-board reference for BMS IC derating. AFE ICs have operating limits (typically −40°C to +85°C). PCB temperature correlates with enclosure interior.
Optional
Cell Bottom Centre
Best position to detect cell internal hot spots. Core-to-surface lag ≈ 30–120s at 1C. Bottom sensors respond faster than side sensors for prismatic cells.
⚠
Surface Temperature Underestimates Core Temperature

At 1C discharge, the cell core is typically 5–15°C hotter than its surface. An NTC on the cell surface reading 50°C may mean the cell core is at 60–65°C — approaching the SEI decomposition zone. The BMS must apply a thermal lag correction that is a function of current magnitude and cell thermal resistance:

T_core_est = T_surface + k_lag × I² × R_thermal_cell

Where k_lag is an empirically determined correction factor (measured during cell characterisation using a thermocouple inserted into a test cell core vs surface NTC). Typical values: k_lag ≈ 0.08 K/A² for 280Ah prismatic LFP.
