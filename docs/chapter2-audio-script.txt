SECTION 2.1 — HOW A BATTERY CELL ACTUALLY WORKS
============================================================

Before we look at lithium iron phosphate specifically, we need to understand
what every rechargeable battery has in common.

A cell is a device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy —
and back again. That "and back again" part is what makes it rechargeable.

Every cell has three parts.

The first part is the negative electrode — called the anode during discharge.
In our LFP cell, this is graphite — the same material found in pencils.

The second part is the positive electrode — called the cathode during discharge.
In our LFP cell, this is lithium iron phosphate — a compound of lithium, iron,
phosphorus, and oxygen.

The third part is the electrolyte — a liquid that sits between the two electrodes.
It is not a conductor of electrons, but it is a conductor of lithium ions
— charged lithium atoms.

Here is what happens when the cell discharges — when it is powering a load.

Lithium atoms inside the graphite anode give up an electron each. Those electrons
travel through the external circuit — through your inverter, your load, your wires.
Meanwhile, the lithium ions — now positively charged because they lost an electron —
travel through the electrolyte to the cathode.

At the cathode, the iron atoms in the lithium iron phosphate absorb those electrons
coming from the external circuit. The lithium ions arriving from the electrolyte
slot into the cathode crystal structure. Energy has been converted from chemical form
to electrical form.

Charging reverses every step. External voltage pushes electrons back through the
circuit in the opposite direction. Lithium ions migrate back to the anode.
The chemical state is restored. Energy is stored again.

This back-and-forth migration of lithium ions is called intercalation.
The lithium is not reacting in a destructive sense — it is not being consumed.
It is sliding into and out of crystal structures, like poker chips being picked up
from one stack and placed on another. That is why lithium-ion cells can be recharged
thousands of times without the fundamental chemistry breaking down.


============================================================
SECTION 2.2 — THE LFP CATHODE REACTION
============================================================

Now let's look specifically at what happens inside the lithium iron phosphate cathode.

The LFP cathode is made of tiny particles — each one a crystal of iron phosphate.
When the cell is fully charged, those crystals hold lithium atoms in their structure.
The technical name for the fully lithiated form is lithium iron phosphate — LiFePO4.

When the cell discharges, lithium leaves the cathode crystal. The crystal that remains
— after the lithium is gone — is simply iron phosphate. Written as FePO4.
Same crystal structure. No lithium inside.

Here is the most important thing about this reaction.

There is no in-between state. A given crystal particle is either in the lithiated form
— full of lithium — or in the unlithiated form — empty of lithium. There is no state
where a particle is, say, half full of lithium.

Think of it like a light switch. It is either on or off. Not dimmed.

This "either full or empty" behaviour is called a two-phase reaction. And it has a
profound consequence for everything the BMS has to do.

When a two-phase reaction is happening — when some particles are full and others are
empty and the conversion is in progress — the voltage of the cell stays almost perfectly
flat. It barely moves. The voltage only changes noticeably when almost all particles
have converted, either to the full state or the empty state.

This flat voltage region is called the plateau. For LFP, it sits at roughly
three point three to three point four volts per cell. And it covers most of the
cell's useful charge range — from about twenty percent full to about ninety percent full.

That flat plateau is both a gift and a curse, as we will see in a moment.

The voltage windows the BMS must enforce are:
Upper limit: three point six five volts per cell. Above this, the chemistry breaks down.
Lower limit: two point five volts per cell. Below this, copper dissolution begins.
These are absolute limits. The BMS must never allow any cell to cross them.


============================================================
SECTION 2.3 — CELL FORMATS: CYLINDRICAL, PRISMATIC, POUCH
============================================================

Before we go deeper into the chemistry, let's briefly cover cell formats —
because the format affects how the BMS interacts with the cells physically.

There are three main formats for lithium-ion cells.

CYLINDRICAL CELLS

The cylindrical cell wraps the anode, cathode, and separator into a tight spiral
— like a jellyroll — and seals it inside a metal can. The most famous sizes are
the eighteen-six-fifty, which is eighteen millimetres wide and sixty-five millimetres
tall, and the newer forty-six-eighty, which is forty-six millimetres wide and
eighty millimetres tall.

Cylindrical cells are mechanically robust. The round shape resists deformation under
internal pressure. Their weakness: they pack together with air gaps between them,
which wastes volume. And they generate heat in the centre of the spiral, which is
hard to conduct away.

For LFP home energy storage, cylindrical cells are less common than prismatic.

PRISMATIC CELLS

Prismatic cells stack flat sheets of anode, cathode, and separator inside a rigid
aluminium or steel case. No wasted space between cells. Flat surfaces press together
efficiently. Easy to stack into a module.

The two-eighty amp-hour prismatic LFP cell — the one used in our reference design —
is the dominant format for home energy storage. It is roughly one hundred and
seventy-five millimetres tall, seventy-one millimetres wide, and twenty-seven
millimetres thick. One cell weighs about five and a half kilograms.

The thermal concern with prismatic cells: heat is generated uniformly across the
flat surfaces, but the centre of a large cell stack is still harder to cool
than the edges. The BMS must monitor temperature in multiple locations,
not just at the surface.

POUCH CELLS

Pouch cells are flat, flexible cells sealed in a laminated aluminium-plastic bag.
No rigid case. Maximum energy density — nothing wasted on a heavy metal housing.

The downside: they swell as they age and cycle. The BMS enclosure must allow for
this swelling, and the BMS must monitor swelling-induced pressure as a health
indicator. Pouch cells are common in electric vehicles but less common in
stationary energy storage.


============================================================
SECTION 2.4 — THE OCV CURVE AND THE BMS'S HARDEST PROBLEM
============================================================

Now we arrive at the single most important concept in this entire chapter.
The open-circuit voltage curve. And why it is the root cause of most of the
complexity in BMS software.

WHAT IS OPEN-CIRCUIT VOLTAGE?

Open-circuit voltage — OCV for short — is the voltage you measure when no current
is flowing and the cell has been resting long enough for everything inside to
reach equilibrium. It is the "true" resting voltage of the cell.

For lead-acid batteries, OCV is very useful. At twelve point six volts, the battery
is fully charged. At twelve point zero volts, it is roughly half full. At eleven
point eight volts, it is nearly empty. You can look at the voltage and know where
you are.

For LFP, this does not work. At all.

Here is why. Remember the two-phase reaction we just described — where crystals are
either full or empty, with nothing in between? During that conversion — which covers
the twenty to ninety percent range of charge — the voltage of the cell barely moves.
The entire plateau spans about fifty millivolts.

Fifty millivolts. Across seventy percent of the charge range.

A typical voltage measurement has noise of one to two millivolts. The OCV change
across ten percent of the charge range in the plateau is roughly five millivolts.
That is barely above the noise floor. You are trying to distinguish five millivolts
of signal buried in two millivolts of noise — and draw conclusions about whether
the pack has enough energy to run for eight more hours or two more hours.

It cannot be done reliably.

This is not a measurement problem. It is not a hardware problem. It is a
fundamental information problem. The information is not in the voltage.
No amount of better sensors or smarter software can extract what is not there.

THE IR DROP MAKES IT EVEN HARDER

Even if the cell is at rest and you measure carefully — there is another complication
when current is flowing.

The measured terminal voltage is not the same as OCV.
It is OCV minus the voltage drop caused by current flowing through the cell's
internal resistance.

At one hundred amps discharge from our two-eighty amp-hour cell, with an internal
resistance of about zero point five milliohms, that voltage drop is fifty millivolts.
That is the same magnitude as the entire useful OCV variation across the plateau.

The voltage you read during discharge tells you almost nothing about how full the
cell is. It tells you mostly about how much current is flowing and what the
cell's resistance is.

HYSTERESIS — THE CHARGE AND DISCHARGE CURVES ARE NOT THE SAME

One more complication. The OCV curve for LFP is not a single line.
It is actually two slightly different curves — one for when the cell was last
charging, and one for when it was last discharging.

In the plateau region, these two curves are separated by about ten to fifteen
millivolts. This hysteresis happens because the crystal transition is slightly
asymmetric — it takes a tiny bit more energy to push lithium in one direction
than the other.

For the BMS, this means you need to know the last current direction before
you can look up the OCV table. If you use the wrong table, you add another
ten to fifteen millivolts of error to your already very uncertain SOC estimate.

TEMPERATURE MAKES IT WORSE STILL

At cold temperatures, the OCV is slightly lower and the internal resistance is
much higher. At minus twenty degrees Celsius, the resistance of an LFP cell is
three to four times higher than at twenty-five degrees. At one hundred amps,
that means the terminal voltage during discharge is one hundred and fifty millivolts
lower than at room temperature. Enough to falsely trigger the low-voltage alarm
before the cell is actually empty.

The BMS must compensate for all of these effects simultaneously.

This is why simple voltage-based battery monitoring — which works fine for lead-acid —
completely fails for LFP. And this is why the BMS needs algorithms.


============================================================
SECTION 2.5 — CAPACITY, C-RATE, AND COULOMB COUNTING
============================================================

Now that we understand the problem — voltage tells us almost nothing about LFP
charge level — let's look at the first and most important solution: counting the
charge that flows in and out of the pack.

CAPACITY — WHAT IT MEANS

Capacity is measured in ampere-hours. Our reference cell is rated at two-eighty
ampere-hours. That means it can deliver one ampere for two-hundred-and-eighty hours,
or two-hundred-and-eighty amperes for one hour, or anything in between where the
current multiplied by the time equals two-eighty.

Physically, that two-eighty amp-hour figure represents the total amount of lithium
that can shuttle between anode and cathode. The exact number works out to about
ten and a half moles of lithium atoms — roughly seventy-two and a half grams.
Every charge cycle, those seventy-two and a half grams of lithium travel from one
electrode to the other and back. Over five thousand cycles, each lithium atom
makes that journey five thousand times.

ENERGY — CAPACITY TIMES VOLTAGE

Energy, measured in watt-hours, is capacity multiplied by the average voltage.
Our cell at two-eighty amp-hours and three point two volts average: that is
eight-hundred-and-ninety-six watt-hours per cell. Multiply by sixteen cells in
our pack: fourteen-point-three kilowatt-hours total.

C-RATE — NORMALISING CURRENT

The C-rate is a way to describe current relative to the cell's capacity, so that
comparisons between cells of different sizes make sense.

One C for our cell means two-eighty amps — the current that would fully discharge
it in exactly one hour. Half C means one-hundred-and-forty amps. Two C means
five-hundred-and-sixty amps. Point two C means fifty-six amps.

Higher C-rate means more current, more heat, and faster aging. The BMS uses
C-rate specifications from the cell datasheet to set current protection limits.

COULOMB COUNTING — THE CORE ALGORITHM

Coulomb counting — also called current integration — is the primary method the BMS
uses to track state of charge.

The idea is simple. You know how full the pack was at some starting point.
You measure how much current flows in and out over time. You add charge received
and subtract charge delivered. The running total tells you how full the pack is now.

The BMS does this calculation every two-hundred-and-fifty milliseconds or so.
Every sample, it reads the current sensor, works out how many amp-hours flowed in
that slice of time, and adds it to a running total. Over hours of use, millions of
these tiny additions build up the state-of-charge estimate shown on the display.

THE DRIFT PROBLEM

Here is the fundamental weakness of coulomb counting.

Every tiny measurement error accumulates. If the current sensor reads even a
tenth of a percent high — all day, every day — that error gets added to the total
every two-hundred-and-fifty milliseconds. By the end of the day, the accumulated
error can be two to five percent of the pack's capacity.

For a fourteen-point-three kilowatt-hour pack, five percent error is seven hundred
watt-hours of unknown energy. That is the difference between a comfortable margin
and an unexpected shutdown.

The BMS cannot correct this drift by itself. It needs external reference points.
And there are two main ones. First: when charging is complete, the BMS knows the
pack is at one hundred percent, and it resets the counter. Second: when the pack
has been idle for at least thirty minutes, the voltage settles to a meaningful
resting level that can be used — imperfectly — to correct the count.

This combination of coulomb counting plus periodic correction is the foundation
of all real-world LFP battery management.

THE PEUKERT EFFECT — CAPACITY DROPS AT HIGH CURRENT

One more subtlety. When a cell discharges at a high rate, the apparent capacity
is slightly less than at a slow rate.

Here is the reason. Think of each electrode particle as a sponge. At low current,
lithium has time to soak all the way through. At high current, only the outer
layer gets used. The inside is still full, but the surface voltage drops and the
BMS sees a low-voltage alarm — cutting off discharge before the interior lithium
is accessible.

For LFP, this effect is small — about three percent at one C and seven percent
at two C. But it means that at high current, the coulomb counter will show a few
percent remaining when the alarm fires. This is expected behaviour, not a bug.
Advanced BMS firmware adjusts the assumed capacity based on current.

SECTION 2.6 — SOC ESTIMATION: FIVE TECHNIQUES
============================================================

State of charge estimation is the hardest job the BMS has to do.
Let's go through the five main techniques — what each one does, how accurate it is,
and when you would use it.

TECHNIQUE ONE — OCV LOOKUP TABLE

The simplest approach: wait for the cell to rest, measure its voltage, look it up
in a pre-built table, and read off the corresponding charge level.

For LFP, this works at the extremes. Above three point six volts, the cell is
definitely more than ninety percent full. Below three point two volts, it is
definitely less than fifteen percent full. At the extremes the voltage slope is steep
enough to give a meaningful reading.

In the middle — the entire plateau from twenty to eighty percent — this method gives
roughly plus or minus fifteen to twenty-five percent accuracy. That is not useful
for anything except a rough starting guess.

It also requires at least thirty minutes of rest time. You cannot do it under load.

Best use: getting an initial estimate at startup, before the coulomb counter takes over.

TECHNIQUE TWO — COULOMB COUNTING

Already covered in the previous section. This is the primary tracking method
during active operation.

Accuracy when freshly reset: plus or minus one to two percent.
Accuracy after twenty-four hours without a reset: can drift to plus or minus five to ten percent.
Solution: reset against a known reference whenever the opportunity arises.

Best use: the main workhorse algorithm. Runs constantly.

TECHNIQUE THREE — EQUIVALENT CIRCUIT MODEL

The ECM approach replaces the cell with a simple electrical model.
The model has three parts.

First, an OCV source — a voltage that depends on state of charge.
Second, a resistor called R-zero — representing the instant voltage drop when current flows.
Third, a resistor and capacitor in parallel — representing the slow voltage recovery
that happens after current changes. This slow recovery takes thirty to three hundred seconds
for LFP.

Every sample, the model predicts what the terminal voltage should be given the current SOC
estimate. If the measured voltage differs from the prediction, the BMS nudges the SOC
estimate to reduce the gap.

This is better than pure coulomb counting because voltage is used as a reality check.
But for LFP, the flat plateau means the voltage carries almost no information in the
middle range — so the correction is weak there, and the model still leans heavily on
current counting.

Best use: mid-tier BMS designs where some improvement over pure coulomb counting is wanted
without the complexity of the next method.

TECHNIQUE FOUR — EXTENDED KALMAN FILTER

The Extended Kalman Filter — EKF — is the gold standard. It combines coulomb counting
and ECM-based voltage prediction using a mathematical framework that automatically
weights each source by how trustworthy it is at any given moment.

The analogy: imagine tracking the position of a car using two methods simultaneously.
One method uses an odometer — it measures distance but accumulates errors over time.
The other uses GPS — it gives absolute position but with noise.
The Kalman filter is the mathematically optimal way to combine both signals.

In the battery context, the odometer is coulomb counting. The GPS is the measured
voltage compared to what the ECM model predicts.

For LFP in the plateau, the GPS signal is almost silent — the voltage barely changes
and carries almost no corrective information. So the EKF falls back to mostly
counting current, just like a simple coulomb counter, in that range.

At the edges — below twenty percent and above ninety percent — where the OCV curve
steepens, the voltage becomes more informative and the EKF's advantage becomes clear.
It pulls the estimate back toward reality faster.

Despite the sophisticated mathematics, the EKF is very cheap to run on a modern
microcontroller. For our sixteen-cell pack, it uses less than one percent of processor
time on a standard STM32 chip.

Best use: high-accuracy BMS designs, safety-critical energy storage systems.

TECHNIQUE FIVE — HYBRID LOOKUP TABLE

This is what most real commercial products actually use.

Coulomb counting runs continuously as the main tracking method.
Whenever the pack has been at rest for long enough, the OCV is measured and used
to correct the coulomb count.
When charging finishes — when the current has tapered to a trickle and all cells
have reached the upper voltage limit — the BMS sets the state of charge to one hundred
percent with high confidence.
The assumed capacity is updated periodically from full discharge measurements
to track the slow capacity loss of aging cells.

This combination gives plus or minus three to seven percent accuracy.
It uses very little processor time. It is reliable, predictable, and easy to
verify in testing.

It is the engineering sweet spot for home energy storage and UPS applications.

SECTION 2.7 — SOH, AGING, AND DEGRADATION
============================================================

A new LFP cell rated at two-eighty amp-hours will not deliver two-eighty amp-hours
forever. Slow chemical processes eat away at its capacity whether the cell is in use
or sitting idle. Understanding these processes lets the BMS track the battery's
health, predict when it will need replacing, and operate it in ways that slow the decline.

STATE OF HEALTH — WHAT IT MEANS

State of Health — SOH — is the ratio of the cell's current capacity to its original
rated capacity, expressed as a percentage.

A cell at one hundred percent SOH delivers exactly its rated capacity.
A cell at eighty percent SOH delivers eighty percent — for our two-eighty amp-hour cell,
that is two-hundred-and-twenty-four amp-hours.

The industry standard end-of-life threshold is eighty percent SOH.
Below eighty percent, the cell cannot reliably meet runtime guarantees.
In practice, many ESS installations replace cells at seventy to seventy-five percent
SOH to maintain comfortable safety margins.

HOW THE BMS MEASURES SOH

The most accurate method is a Reference Performance Test.
Charge the pack fully to one hundred percent.
Rest for thirty minutes.
Discharge at a slow, steady rate — point two C — down to the cutoff voltage,
while precisely counting every amp-hour delivered.
The total amp-hours measured is the current actual capacity.
Divide by the rated capacity. That is the SOH.

In practice, the BMS runs this test automatically every thirty to one hundred cycles,
typically overnight when the pack is not needed.

Between tests, the BMS keeps a rolling average of the amp-hours delivered on each
full cycle. This running average drifts slowly downward as the cell ages.

THE SEI LAYER — THE MAIN AGING MECHANISM

The most important aging process in lithium-ion cells is the formation and slow
growth of the Solid Electrolyte Interphase — the SEI layer.

Here is what it is.

When a lithium-ion cell is first charged, the liquid electrolyte inside comes into
contact with the graphite anode at a very low voltage. At that voltage, the electrolyte
is chemically unstable — it breaks down and leaves a thin solid residue on the
graphite surface. This layer of residue is the SEI.

It forms mostly during the very first charge — this is called the formation step
at the battery factory. Once it is there, it acts as a shield.

The SEI is both essential and costly.

On the positive side: it protects the anode from the electrolyte. It lets lithium
ions through while blocking electrons. If it cracks, it self-heals.

On the negative side: it permanently locks up lithium. Every atom consumed in
SEI formation is gone forever — it cannot carry charge anymore. It adds resistance
as it thickens. And it never truly stops growing — it keeps slowly building up
throughout the cell's entire life.

Every ten degrees Celsius of temperature increase roughly doubles the speed of
SEI growth. That is why temperature is the most important factor in battery aging.
A pack stored at forty degrees runs out of life two to three times faster than one
stored at twenty-five degrees, even if they are cycled identically.

CALENDAR AGING — DEGRADATION WITHOUT USE

A cell stored at full charge is aging even when nothing is drawing from it.

Three things cause calendar aging.

First: ongoing SEI growth. This never stops, even at rest.

Second: electrolyte breakdown at high charge levels. Keeping an LFP cell at full charge
— three point six five volts per cell — speeds up chemical breakdown at the cathode surface.
Storing at fifty percent charge is significantly better for longevity.

Third: if the cell has previously suffered from lithium plating — which we will cover
in a moment — the leftover metallic lithium keeps reacting with the electrolyte
during storage, causing additional ongoing damage.

The practical implication for UPS systems: a battery that sits fully charged for
months waiting for a power outage suffers more from calendar aging than from
the actual discharge cycles. The BMS should float at ninety to ninety-five percent
state of charge — about three point four to three point four-five volts per cell —
rather than at full charge. This one setting can extend calendar life by twenty to forty percent.

LITHIUM PLATING — THE FAST-AGING FAILURE MODE

Lithium plating happens when lithium ions arrive at the anode faster than the
graphite can absorb them.

Instead of sliding into the crystal lattice, the lithium is forced to deposit
as solid metal on the surface.

This causes three types of damage.

First: the deposited lithium reacts with the electrolyte and becomes permanently dead.
It can never carry charge again. Capacity drops immediately and irreversibly.

Second: the deposits can grow into sharp spikes called dendrites. These can punch
through the separator inside the cell and cause an internal short circuit.
Internal short circuits are a root cause of real-world lithium battery fires.

Third: some plated lithium dissolves back into the electrolyte during rest,
recovering a little capacity — but it leaves SEI residue and dead patches behind
each time. Each cycle of plating and partial recovery leaves the cell slightly worse.

When does lithium plating happen?

At cold temperatures: below zero degrees Celsius, lithium moves more slowly
through the graphite. Even a gentle charge rate of point one C can cause plating
below minus ten degrees for some cell formulations.

Charging too fast: at two C or more, lithium arrives at the anode faster than it
can be absorbed, forcing it to plate.

Near a full cell: as the anode fills up, the remaining empty spots are deep inside
the particles and hard to reach. The effective absorption rate slows even if the
current has not changed.

In aged cells: old SEI patches block parts of the anode surface, funnelling current
into a smaller area — effectively raising the local current density.

The BMS must prohibit charging at or below minus twenty degrees Celsius.
Between zero and five degrees, the charge rate must be limited to point two C or less.
These are hard rules, not guidelines. The BMS enforces them automatically.

LFP VERSUS NMC — WHY LFP LASTS LONGER

LFP cells typically reach eighty percent SOH at three thousand to five thousand cycles.
NMC cells reach the same threshold at eight hundred to fifteen hundred cycles.
That is three to four times fewer cycles.

Three structural reasons explain the difference.

LFP expands and contracts less with each cycle — about six point eight percent
volume change versus ten to fifteen percent for NMC. Less mechanical stress means
the SEI cracks less and particles crumble more slowly.

LFP does not contain nickel or manganese. In NMC cells, those metals slowly dissolve
into the electrolyte and migrate to the anode, where they speed up SEI growth.
This contamination pathway does not exist in LFP.

LFP charges to three point six five volts per cell versus NMC's four point two volts.
SEI grows faster at higher voltages. Even that half-volt difference translates
to substantially faster aging in NMC.

SECTION 2.8 — TEMPERATURE EFFECTS ON CELL PERFORMANCE
============================================================

Temperature is the single biggest outside factor affecting how an LFP cell behaves.
It changes how much capacity the cell can deliver, how fast it charges safely,
how long it lasts, and how much the voltage sags under load — all at the same time.

WHY TEMPERATURE MATTERS SO MUCH

The chemical reactions inside a battery all go faster as temperature rises.
This is a universal law of chemistry. The practical rule: every ten degrees Celsius
increase roughly doubles the speed of chemical reactions.

That applies equally to useful reactions — faster ion movement, better charge kinetics —
and to harmful ones — faster SEI growth, faster electrolyte breakdown.
You cannot have the benefit without accepting the cost.
Managing temperature is always a trade-off.

LOW TEMPERATURE — THE LITHIUM PLATING CLIFF

Below zero degrees Celsius, the graphite anode struggles to absorb lithium ions
during charging. The ions arrive faster than the graphite can take them in.
The result is lithium plating, which we covered in the previous section.

The hard limits the BMS must enforce:

No charging at all at minus twenty degrees Celsius or colder.
Maximum charge rate of point two C between zero and five degrees Celsius.
These limits override any external charging command.

Cold temperature also raises internal resistance dramatically. At minus twenty degrees,
the resistance is three to four times higher than at twenty-five degrees.
At one hundred amps, this raises the voltage drop by one hundred and fifty millivolts.
Enough to falsely trigger the undervoltage alarm before the cell is actually empty.

The BMS must compensate: when temperature is low, the BMS widens the effective
voltage window slightly — or, equivalently, does not trigger the low-voltage alarm
as aggressively — to avoid shutting down a cold pack that still has useful charge.

HIGH TEMPERATURE — ACCELERATED AGING AND SAFETY LIMITS

At the other extreme:

Every ten degrees above twenty-five doubles the SEI growth rate. A pack running
at forty-five degrees Celsius reaches eighty percent SOH in roughly one thousand cycles
instead of three thousand or more. That is three times shorter life from a
twenty-degree temperature difference.

Above sixty degrees Celsius, the electrolyte begins to break down, producing gas.
Prismatic cells have pressure relief vents for this. The BMS should treat a sudden
drop in internal resistance as an early warning of thermal stress — gas forming
between the electrodes reduces their contact — often before the temperature sensor
even responds.

The BMS's hard cutoff for high temperature is typically sixty degrees Celsius for
the cell surface. Above this, both charge and discharge are prohibited until the
pack cools down.

CAPACITY CHANGES WITH TEMPERATURE

The rated capacity — two-eighty amp-hours — is measured at twenty-five degrees.
In the real world, the cell can deliver more or less depending on temperature.

At minus twenty degrees: only about sixty percent of rated capacity is accessible.
At minus ten degrees: about seventy-eight percent.
At zero degrees: about eighty-eight percent.
At twenty-five degrees: one hundred percent — the reference.
At forty-five degrees: about ninety-seven percent — barely different.

The BMS must adjust its SOC calculation for temperature. If it does not,
a cold pack at zero degrees might show eighty-eight percent on the display,
but the usable energy relative to the original rating is actually eighty-eight
times eighty-eight — which is about seventy-seven percent. That eleven-percent gap
is why loads shut down earlier than expected on cold mornings.

WHERE TO PUT THE TEMPERATURE SENSORS

A single temperature sensor on the BMS circuit board is not enough.
The circuit board can be ten to fifteen degrees cooler than the inside of the
cell core during a high-current discharge.

Best practice is four sensor positions:
At the middle of the cell surface — catches the main heat buildup.
At the terminal tabs — catches heating at the connection points.
At the pack air inlet and outlet — shows whether cooling is working.
On the BMS board itself — reference for the BMS chip's own temperature limits.

The BMS uses the highest reading across all sensors for safety cutoffs,
and the average reading for SOC correction.

SECTION 2.9 — WORKED EXAMPLES: REAL NUMBERS
============================================================

Let's make everything concrete with three calculations using our actual pack.

EXAMPLE ONE — UPS RUNTIME PREDICTION

Setup: sixteen-cell LFP pack, two-eighty amp-hour cells, SOH is ninety-two percent,
temperature is fifteen degrees Celsius, starting charge is ninety-five percent.
Load: eight hundred watts for the first ten minutes, then four hundred watts until empty.

Step one — how much energy is actually available?

The cell capacity adjusted for health and temperature is:
two-eighty times zero-point-nine-two times zero-point-nine-four.
That gives two-hundred-and-forty-two amp-hours.
Multiply by fifty-one-point-two volts: twelve-thousand-three-hundred-and-ninety-five watt-hours.
At ninety-five percent starting charge: eleven-thousand-seven-hundred-and-seventy-five watt-hours available.

Step two — first ten minutes at eight hundred watts.

Energy used: eight hundred times ten-sixtieths of an hour: one-hundred-and-thirty-three watt-hours.
State of charge drops from ninety-five percent to ninety-three-point-nine percent.

Step three — remaining runtime at four hundred watts.

Energy remaining: eleven-thousand-seven-hundred-and-seventy-five minus one-hundred-and-thirty-three.
That is eleven-thousand-six-hundred-and-forty-two watt-hours.
Divided by four hundred watts: twenty-nine-point-one hours.

Total predicted runtime: just over twenty-nine hours.

Why can we use a flat fifty-one-point-two volts for this calculation?
Because of LFP's flat OCV curve. The voltage barely changes during discharge,
so the error from using a fixed voltage is less than three percent.
With NMC, the voltage drops noticeably and you would need to account for it.
This is one of LFP's underappreciated practical advantages.

EXAMPLE TWO — SOC DRIFT OVER TWENTY-FOUR HOURS

Setup: same pack, sitting idle. Current sensor has a point-zero-five percent
gain error and a half-milliamp offset. Natural self-discharge is fifty milliamps.

After twenty-four hours at idle:
Gain error adds: roughly zero-point-zero-zero-zero-two percent drift. Negligible.
Offset error adds: zero-point-zero-zero-four percent. Also negligible.
Unmodelled self-discharge: fifty milliamps times twenty-four hours is one-thousand-two-hundred
milliamp-hours. Divided by the cell capacity: zero-point-four-three percent per day.

So the dominant drift is the natural self-discharge that the BMS does not know about —
not the sensor errors. Over thirty days idle: about thirteen percent accumulated drift.

The fix: the BMS subtracts a small self-discharge current from the coulomb counter
even when no external current flows. This background subtraction keeps the SOC
reading accurate during long idle periods — essential for UPS systems
that may go months without a discharge event.

EXAMPLE THREE — PASSIVE BALANCING TIME

Setup: after a full charge, one cell sits at three-point-six-five volts,
while the others are at three-point-six-two. The BMS uses ten-ohm balancing resistors.

Balancing current: the thirty-millivolt gap divided by ten ohms: three milliamps.
Power dissipated: ninety microwatts per cell. Almost nothing.

Charge to remove: the LFP plateau has such a flat voltage curve that
a thirty-millivolt gap corresponds to about one-hundred-and-sixty-eight milliamp-hours
of imbalance.

Time to balance: one-hundred-and-sixty-eight milliamp-hours divided by three milliamps:
fifty-six hours.

This reveals why passive balancing for LFP must happen continuously over many charging
cycles — not just in a single session. If the imbalance were larger, say one hundred
millivolts, it could take several days to correct with a ten-ohm resistor.
This is why high-power applications sometimes use active balancing — which transfers
charge from full cells to empty ones rather than just burning it off as heat.

SECTION 2.10 — BMS COMMISSIONING: FIRST POWER ON A REAL PACK
============================================================

At some point the design leaves the computer and becomes real hardware on a bench.
Let's walk through what a production engineer actually does when powering up
a new BMS design for the first time.

PHASE ONE — CHECKS BEFORE CONNECTING THE BATTERY

A sixteen-cell LFP pack at full charge presents fifty-eight-point-four volts.
That is a live energy source capable of destroying circuit board traces
and starting fires if there is a wiring error.

Before connecting the battery, the engineer checks:

All cell-sense wire connections match the schematic.
Thermistor resistance at room temperature matches the datasheet.
Current sensor is oriented correctly — positive current reads positive.
Balancing resistor values are correct.
Protection FET leakage is within specification.
The logic power rails — five volts and three-point-three volts — regulate correctly
from a bench supply before any battery is connected.

PHASE TWO — INITIAL SOC ESTIMATE

With cells connected and no current flowing for at least thirty minutes,
the BMS reads each cell's resting voltage and looks it up in the OCV table.

The BMS checks that the spread between the highest and lowest cell voltage
is less than fifty millivolts. A larger spread suggests cells were not matched
or one cell has a problem.

The starting SOC is set to the average across all cells.
Any cell more than five percent away from the average is flagged for investigation.

Why thirty minutes? After any recent charge or discharge, the voltage includes a
temporary component from diffusion inside the particles. Thirty minutes is enough
for that to decay and the voltage to settle to its true resting level.

PHASE THREE — PROTECTION THRESHOLD TESTING

Every protection limit must be tested by deliberately triggering it.

Overvoltage: charge slowly until one cell reaches the overvoltage threshold.
Confirm the charge FET opens and the alarm fires within one hundred milliseconds.

Undervoltage: discharge slowly until the lowest cell reaches the cutoff.
Confirm the discharge FET opens within one hundred milliseconds.

Overcurrent: ramp the load current to one-point-two times the overcurrent threshold.
Confirm both FETs open within five milliseconds.

Short circuit: apply a low-resistance load through a relay and confirm the BMS
responds within five hundred microseconds. This test requires a fire-safe enclosure
and full protective equipment — it involves deliberately creating a short circuit
on a live pack.

Overtemperature: use a heat source on the thermistor and confirm the BMS
reduces the charge rate at forty-five degrees and cuts off completely at sixty degrees.

Undertemperature: cool the thermistor and confirm charging is blocked at zero degrees
and cut off completely at minus twenty degrees.

PHASE FOUR — SOC ACCURACY VERIFICATION

After protection testing, run a full Reference Performance Test.
Charge to one hundred percent, rest thirty minutes, discharge at point-two C
to the cutoff voltage while counting every amp-hour with a calibrated reference meter.

Compare the measured capacity to what the BMS reports.
If the error exceeds two percent, update the nominal capacity figure in firmware.

This test should be repeated every six months during normal operation
as a scheduled health check. When the measured capacity drops below eighty percent
of the original rated capacity, the pack has reached end of life.

SECTION 2.11 — SOH TRENDING AND STATE OF POWER
============================================================

State of Health is not a yes-or-no value — it is a number that slowly decreases
over years of use. The BMS needs to track this decline, predict when replacement
is needed, and communicate to the host system exactly how hard the pack can
be pushed right now.

SOH TRENDING — TWO PHASES OF AGING

LFP capacity fade follows a predictable two-phase pattern.

Phase one covers most of the cell's life — roughly from new to about eighty percent
of its total cycle count. In this phase, capacity declines at a nearly constant rate:
about point zero one to point zero three percent per cycle at twenty-five degrees
and one C. This is predictable. The BMS can project when the pack will reach
eighty percent SOH simply by extending the trend line.

Phase two is the knee — the last portion of the pack's life. The rate of decay
accelerates. The SEI has grown thick. Lithium inventory is running low. Resistance
has risen enough to compound the problem. Once a pack enters phase two, remaining
life prediction becomes less reliable, and a maintenance alert should be triggered.

In practice, the BMS logs the measured capacity after each Reference Performance Test,
alongside a timestamp and cycle count. When this data is uploaded to a cloud platform,
fleet-level analytics can catch problems that affect many packs simultaneously —
a bad batch of cells, an installation that runs too hot, or an application that
cycles the packs too aggressively.

STATE OF POWER — WHAT CAN THE PACK DELIVER RIGHT NOW?

SOC tells you how much energy is stored.
State of Power — SoP — answers a different question: how hard can you push the pack
right now, without violating any safety limit?

This matters enormously for UPS applications. When mains power fails, the inverter
switches to battery in less than ten milliseconds and immediately demands full power.
If the BMS's State of Power figure is one second old — from when the temperature or
SOC was slightly different — the inverter may ask for more than the pack can currently
deliver. The result: a voltage collapse that takes the UPS offline at exactly the
wrong moment.

State of Power is calculated from three things: the current cell voltage, the internal
resistance, and the minimum safe cell voltage. The maximum current is the voltage
headroom divided by the resistance. The maximum power is that current times the
pack voltage.

For a ten-second burst — a common UPS requirement — the calculation uses
a slightly lower effective resistance than the steady-state value, because the
slow diffusion component of the resistance has not had time to fully build up.

The key point: the BMS must recalculate State of Power every one hundred milliseconds
and broadcast it on the communication bus as a high-priority message.
It cannot be updated only once per second like less time-critical values.

RESISTANCE GROWTH — HOW AGING AFFECTS PEAK POWER

As cells age, not only does capacity decrease — internal resistance increases.

After two thousand cycles, the internal resistance of an LFP cell is typically
forty percent higher than when new. That forty percent increase reduces peak
current capability by about thirty percent — even though capacity has only
dropped by roughly twenty percent.

This means a pack at eighty percent SOH can deliver only about seventy percent
of its original peak power. This must be accounted for when sizing a UPS:
always derate peak power for aged cells, not just energy capacity.

SECTION 2.12 — SERIES AND PARALLEL PACKS
============================================================

Real LFP packs are not single cells. They are groups of cells connected together.
How those cells are connected changes everything about how the BMS must manage them.

SERIES STRINGS — VOLTAGE ADDS UP

When cells are connected in series — one after another — their voltages add up
while they all carry the same current.

Sixteen cells in series: sixteen times three-point-two volts equals fifty-one-point-two volts.
At full charge: sixteen times three-point-six-five equals fifty-eight-point-four volts.
At the cutoff: sixteen times two-point-five equals forty volts.

This is the "forty-eight volt nominal" system used in home energy storage.

The most important consequence of series connection: the weakest cell sets the limit.
All cells carry the same current. When the cell with the lowest capacity runs empty,
its voltage hits the cutoff — and the BMS must stop the entire discharge,
even though fifteen other cells still have charge left.

This is why the BMS must measure every cell's voltage individually, all the time.

A common shortcut in cheap BMS designs: measure only the total pack voltage
and divide by the number of cells to estimate the average. This is dangerous.
If cell seven is at two-point-three volts while the others are at three-point-five,
the total pack voltage looks completely normal — about fifty-four-point-five volts.
Meanwhile, cell seven is being driven below its safe limit. Its copper current
collector is dissolving. After a few such events, cell seven fails with an internal
short circuit — which then dumps all the other cells' energy through the fault.
Per-cell voltage monitoring is not a premium feature. It is the minimum safe architecture.

PARALLEL GROUPS — CAPACITY ADDS UP

When cells are connected in parallel — all positive terminals together,
all negative terminals together — their capacities add while the voltage stays the same.

Two two-eighty amp-hour cells in parallel: five-hundred-and-sixty amp-hours total.
Parallel cells naturally balance themselves — if one gets slightly higher voltage,
current flows automatically to equalise them. No active balancing is needed
within a parallel group.

The risk: if one parallel cell fails internally — a short circuit — it pulls charge
from all the other cells in the group at potentially destructive current levels.

THERMAL GRADIENTS WITHIN A MODULE

In a sixteen-cell module, not every cell runs at the same temperature.
Cells in the middle of a stack can be five to fifteen degrees hotter than
the ones on the edges.

That temperature difference causes uneven aging. Inner cells develop higher resistance
faster, carry less current, and force the outer cells to work harder.
Over years of cycling, this imbalance compounds.

The BMS should monitor temperature at multiple points inside the module —
not just on the outer surface — and include the temperature gradient in its
health tracking models.

CELL BALANCING — KEEPING CELLS IN STEP

Over time, cells in a series string drift apart. Differences in manufacturing,
slight differences in temperature, and tiny differences in self-discharge rate
all cause one cell to have slightly more or less charge than its neighbours
after each cycle.

If left uncorrected, the weakest cell hits the lower cutoff before the others
are empty, and the strongest cell hits the upper cutoff before the others
are fully charged. The usable capacity of the pack shrinks — not because the
cells have aged, but because they are out of step.

Passive balancing is the most common solution. A small resistor is switched
across each cell that is too full, burning off a little charge as heat
until all cells are at the same level.

The best time to balance is at the end of a charge cycle — when all cells are
approaching their upper voltage limit. The cell that is slightly more full
hits the limit first, and the balancing resistor bleeds off the excess.
The other cells continue charging and eventually catch up.

Fifty-six hours to correct a thirty-millivolt imbalance using a ten-ohm resistor —
as we calculated earlier — shows why balancing must happen over many charging cycles,
not in a single session.

SECTION 2.13 — NMC AND NCA: THE ALTERNATIVES
============================================================

Even if you only work with LFP, you need to understand the two main alternatives:
NMC and NCA. Customers compare them. System integrators may specify them.
And understanding the differences explains why LFP BMS settings cannot simply
be copied to an NMC system.

NMC — NICKEL MANGANESE COBALT

NMC uses a layered crystal structure with three active metals: nickel, manganese,
and cobalt. Each metal plays a different role.

Nickel is the main energy carrier — more nickel means higher energy density.
Manganese is a structural support that holds the crystal together and reduces swelling.
Cobalt improves charge rate capability but is expensive and raises ethical
sourcing concerns.

The industry trend has been to maximise nickel: from equal thirds of each metal
to sixty percent nickel, to eighty percent nickel. Each step increases energy density
but reduces thermal stability. At eighty percent nickel, NMC starts releasing oxygen
at around one hundred and eighty degrees Celsius — only sixty degrees above conditions
possible in an abuse scenario.

What this means for the BMS:

NMC has a much more useful voltage curve. It spans about one-point-two volts
from fully charged to fully discharged, compared to LFP's fifty millivolts in
the plateau. Voltage-based SOC estimation actually works for NMC. This is a
significant BMS simplification.

But NMC requires tighter protection: charge cutoff at four-point-two volts per cell
versus LFP's three-point-six-five. That higher voltage speeds up electrolyte breakdown.
Overcharging NMC even slightly is far more dangerous than with LFP.

NCA — NICKEL COBALT ALUMINIUM

NCA replaces manganese with aluminium. Aluminium is a smaller, more stable atom
that lets the cell push nickel content even higher — up to eighty percent —
without the crystal structure collapsing. The result is the highest energy density
of any major cathode chemistry in production: two hundred to two-eighty watt-hours
per kilogram at cell level. Tesla's original cells and modern large-format cells
use NCA-based formulations.

The trade-offs are severe.

NCA starts releasing oxygen at around one-hundred-and-sixty degrees Celsius —
the lowest onset temperature of any major chemistry. It is very sensitive to moisture.
It has higher self-discharge at warm temperatures. Every protection limit must be
tighter, every temperature threshold lower, every SOC uncertainty smaller.

This is why large NCA packs in electric vehicles use the most sophisticated
SOC algorithms — Extended Kalman Filter at minimum, sometimes more advanced physics-based
models. The consequences of getting SOC wrong with NCA are more serious.

THE COMPARISON IN ONE SENTENCE:

LFP: safest, longest life, lowest energy density, hardest SOC estimation.
NMC: good balance of energy and safety, easier SOC estimation, shorter cycle life.
NCA: highest energy density, highest risk, requires the most sophisticated BMS.

For home energy storage and UPS: LFP is the right choice. Every time.
