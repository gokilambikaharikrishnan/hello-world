--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3.1 — WHERE DOES THE HEAT ACTUALLY COME FROM?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Okay, so first things first. When a lithium battery is charging or
discharging, it produces heat. Why? Where is the heat coming from?

ALEX: There are three separate sources, and understanding each one matters
because they behave very differently.

BLAKE: Let's go through them one at a time.

ALEX: Source one is called Joule heating. And this is by far the biggest source
of heat in normal operation. The formula is incredibly simple: Q equals I squared
times R. That's heat equals current squared times resistance.

BLAKE: And that I-squared is the key part.

ALEX: It's huge. Think about what that means — if you double the current, you
don't double the heat. You quadruple it. Four times as much heat for double
the current.

BLAKE: Because you're squaring the current.

ALEX: Exactly. So if a cell generates, say, one watt of heat at half C-rate,
it generates four watts at one C, and sixteen watts at two C. The heat doesn't
scale linearly — it explodes.

BLAKE: So high charge and discharge rates are not just harder on the battery
chemically — they're thermally dangerous too.

ALEX: Right. And there's a really practical implication here for pack design.
If you have one big cell doing the work versus four smaller cells in parallel
doing the same work — the parallel cells each carry a quarter of the current.
Quarter the current squared means one-sixteenth the heat per cell.

BLAKE: That's a massive difference. So just adding cells in parallel is a
thermal management strategy, not just a capacity strategy.

ALEX: Exactly. Each parallel cell runs cooler because the current is shared.

BLAKE: Now, where does this resistance actually come from inside the cell?
Because it's not one thing, right?

ALEX: There are several layers of resistance stacked up inside every cell.
First, there's the resistance of the electrode materials themselves — the
graphite anode and the iron phosphate cathode. Second, there's the contact
resistance between the electrode and the current collector — the thin copper
or aluminium foil that the electrode is coated onto. Third, there's the
resistance of the electrolyte, the liquid that lithium ions travel through.
And fourth, there's the SEI layer — a thin film that forms on the graphite
surface. We talked about that in Chapter Two.

BLAKE: And all of those add up to the internal resistance number you see on a
cell datasheet.

ALEX: Right. For our reference cell — the two-hundred-and-eighty amp-hour
prismatic LFP cell — the total internal resistance is about half a milli-ohm.
That's zero point five thousandths of an ohm. Tiny.

BLAKE: That sounds tiny, but clearly it generates significant heat.

ALEX: Because the currents are enormous. At one C-rate for a two-eighty amp-hour
cell, you're flowing two-hundred-and-eighty amps. Plug that into the formula —
two-eighty squared times zero-point-zero-zero-zero-five — and you get about
thirty-nine watts of heat from a single cell.

BLAKE: Thirty-nine watts. That's like leaving a dim light bulb running inside
the cell.

ALEX: Continuously. And if you go to two C, that's eighty amps more current —
four times the heat — over a hundred and fifty watts from one cell.

BLAKE: Okay, I feel the urgency now.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ALEX: Source two is electrochemical reaction heat. This one is smaller — maybe
five to fifteen percent of total heat — but it's always present.

BLAKE: Where does it come from?

ALEX: No chemical reaction is perfectly efficient. When lithium moves in and out
of the electrodes, some of the energy gets converted to heat rather than
electrical energy. You can actually measure this by comparing the battery's
terminal voltage — the voltage you measure at the terminals — to the OCV, the
open circuit voltage, which is what the cell would show if it were perfectly
at rest.

BLAKE: And the difference between those two voltages represents...

ALEX: Wasted energy. The gap between terminal voltage and OCV during operation
is energy being dissipated as heat. It's called overpotential.

BLAKE: So even if the cell had zero ohmic resistance, it would still produce
some heat just from the chemistry.

ALEX: Yes. You can't get around it. It's a fundamental property of how
electrochemical reactions work.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ALEX: Source three is the strangest one — entropic heat. And this one is
fascinating because it can go either way.

BLAKE: Wait — it can cool the cell?

ALEX: Yes! Depending on the state of charge, entropy changes as lithium moves
in and out of the crystal. And that entropy change can either release heat or
absorb heat from the surroundings.

BLAKE: Like a reversible heat pump inside the cell.

ALEX: Exactly the right analogy. For LFP specifically, during discharge at low
states of charge, this entropic effect actually absorbs heat — the cell
cools itself slightly. During discharge at high states of charge, it adds
a little heat. The effect is only a few watts, but it's real and measurable.

BLAKE: And some BMS firmware actually accounts for this?

ALEX: Advanced ones do. They include a state-of-charge correction in the
thermal model — because if you don't, your temperature estimates will be
slightly wrong at the extremes.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE THERMAL GRADIENT — SURFACE VS. CORE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: So you've got three sources of heat all happening simultaneously inside
the cell. But here's what I keep thinking about — where is all that heat
actually generated? Is it uniform through the whole cell?

ALEX: No, and this is really important for BMS design. The heat is generated
in the electrode stack — which is right in the centre of the cell. And to
get out of the cell, it has to travel outward through the cell material.
But the electrode stack is a poor thermal conductor.

BLAKE: So heat builds up in the middle.

ALEX: Right. Even if you're cooling the outside of the cell perfectly, the
inside is still hotter. There's always a temperature gap between the core
and the surface during operation. At one C-rate, that gap is typically
five to fifteen degrees Celsius.

BLAKE: Five to fifteen degrees. So if my temperature sensor on the outside
of the cell reads fifty degrees...

ALEX: The core could be at sixty or sixty-five degrees. And that starts to
matter because the dangerous temperature thresholds — where the chemistry
starts to break down — are in the core, not the surface.

BLAKE: And the sensor is on the surface.

ALEX: Always. You can't put a sensor inside a live cell. So the BMS has to
estimate the core temperature using a correction formula — the estimated
core temperature equals the surface temperature plus a correction factor
times the current squared.

BLAKE: Current squared again.

ALEX: Because the core-to-surface gap is driven by Joule heating, which
scales as I squared.

BLAKE: For our reference cell — the two-eighty amp-hour, forty-five
millimetre thick LFP — what does the thermal resistance work out to?

ALEX: The thermal resistance from core to surface is about one-point-one
degrees Celsius per watt. At one C, the cell generates about thirty-nine
watts — giving a core-to-surface gap of about twenty-two degrees when
cooled symmetrically from both faces. That's enormous.

BLAKE: So the surface sensor is reading twenty-two degrees cooler than
the hottest point.

ALEX: Which is why you set your protection thresholds conservatively.
If you set the alarm at fifty-five degrees on the surface sensor, the
actual core is already at seventy-seven degrees when that alarm fires.

BLAKE: This is really making me rethink what "temperature monitoring" even
means.

ALEX: It's more complicated than just sticking a thermistor on and reading
the number. You have to know what you're measuring and what you're
not measuring.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3.2 — THERMAL RUNAWAY: THE MOST DANGEROUS THING THAT CAN HAPPEN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Okay. Section three-point-two. Thermal runaway. I've been dreading
this section in a good way because it's genuinely alarming physics.

ALEX: It is. And I want to start with the definition, because the word
"runaway" is very deliberate.

BLAKE: Let's hear it.

ALEX: Thermal runaway is what happens when a cell produces heat faster than
it can lose heat — and the extra heat accelerates the reactions that produce
even more heat. It's a positive feedback loop. More heat makes things hotter.
Hotter things generate more heat. More heat makes things even hotter still.

BLAKE: And at some point you can't stop it.

ALEX: That's the defining characteristic. Once the loop is established, no
external action can stop it. The cell will consume itself. Prevention is the
only strategy. That is literally the entire reason the BMS exists.

BLAKE: That's a strong statement.

ALEX: It's true though. Every overvoltage protection, every temperature
cutoff, every current limit in the BMS firmware — all of it ultimately exists
to prevent this one event.

BLAKE: So what triggers it? What starts the loop?

ALEX: There are four main triggers. First: overcharging. If you push a cell
above three-point-six-five volts per cell, lithium starts depositing as
metallic lithium rather than inserting into the graphite properly. The cathode
also becomes chemically unstable at that voltage. The BMS over-voltage
protection has to cut the charge before this point.

BLAKE: The second trigger?

ALEX: External heat. A neighbouring cell that's already in runaway can bake
the cells next to it. A fire in the room. A stuck heater element. If any
external heat source pushes the cell above about sixty degrees Celsius for
sustained periods, it can start the cascade.

BLAKE: Third?

ALEX: Internal short circuit. This is the scariest one because there's no
external cause. Tiny metallic spikes — called dendrites — can grow on the
graphite anode over time, especially if the cell has been overcharged or
charged at sub-zero temperatures. If a dendrite grows long enough to punch
through the thin plastic separator and touch the cathode on the other side,
you have a direct metal-to-metal connection between the two electrodes
inside the cell.

BLAKE: And that means...

ALEX: All the stored energy in the cell dumps as heat, internally, in
seconds. No current flowing through the external circuit — just a sudden
internal short that turns two-hundred-and-eighty amp-hours of stored energy
into pure heat right inside the cell. The BMS may see a voltage collapse,
but by then it's too late.

BLAKE: No way to prevent it once the dendrite forms and punctures.

ALEX: No way. Which is why the BMS works so hard to prevent the conditions
that allow dendrites to grow in the first place — no overcharge, no charging
at freezing temperatures, no excessive high-current charge events.

BLAKE: And the fourth trigger?

ALEX: Mechanical damage. Crushing the cell, puncturing it with a nail,
dropping it hard enough to deform the casing — all of these can physically
collapse the electrode layers inside, creating the same kind of internal
short as a dendrite. The BMS might detect a sudden change in internal
resistance as the first warning, but again, by the time the short forms,
the cascade is starting.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE THREE-STAGE CASCADE — UNDERSTANDING EACH MOMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Okay, so the loop starts. What actually happens inside the cell?
Walk me through the physics step by step.

ALEX: Thermal runaway doesn't happen all at once. It unfolds in three
distinct stages, each one hotter than the last, each one triggering the next.
And knowing where you are in the cascade tells you whether you can still
stop it.

BLAKE: Stage zero?

ALEX: Stage zero is just normal operation. Twenty-five to sixty degrees
Celsius. Heat from Joule heating and chemistry is being produced and removed
by the cooling system. The BMS is watching temperature, voltage, and current
continuously. This is the wide-open intervention window — anything going
wrong should be caught and fixed here.

BLAKE: That's the safe zone.

ALEX: Right. And the whole design philosophy is to keep every cell in
Stage Zero all the time.

BLAKE: Stage one.

ALEX: Stage one starts when the cell temperature crosses about ninety to
a hundred-and-twenty degrees Celsius. At that temperature, something
called the SEI layer starts to break apart.

BLAKE: The SEI layer — we covered this in Chapter Two. That's the protective
coating on the graphite anode.

ALEX: Exactly. It forms naturally during the first few charge cycles and
it's actually essential — without it, the electrolyte would just decompose
on the graphite surface. But at around ninety degrees, it starts to
decompose itself. And the decomposition is exothermic — it releases heat.

BLAKE: So the cell is now generating extra heat beyond just Joule heating.

ALEX: Yes. And that extra heat raises the temperature, which accelerates
the decomposition, which produces more heat. The loop has started. CO and
CO₂ gas are produced — which raises pressure inside the cell.

BLAKE: Can the BMS detect this?

ALEX: Yes, and this is crucial — Stage One is the last moment where BMS
intervention can stop the cascade. The temperature is rising at maybe
half a degree to two degrees per minute at Stage One onset. That's slow
enough to detect and fast enough to act on. The BMS can cut the load,
activate full cooling, and send alerts to the host system.

BLAKE: So Stage One is the warning stage. The last checkpoint.

ALEX: Yes. If the BMS catches it at Stage One, you can potentially walk
away with a very hot pack but no fire. Miss it, and you're in Stage Two.

BLAKE: Stage two.

ALEX: Stage two is the point of no return. The temperature hits somewhere
between a hundred-and-thirty and a hundred-and-sixty degrees Celsius —
and at that temperature, the separator melts.

BLAKE: The separator — the thin sheet between the anode and cathode.

ALEX: Yes. It's a thin polyolefin plastic — polyethylene or polypropylene —
with a melting point in that range. When it melts, it collapses. The anode
and cathode are now in direct physical contact. That's a permanent internal
short circuit.

BLAKE: And all the stored energy...

ALEX: All of it dumps as heat, internally, right now. For our two-eighty
amp-hour cell at ninety percent charge, that's about eight-hundred-and-six
watt-hours of energy — all released as heat inside the cell in seconds to
minutes. Think about what that means physically — eight-hundred watt-hours
is enough to run a microwave oven for forty minutes, released inside a
metal can in seconds.

BLAKE: That's terrifying.

ALEX: And at Stage Two, nothing the BMS does matters anymore. The internal
short is physical — the electrodes are literally touching. No firmware
action can un-touch them.

BLAKE: So Stage Two is game over.

ALEX: For that cell, yes. The question now is whether the fire spreads to
the rest of the pack.

BLAKE: Stage three.

ALEX: Stage three is where LFP and NMC diverge dramatically — and this is
where the real story of why LFP is chosen for indoor systems gets told.

BLAKE: Walk me through it.

ALEX: In NMC and NCA chemistries — which are common in electric vehicles
and some consumer electronics — the cathode material is a layered oxide.
At around a hundred-and-eighty degrees Celsius, this layered oxide structure
breaks down and releases free oxygen.

BLAKE: Oxygen from inside the cell.

ALEX: Yes. And that free oxygen reacts with the liquid electrolyte — which
is an organic solvent and is flammable. You now have oxygen and fuel in
direct contact at very high temperature. The result is a self-feeding
combustion reaction inside the cell — venting, fire, potentially explosion.
And if one cell does this in a sixteen-cell pack, the heat and flame jump
to adjacent cells, which then do the same thing. A cascade that can consume
the entire pack in seconds.

BLAKE: That's the battery fires you see on the news.

ALEX: Exactly. EV fires, phone fires, hoverboard fires — often Stage Three
NMC or NCA chemistry reaching that oxygen-release temperature.

BLAKE: And LFP?

ALEX: LFP's crystal structure is fundamentally different. It has what's called
an olivine structure — and the oxygen atoms in the crystal are held by
phosphate groups. The bond between phosphorus and oxygen in that crystal
has a dissociation energy of five-hundred-and-forty-four kilojoules per mole.
That's an extremely strong bond.

BLAKE: Strong enough to survive extremely high temperatures.

ALEX: Even at two-hundred-and-seventy degrees Celsius — which is Stage Three
for LFP — this bond does not break. The oxygen is never released from the
crystal. Without oxygen release, there is no combustion chain reaction.

BLAKE: So LFP Stage Three is... what?

ALEX: The cell vents — hot gas and electrolyte vapour come out. Unpleasant.
Potentially dangerous if you're standing right next to it. But there is no
self-feeding fire. No explosion. The event is locally contained in a way
that NMC Stage Three simply is not.

BLAKE: And that's not just a nice-to-have — it's why building codes and
insurance policies treat LFP differently from NMC.

ALEX: In many jurisdictions, LFP is specifically permitted for indoor
residential installations — home energy storage systems — without requiring
dedicated fire suppression systems. NMC in the same application often
requires sprinklers, flame barriers, and gas venting infrastructure that
can add tens of thousands of dollars to an installation.

BLAKE: So the phosphorus-oxygen bond is literally worth thousands of dollars
in installation cost.

ALEX: And potentially priceless in terms of lives.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CELL-TO-CELL PROPAGATION — WHEN ONE BAD CELL THREATENS THE PACK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: So one cell goes into thermal runaway. Does the pack survive?

ALEX: It depends entirely on the pack design. Without intentional mitigation,
heat from the failed cell radiates into the neighbouring cells, pushing them
toward Stage One. They start their own cascade, which heats the cells next
to them, and so on. A pack-wide fire can happen in seconds.

BLAKE: So what does good design look like?

ALEX: Several things. First, cell spacing — leaving at least three millimetres
of air gap between adjacent cells. Air is a thermal insulator, and that gap
slows the heat transfer significantly. Second, thermal gap-filler pads made
of low-conductivity materials — things like vermiculite or ceramic pads at
zero-point-three to zero-point-five watts per metre-kelvin — placed between
cells to further slow heat transfer. Third, pyro fuses at each cell terminal
— these are fuses designed to blow in microseconds, disconnecting a failed
cell electrically so it can't short-circuit its neighbours while it vents.

BLAKE: And the BMS?

ALEX: The BMS's early detection is critical. If it catches Stage One — the
temperature rising faster than current alone can explain — and disconnects
the pack before Stage Two, the failed cell goes through thermal runaway but
the adjacent cells never get hot enough to start their own cascade.

BLAKE: So the BMS is the last line of defence against a one-cell failure
becoming a pack-level catastrophe.

ALEX: Exactly. And this is why dT/dt monitoring — tracking how fast
temperature is rising, not just the absolute temperature — is so critical.
We'll come back to that in detail in Section Three-point-Five.
