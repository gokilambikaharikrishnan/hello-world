--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3.1 — WHERE DOES THE HEAT ACTUALLY COME FROM?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Okay, so first things first. When a lithium battery is charging or
discharging, it produces heat. Why? Where is the heat coming from?

ALEX: There are three separate sources, and understanding each one matters
because they behave very differently.

BLAKE: Let's go through them one at a time.

ALEX: Source one is called Joule heating. And this is by far the biggest source
of heat in normal operation. The formula is incredibly simple: Q equals I squared
times R. That's heat equals current squared times resistance.

BLAKE: And that I-squared is the key part.

ALEX: It's huge. Think about what that means — if you double the current, you
don't double the heat. You quadruple it. Four times as much heat for double
the current.

BLAKE: Because you're squaring the current.

ALEX: Exactly. So if a cell generates, say, one watt of heat at half C-rate,
it generates four watts at one C, and sixteen watts at two C. The heat doesn't
scale linearly — it explodes.

BLAKE: So high charge and discharge rates are not just harder on the battery
chemically — they're thermally dangerous too.

ALEX: Right. And there's a really practical implication here for pack design.
If you have one big cell doing the work versus four smaller cells in parallel
doing the same work — the parallel cells each carry a quarter of the current.
Quarter the current squared means one-sixteenth the heat per cell.

BLAKE: That's a massive difference. So just adding cells in parallel is a
thermal management strategy, not just a capacity strategy.

ALEX: Exactly. Each parallel cell runs cooler because the current is shared.

BLAKE: Now, where does this resistance actually come from inside the cell?
Because it's not one thing, right?

ALEX: There are several layers of resistance stacked up inside every cell.
First, there's the resistance of the electrode materials themselves — the
graphite anode and the iron phosphate cathode. Second, there's the contact
resistance between the electrode and the current collector — the thin copper
or aluminium foil that the electrode is coated onto. Third, there's the
resistance of the electrolyte, the liquid that lithium ions travel through.
And fourth, there's the SEI layer — a thin film that forms on the graphite
surface. We talked about that in Chapter Two.

BLAKE: And all of those add up to the internal resistance number you see on a
cell datasheet.

ALEX: Right. For our reference cell — the two-hundred-and-eighty amp-hour
prismatic LFP cell — the total internal resistance is about half a milli-ohm.
That's zero point five thousandths of an ohm. Tiny.

BLAKE: That sounds tiny, but clearly it generates significant heat.

ALEX: Because the currents are enormous. At one C-rate for a two-eighty amp-hour
cell, you're flowing two-hundred-and-eighty amps. Plug that into the formula —
two-eighty squared times zero-point-zero-zero-zero-five — and you get about
thirty-nine watts of heat from a single cell.

BLAKE: Thirty-nine watts. That's like leaving a dim light bulb running inside
the cell.

ALEX: Continuously. And if you go to two C, that's eighty amps more current —
four times the heat — over a hundred and fifty watts from one cell.

BLAKE: Okay, I feel the urgency now.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ALEX: Source two is electrochemical reaction heat. This one is smaller — maybe
five to fifteen percent of total heat — but it's always present.

BLAKE: Where does it come from?

ALEX: No chemical reaction is perfectly efficient. When lithium moves in and out
of the electrodes, some of the energy gets converted to heat rather than
electrical energy. You can actually measure this by comparing the battery's
terminal voltage — the voltage you measure at the terminals — to the OCV, the
open circuit voltage, which is what the cell would show if it were perfectly
at rest.

BLAKE: And the difference between those two voltages represents...

ALEX: Wasted energy. The gap between terminal voltage and OCV during operation
is energy being dissipated as heat. It's called overpotential.

BLAKE: So even if the cell had zero ohmic resistance, it would still produce
some heat just from the chemistry.

ALEX: Yes. You can't get around it. It's a fundamental property of how
electrochemical reactions work.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ALEX: Source three is the strangest one — entropic heat. And this one is
fascinating because it can go either way.

BLAKE: Wait — it can cool the cell?

ALEX: Yes! Depending on the state of charge, entropy changes as lithium moves
in and out of the crystal. And that entropy change can either release heat or
absorb heat from the surroundings.

BLAKE: Like a reversible heat pump inside the cell.

ALEX: Exactly the right analogy. For LFP specifically, during discharge at low
states of charge, this entropic effect actually absorbs heat — the cell
cools itself slightly. During discharge at high states of charge, it adds
a little heat. The effect is only a few watts, but it's real and measurable.

BLAKE: And some BMS firmware actually accounts for this?

ALEX: Advanced ones do. They include a state-of-charge correction in the
thermal model — because if you don't, your temperature estimates will be
slightly wrong at the extremes.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE THERMAL GRADIENT — SURFACE VS. CORE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: So you've got three sources of heat all happening simultaneously inside
the cell. But here's what I keep thinking about — where is all that heat
actually generated? Is it uniform through the whole cell?

ALEX: No, and this is really important for BMS design. The heat is generated
in the electrode stack — which is right in the centre of the cell. And to
get out of the cell, it has to travel outward through the cell material.
But the electrode stack is a poor thermal conductor.

BLAKE: So heat builds up in the middle.

ALEX: Right. Even if you're cooling the outside of the cell perfectly, the
inside is still hotter. There's always a temperature gap between the core
and the surface during operation. At one C-rate, that gap is typically
five to fifteen degrees Celsius.

BLAKE: Five to fifteen degrees. So if my temperature sensor on the outside
of the cell reads fifty degrees...

ALEX: The core could be at sixty or sixty-five degrees. And that starts to
matter because the dangerous temperature thresholds — where the chemistry
starts to break down — are in the core, not the surface.

BLAKE: And the sensor is on the surface.

ALEX: Always. You can't put a sensor inside a live cell. So the BMS has to
estimate the core temperature using a correction formula — the estimated
core temperature equals the surface temperature plus a correction factor
times the current squared.

BLAKE: Current squared again.

ALEX: Because the core-to-surface gap is driven by Joule heating, which
scales as I squared.

BLAKE: For our reference cell — the two-eighty amp-hour, forty-five
millimetre thick LFP — what does the thermal resistance work out to?

ALEX: The thermal resistance from core to surface is about one-point-one
degrees Celsius per watt. At one C, the cell generates about thirty-nine
watts — giving a core-to-surface gap of about twenty-two degrees when
cooled symmetrically from both faces. That's enormous.

BLAKE: So the surface sensor is reading twenty-two degrees cooler than
the hottest point.

ALEX: Which is why you set your protection thresholds conservatively.
If you set the alarm at fifty-five degrees on the surface sensor, the
actual core is already at seventy-seven degrees when that alarm fires.

BLAKE: This is really making me rethink what "temperature monitoring" even
means.

ALEX: It's more complicated than just sticking a thermistor on and reading
the number. You have to know what you're measuring and what you're
not measuring.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3.2 — THERMAL RUNAWAY: THE MOST DANGEROUS THING THAT CAN HAPPEN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Okay. Section three-point-two. Thermal runaway. I've been dreading
this section in a good way because it's genuinely alarming physics.

ALEX: It is. And I want to start with the definition, because the word
"runaway" is very deliberate.

BLAKE: Let's hear it.

ALEX: Thermal runaway is what happens when a cell produces heat faster than
it can lose heat — and the extra heat accelerates the reactions that produce
even more heat. It's a positive feedback loop. More heat makes things hotter.
Hotter things generate more heat. More heat makes things even hotter still.

BLAKE: And at some point you can't stop it.

ALEX: That's the defining characteristic. Once the loop is established, no
external action can stop it. The cell will consume itself. Prevention is the
only strategy. That is literally the entire reason the BMS exists.

BLAKE: That's a strong statement.

ALEX: It's true though. Every overvoltage protection, every temperature
cutoff, every current limit in the BMS firmware — all of it ultimately exists
to prevent this one event.

BLAKE: So what triggers it? What starts the loop?

ALEX: There are four main triggers. First: overcharging. If you push a cell
above three-point-six-five volts per cell, lithium starts depositing as
metallic lithium rather than inserting into the graphite properly. The cathode
also becomes chemically unstable at that voltage. The BMS over-voltage
protection has to cut the charge before this point.

BLAKE: The second trigger?

ALEX: External heat. A neighbouring cell that's already in runaway can bake
the cells next to it. A fire in the room. A stuck heater element. If any
external heat source pushes the cell above about sixty degrees Celsius for
sustained periods, it can start the cascade.

BLAKE: Third?

ALEX: Internal short circuit. This is the scariest one because there's no
external cause. Tiny metallic spikes — called dendrites — can grow on the
graphite anode over time, especially if the cell has been overcharged or
charged at sub-zero temperatures. If a dendrite grows long enough to punch
through the thin plastic separator and touch the cathode on the other side,
you have a direct metal-to-metal connection between the two electrodes
inside the cell.

BLAKE: And that means...

ALEX: All the stored energy in the cell dumps as heat, internally, in
seconds. No current flowing through the external circuit — just a sudden
internal short that turns two-hundred-and-eighty amp-hours of stored energy
into pure heat right inside the cell. The BMS may see a voltage collapse,
but by then it's too late.

BLAKE: No way to prevent it once the dendrite forms and punctures.

ALEX: No way. Which is why the BMS works so hard to prevent the conditions
that allow dendrites to grow in the first place — no overcharge, no charging
at freezing temperatures, no excessive high-current charge events.

BLAKE: And the fourth trigger?

ALEX: Mechanical damage. Crushing the cell, puncturing it with a nail,
dropping it hard enough to deform the casing — all of these can physically
collapse the electrode layers inside, creating the same kind of internal
short as a dendrite. The BMS might detect a sudden change in internal
resistance as the first warning, but again, by the time the short forms,
the cascade is starting.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE THREE-STAGE CASCADE — UNDERSTANDING EACH MOMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Okay, so the loop starts. What actually happens inside the cell?
Walk me through the physics step by step.

ALEX: Thermal runaway doesn't happen all at once. It unfolds in three
distinct stages, each one hotter than the last, each one triggering the next.
And knowing where you are in the cascade tells you whether you can still
stop it.

BLAKE: Stage zero?

ALEX: Stage zero is just normal operation. Twenty-five to sixty degrees
Celsius. Heat from Joule heating and chemistry is being produced and removed
by the cooling system. The BMS is watching temperature, voltage, and current
continuously. This is the wide-open intervention window — anything going
wrong should be caught and fixed here.

BLAKE: That's the safe zone.

ALEX: Right. And the whole design philosophy is to keep every cell in
Stage Zero all the time.

BLAKE: Stage one.

ALEX: Stage one starts when the cell temperature crosses about ninety to
a hundred-and-twenty degrees Celsius. At that temperature, something
called the SEI layer starts to break apart.

BLAKE: The SEI layer — we covered this in Chapter Two. That's the protective
coating on the graphite anode.

ALEX: Exactly. It forms naturally during the first few charge cycles and
it's actually essential — without it, the electrolyte would just decompose
on the graphite surface. But at around ninety degrees, it starts to
decompose itself. And the decomposition is exothermic — it releases heat.

BLAKE: So the cell is now generating extra heat beyond just Joule heating.

ALEX: Yes. And that extra heat raises the temperature, which accelerates
the decomposition, which produces more heat. The loop has started. CO and
CO₂ gas are produced — which raises pressure inside the cell.

BLAKE: Can the BMS detect this?

ALEX: Yes, and this is crucial — Stage One is the last moment where BMS
intervention can stop the cascade. The temperature is rising at maybe
half a degree to two degrees per minute at Stage One onset. That's slow
enough to detect and fast enough to act on. The BMS can cut the load,
activate full cooling, and send alerts to the host system.

BLAKE: So Stage One is the warning stage. The last checkpoint.

ALEX: Yes. If the BMS catches it at Stage One, you can potentially walk
away with a very hot pack but no fire. Miss it, and you're in Stage Two.

BLAKE: Stage two.

ALEX: Stage two is the point of no return. The temperature hits somewhere
between a hundred-and-thirty and a hundred-and-sixty degrees Celsius —
and at that temperature, the separator melts.

BLAKE: The separator — the thin sheet between the anode and cathode.

ALEX: Yes. It's a thin polyolefin plastic — polyethylene or polypropylene —
with a melting point in that range. When it melts, it collapses. The anode
and cathode are now in direct physical contact. That's a permanent internal
short circuit.

BLAKE: And all the stored energy...

ALEX: All of it dumps as heat, internally, right now. For our two-eighty
amp-hour cell at ninety percent charge, that's about eight-hundred-and-six
watt-hours of energy — all released as heat inside the cell in seconds to
minutes. Think about what that means physically — eight-hundred watt-hours
is enough to run a microwave oven for forty minutes, released inside a
metal can in seconds.

BLAKE: That's terrifying.

ALEX: And at Stage Two, nothing the BMS does matters anymore. The internal
short is physical — the electrodes are literally touching. No firmware
action can un-touch them.

BLAKE: So Stage Two is game over.

ALEX: For that cell, yes. The question now is whether the fire spreads to
the rest of the pack.

BLAKE: Stage three.

ALEX: Stage three is where LFP and NMC diverge dramatically — and this is
where the real story of why LFP is chosen for indoor systems gets told.

BLAKE: Walk me through it.

ALEX: In NMC and NCA chemistries — which are common in electric vehicles
and some consumer electronics — the cathode material is a layered oxide.
At around a hundred-and-eighty degrees Celsius, this layered oxide structure
breaks down and releases free oxygen.

BLAKE: Oxygen from inside the cell.

ALEX: Yes. And that free oxygen reacts with the liquid electrolyte — which
is an organic solvent and is flammable. You now have oxygen and fuel in
direct contact at very high temperature. The result is a self-feeding
combustion reaction inside the cell — venting, fire, potentially explosion.
And if one cell does this in a sixteen-cell pack, the heat and flame jump
to adjacent cells, which then do the same thing. A cascade that can consume
the entire pack in seconds.

BLAKE: That's the battery fires you see on the news.

ALEX: Exactly. EV fires, phone fires, hoverboard fires — often Stage Three
NMC or NCA chemistry reaching that oxygen-release temperature.

BLAKE: And LFP?

ALEX: LFP's crystal structure is fundamentally different. It has what's called
an olivine structure — and the oxygen atoms in the crystal are held by
phosphate groups. The bond between phosphorus and oxygen in that crystal
has a dissociation energy of five-hundred-and-forty-four kilojoules per mole.
That's an extremely strong bond.

BLAKE: Strong enough to survive extremely high temperatures.

ALEX: Even at two-hundred-and-seventy degrees Celsius — which is Stage Three
for LFP — this bond does not break. The oxygen is never released from the
crystal. Without oxygen release, there is no combustion chain reaction.

BLAKE: So LFP Stage Three is... what?

ALEX: The cell vents — hot gas and electrolyte vapour come out. Unpleasant.
Potentially dangerous if you're standing right next to it. But there is no
self-feeding fire. No explosion. The event is locally contained in a way
that NMC Stage Three simply is not.

BLAKE: And that's not just a nice-to-have — it's why building codes and
insurance policies treat LFP differently from NMC.

ALEX: In many jurisdictions, LFP is specifically permitted for indoor
residential installations — home energy storage systems — without requiring
dedicated fire suppression systems. NMC in the same application often
requires sprinklers, flame barriers, and gas venting infrastructure that
can add tens of thousands of dollars to an installation.

BLAKE: So the phosphorus-oxygen bond is literally worth thousands of dollars
in installation cost.

ALEX: And potentially priceless in terms of lives.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CELL-TO-CELL PROPAGATION — WHEN ONE BAD CELL THREATENS THE PACK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: So one cell goes into thermal runaway. Does the pack survive?

ALEX: It depends entirely on the pack design. Without intentional mitigation,
heat from the failed cell radiates into the neighbouring cells, pushing them
toward Stage One. They start their own cascade, which heats the cells next
to them, and so on. A pack-wide fire can happen in seconds.

BLAKE: So what does good design look like?

ALEX: Several things. First, cell spacing — leaving at least three millimetres
of air gap between adjacent cells. Air is a thermal insulator, and that gap
slows the heat transfer significantly. Second, thermal gap-filler pads made
of low-conductivity materials — things like vermiculite or ceramic pads at
zero-point-three to zero-point-five watts per metre-kelvin — placed between
cells to further slow heat transfer. Third, pyro fuses at each cell terminal
— these are fuses designed to blow in microseconds, disconnecting a failed
cell electrically so it can't short-circuit its neighbours while it vents.

BLAKE: And the BMS?

ALEX: The BMS's early detection is critical. If it catches Stage One — the
temperature rising faster than current alone can explain — and disconnects
the pack before Stage Two, the failed cell goes through thermal runaway but
the adjacent cells never get hot enough to start their own cascade.

BLAKE: So the BMS is the last line of defence against a one-cell failure
becoming a pack-level catastrophe.

ALEX: Exactly. And this is why dT/dt monitoring — tracking how fast
temperature is rising, not just the absolute temperature — is so critical.
We'll come back to that in detail in Section Three-point-Five.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3.3 — THERMAL MANAGEMENT: KEEPING EVERY CELL IN THE SAFE ZONE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Okay, so we know what happens when temperature goes wrong. Now let's
talk about how you keep it from going wrong in the first place. Section
Three-point-Three: thermal management.

ALEX: Thermal management is the engineering discipline of keeping every single
cell in the pack within its optimal temperature window — fifteen to thirty-five
degrees Celsius — all the time.

BLAKE: And this isn't just about safety.

ALEX: No — this is actually where most of the economic value is. Because
temperature is the single biggest factor in how long a battery lasts. There
is a well-established physical law called the Arrhenius relationship that says
the rate of chemical aging roughly doubles for every ten degrees of temperature
increase above optimal.

BLAKE: Double for every ten degrees.

ALEX: Yes. So a pack running at thirty-five degrees instead of twenty-five
ages about twice as fast. At forty-five degrees, it ages four times faster.
Over ten years of operation in a home energy storage system, that's the
difference between replacing your pack once — maybe — versus replacing it
four times.

BLAKE: At what cost?

ALEX: A fifteen-kilowatt-hour LFP pack replacement runs five-thousand to
eight-thousand dollars at current prices. Multiply that by four replacements
over ten years — you're looking at twenty to thirty thousand dollars in
additional cost compared to a well-designed thermal system.

BLAKE: So thermal management can literally save the cost of multiple pack
replacements.

ALEX: Yes. And this is before we even talk about fire safety. The economics
alone justify spending real money on thermal design.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHY TEMPERATURE UNIFORMITY MATTERS AS MUCH AS ABSOLUTE TEMPERATURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: And it's not just about keeping the pack cool overall. It's about
keeping it uniform too, right?

ALEX: This is a subtlety that many first-time pack designers miss. Even if the
average temperature across all sixteen cells looks fine — say, twenty-eight
degrees — if some cells are at twenty degrees and others are at forty degrees,
you have a serious problem.

BLAKE: Because the hot cells age faster.

ALEX: Much faster. Ten degrees hotter means twice the aging rate. Twenty degrees
hotter means four times the aging rate. The hot cells slowly lose capacity
relative to the cool cells. Over hundreds of cycles, the pack develops a
growing imbalance — some cells are at eighty percent capacity while others
are still at ninety-eight percent.

BLAKE: And the cell balancer can't fix that?

ALEX: Not fully. The balancer can equalise state-of-charge differences — top up
cells that ran low, bleed down cells that ran high. But it cannot fix different
cells having different capacities. That's a permanent consequence of differential
aging. The only solution is to keep the temperature difference — delta-T across
the pack — below five degrees Celsius at all times.

BLAKE: Five degrees maximum spread across the whole pack.

ALEX: That's the design target. If you're seeing more than eight degrees spread,
you need to reduce load or increase cooling. If you're seeing more than twelve
degrees, something in your thermal path has failed — a blocked vent, a dry
gap-filler pad, a failing fan — and you need to investigate.

BLAKE: And the BMS is tracking this continuously?

ALEX: Yes. The BMS computes delta-T-pack — maximum minus minimum cell
temperature — every measurement cycle. A slowly growing delta-T-pack over
months, at the same load, is an early warning of thermal system degradation.
You catch it before any single cell exceeds its protection threshold.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PASSIVE COOLING — THE SIMPLEST THERMAL DESIGN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Okay, so how do you actually achieve that temperature control?
Let's start with the simplest approach.

ALEX: Passive cooling. No fans, no pumps, no powered components. Heat travels
out of the cell through the casing by conduction, then warm air naturally rises
and carries the heat away — that's natural convection. Nothing to break,
nothing to maintain.

BLAKE: When is that sufficient?

ALEX: For a home energy storage system with LFP cells, if your average charge
and discharge rate stays below zero-point-five C-rate and the room temperature
is below thirty degrees, passive cooling with at least five millimetres of
spacing between cells and good pack ventilation is generally enough to keep
cells below thirty-five degrees.

BLAKE: Why is LFP especially good for passive cooling?

ALEX: LFP has inherently low internal resistance — about half a milli-ohm for
our reference cell. At zero-point-five C, you're only pulling a hundred-and-
forty amps from a two-eighty amp-hour cell. The heat generated is about
five to ten watts per cell. Natural air movement can carry that away without
the cell rising more than ten degrees above room temperature.

BLAKE: So for a home storage system cycling daily — charge from solar during
the day, discharge in the evening — passive cooling works.

ALEX: In most cases, yes. It's elegant. The fewer components you have, the
fewer things that can fail.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ACTIVE COOLING — WHEN YOU NEED MORE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: What breaks that assumption? When do you need active cooling?

ALEX: Two situations. First, if the sustained discharge rate exceeds one C
continuously. Second, if the ambient temperature is above thirty-five degrees.
In either case, passive convection can't remove heat fast enough and you need
a powered cooling system.

BLAKE: What are the options?

ALEX: Two main approaches. Forced-air cooling — fans blowing air across the
cells or through channels between them. And liquid cooling — a coolant loop
with a pump, heat exchanger, and cold plates pressed against the cell surfaces.

BLAKE: Tell me about forced air first.

ALEX: Forced air is simpler and cheaper. A fan can achieve heat transfer
coefficients of twenty to eighty watts per square metre per kelvin — meaning
for every degree of temperature difference between the cell surface and the
incoming air, you're removing twenty to eighty watts per square metre of
cell surface. That's typically sufficient for packs up to about five kilowatts
of continuous output in ambient temperatures below forty degrees.

BLAKE: What are the downsides?

ALEX: Dust and debris accumulate in the airflow path over time, clogging
filters and reducing airflow. The fans generate noise. And at very high power
levels, you'd need enormous airflow rates that become impractical.

BLAKE: And the BMS controls the fan speed?

ALEX: Via a PID loop — proportional-integral-derivative control — where the
fan speed is adjusted based on how far the cell temperature is above the
target setpoint. Temperature goes up, fan speeds up. Temperature drops back
down, fan slows down.

BLAKE: What about liquid cooling?

ALEX: Liquid cooling is dramatically more effective. Water-glycol mixtures or
dielectric fluids can achieve heat transfer coefficients of five-hundred to
five-thousand watts per square metre per kelvin — orders of magnitude better
than air. You can cool cells at very high C-rates in any ambient temperature.

BLAKE: What's the downside?

ALEX: Complexity and cost. You need a pump, a coolant reservoir, a heat
exchanger or radiator, and cold plates precisely fitted to each cell surface.
Leak risk — even tiny leaks can damage electronics. And the system needs
maintenance. Liquid cooling is standard in electric vehicles and large commercial
energy storage systems. For home systems, it's usually overkill unless you're
doing very high power cycling.

BLAKE: And there's an important design rule about who controls the cooling system.

ALEX: Yes — and this is a common mistake. Some designs have the BMS and the
cooling controller as separate systems with separate decision-making. That's
dangerous. The BMS detects overtemperature and cuts the load — but the cooling
system is still running at full blast and can push the cells below the minimum
charge temperature. Or the cooling runs on a timer rather than responding to
actual cell temperature.

BLAKE: So one system doesn't know what the other is doing.

ALEX: Right. The correct design is: the BMS is the single authority over thermal
state. It sends commands to the cooling system — fan speed setpoint, coolant
valve position — via CAN bus or a dedicated thermal interface. The cooling
system executes; the BMS decides. One authority, one decision-maker.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHERE TO PUT THE TEMPERATURE SENSORS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: The accuracy of the BMS's thermal decisions is completely limited by
where the sensors are placed.

ALEX: This is so important, and so easy to get wrong. A single sensor on the
circuit board tells you almost nothing about what's happening at the cell core
in a sixteen-cell pack.

BLAKE: What's the right placement strategy?

ALEX: Six sensor locations matter. First — critical — the cell midpoint surface.
Press the sensor flat against the large face of the cell at mid-height. That's
where the surface temperature peaks. You need at least one sensor for every
two cells.

BLAKE: Second?

ALEX: The positive tab junction — right where the busbar is welded to the cell
terminal. The tab area carries the full pack current, making it the highest
current density point. The highest Joule heating happens there. This spot must
not exceed eighty degrees Celsius.

BLAKE: Third?

ALEX: Inlet air or coolant — measuring what temperature the cooling medium is
arriving at. The BMS needs this to calculate how much cooling headroom is
available and to adjust fan or pump speed proactively, before the cells heat up.

BLAKE: Fourth?

ALEX: Outlet air or coolant — measuring how warm the cooling medium is leaving.
Compare inlet to outlet, multiply by flow rate, and you know exactly how much
heat the cooling system is actually removing. This is how you verify the cooling
is working as designed.

BLAKE: Fifth?

ALEX: The BMS circuit board itself — the AFE chips, the MCU, the power
components on the BMS board have their own temperature limits. Typically
minus-forty to plus-eighty-five degrees Celsius. You need to know if the
electronics themselves are getting too hot.

BLAKE: And sixth?

ALEX: The cell bottom centre — optional, but this is where you get the
earliest warning of internal hot spots. Heat from the core takes thirty to
a hundred-and-twenty seconds at one C to reach the surface. Bottom sensors
on prismatic cells respond faster than side sensors because of the thermal
path geometry.

BLAKE: And remember the correction — the surface reading is always cooler
than the core.

ALEX: At one C, five to fifteen degrees cooler. The BMS applies the correction
formula to estimate core temperature, and sets its hard cutoffs accordingly.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3.4 — MECHANICAL BEHAVIOUR: THE BATTERY THAT BREATHES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Section Three-point-Four. Mechanical considerations. And this one
genuinely surprised me when I first learned about it. These cells aren't just
sitting there passively — they physically move.

ALEX: Every single charge cycle. When the cell charges, the electrodes expand.
When it discharges, they contract. It's like the cell is breathing.

BLAKE: Why? What causes the expansion?

ALEX: When lithium ions flow into the graphite anode during charging, they're
inserting themselves into the carbon crystal lattice — squeezing between the
layers of carbon atoms. This forces the lattice to expand. It's like trying
to pack more books onto a shelf — the shelf gets wider to accommodate them.

BLAKE: And when they leave during discharge?

ALEX: The books leave, the shelf contracts. The graphite returns to its
natural size. This happens every single cycle — over thousands and thousands
of charge-discharge cycles.

BLAKE: And the same thing happens at the cathode?

ALEX: Yes, but with slightly different geometry. In LFP, the iron phosphate
crystal changes volume by about six-point-eight percent as lithium moves in
and out. The graphite anode changes by about ten percent at full lithiation.

BLAKE: Ten percent? That's a lot.

ALEX: It is. But for the whole assembled cell — the electrodes, separator,
electrolyte, and casing all packed together — the net thickness change is
more like two percent for LFP. Compare that to NMC, which is four to five
percent. LFP has less mechanical stress per cycle, which is one of the
reasons it has better cycle life.

BLAKE: Let's put some real numbers on it for our reference cell.

ALEX: Our two-eighty amp-hour prismatic LFP cell is forty-five millimetres
thick when empty. At full charge, it's about forty-five-point-nine millimetres.
That's roughly zero-point-nine millimetres of expansion.

BLAKE: Less than a millimetre. That doesn't sound like much.

ALEX: But now multiply it by sixteen cells in a series pack. Each cell expands
zero-point-nine millimetres simultaneously. That's sixteen times zero-point-nine,
which is fourteen-point-four millimetres of total expansion — nearly one-and-a-half
centimetres of movement in the pack.

BLAKE: The pack grows by one-and-a-half centimetres when you charge it.

ALEX: And shrinks back when you discharge. Every day in a home ESS system. Over
three-thousand cycles in a ten-year lifespan. The mounting frame, the end-plates,
the busbars — all of it has to be designed to accommodate this movement or it
will break.

BLAKE: What happens if it's not designed for it?

ALEX: The most common failure is the end-plates cracking, which allows the
cells to expand freely and lose their compression — which hurts performance
as we'll discuss in a moment. Or the busbars work-harden and crack from
repeated flexing. Or the cells themselves develop internal layer separations
as the electrode stack cycles between compressed and expanded states without
proper support.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
IRREVERSIBLE SWELLING — THE HIDDEN HEALTH INDICATOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: You mentioned there's a difference between normal swelling and
irreversible swelling.

ALEX: This is really important for state-of-health monitoring. Normal cyclic
swelling — expand on charge, contract on discharge, back to baseline — is
completely healthy. The problem is when a cell gets permanently thicker over
time, never returning to its original baseline thickness.

BLAKE: What causes permanent swelling?

ALEX: Gas generation inside the cell. Three main causes. First, overcharging
above three-point-six-five volts causes the electrolyte to oxidise at the
cathode surface, producing gas. Second, the SEI layer continues to grow on
the anode over thousands of cycles, consuming electrolyte and producing CO₂.
Third, if there's any moisture contamination in the electrolyte, it reacts
to form hydrofluoric acid and hydrogen gas.

BLAKE: All of which fill up inside the sealed cell.

ALEX: Right. The cell pouch or casing can't expand indefinitely, so internal
pressure builds. The cell gets permanently fatter. And a permanently fatter
cell is a cell that is accumulating degradation.

BLAKE: Can you measure this?

ALEX: Periodically measuring cell thickness is one approach — you'd need to
do this with the cells removed from the pack. The more elegant approach is
to fit a strain gauge or load cell into the compression fixture. As the cells
gas and swell permanently, the force on the fixture increases. A slowly
rising force reading over months tells you the cells are gassing, long before
any voltage or capacity metric shows degradation.

BLAKE: That's a pretty sophisticated health indicator.

ALEX: It's one of the most direct physical measurements of cell health you can
make. Because it catches gassing — which is the early stage of degradation —
before it shows up in electrical measurements.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
COMPRESSION FIXTURES — WHY SQUEEZING IS ACTUALLY GOOD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Now here's the counterintuitive part. You mentioned that applying
controlled pressure to the cells actually improves performance.

ALEX: Yes. This sounds wrong — you'd think clamping a cell tightly would
damage it. But for prismatic LFP cells, controlled compression genuinely
extends cycle life and improves capacity.

BLAKE: Why?

ALEX: Four reasons. First, compression keeps the electrode layers pressed
tightly together. When they separate — even slightly — the resistance between
them increases, and performance drops. Second, it prevents the layers from
peeling apart as the cell breathes through each charge-discharge cycle.
Repeated expansion and contraction without support causes delamination over
time.

BLAKE: Third?

ALEX: Less free space inside for gas bubbles to accumulate in the electrode
stack. If gas collects between layers, it creates dead zones where lithium
can't move, reducing usable capacity. Fourth, limiting how much the anode
can expand each cycle slows the growth rate of the SEI layer.

BLAKE: So the compression fixture is actually part of the ageing management
strategy, not just a structural component.

ALEX: Exactly. And there's a specific target pressure. For a two-eighty
amp-hour prismatic LFP cell, the optimal compression range is ten to thirty
kilopascals — that's about one-point-five to four-point-four PSI. Very light
pressure by engineering standards, but carefully controlled.

BLAKE: What happens if the pressure gets too high?

ALEX: Above a hundred kilopascals, you start forcing electrolyte out of the
electrode pores. The liquid that lithium ions travel through gets squeezed
out, reducing the cell's ability to deliver current. Capacity drops
permanently.

BLAKE: So you need a pressure range — not too little, not too much.

ALEX: And you need to maintain that range as the cell swells. If you use
rigid end-plates and tie-rods with no spring compliance, the pressure
builds as the cells expand — potentially exceeding the maximum. You have to
include spring elements — Belleville washers or spring plates — that absorb
the expansion and keep pressure in the target range throughout the cell's
state of charge swing.

BLAKE: The design target is fifteen kilopascals preload at fifty percent
state of charge — the midpoint — with a plus-or-minus ten kilopascal range
as the cells breathe.

ALEX: Right. So at zero percent state of charge, the cells are their smallest
and the spring is under less compression — maybe five kilopascals. At full
charge, the cells are at their largest and the spring is compressed further —
maybe twenty-five kilopascals. You stay within the ten-to-thirty target range
throughout.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VIBRATION AND SHOCK — SURVIVING REAL-WORLD CONDITIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: And then there's the vibration issue. These packs don't live in a
perfectly still laboratory.

ALEX: No. During shipping, they're bounced around in trucks and warehouses.
During installation, they might get dropped. Once installed, floor-mounted
packs experience continuous low-frequency vibration from building mechanical
systems.

BLAKE: Are there actual test standards for this?

ALEX: Several. IEC sixty-two-one-three-three covers general lithium battery
testing. It includes a vibration test at seven to fifty hertz at one G —
one times the force of gravity — for thirty minutes in each of three axes.
And a shock test at a hundred G for six milliseconds in three directions.

BLAKE: A hundred G shock. That's like a significant drop.

ALEX: Yes. And for transport, there's UN thirty-eight-point-three — the
international regulation for shipping lithium batteries. It covers vibration,
shock, altitude changes, and thermal cycling. Any pack that's shipped by air
has to pass these tests.

BLAKE: From the BMS perspective, what fails in vibration testing?

ALEX: Almost always the physical connections, not the firmware. Crimped
cell-sense wires that aren't strain-relieved properly. Soldered joints that
work-harden and crack. NTC thermistors whose leads fatigue and break. BMS
connector bodies that loosen and increase contact resistance.

BLAKE: What's the fix?

ALEX: Use connectors rated for vibration environments. Molex MX-one-fifty or
equivalent — these are connectors designed with positive retention, dual beams,
and high insertion forces so they don't back out under vibration. Don't use
the small hobby-grade JST or Dupont headers for anything in a production BMS.
They're fine for prototyping, not for a pack that's going to bounce around
in a truck for a week.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3.5 — BMS THERMAL FIRMWARE: FROM PHYSICS TO PROTECTION RULES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Okay. Final technical section — Section Three-point-Five. This is
where we take all the physics we've learned and turn it into actual firmware
decisions. Every number in the BMS code has a physical reason.

ALEX: Yes, and I love this section because it makes explicit what's usually
left implicit. When a BMS designer writes "charge cutoff at forty-five degrees",
that number isn't arbitrary. It comes from a calculation.

BLAKE: Let's go through the key thresholds one by one. Start with the most
important one for normal operation — the overtemperature charge cutoff.

ALEX: The overtemperature charge cutoff is set at forty-five degrees Celsius
as a warning level and fifty-five degrees as the hard cutoff. And these numbers
come from SEI growth kinetics.

BLAKE: Explain the connection.

ALEX: Remember the SEI layer — the protective film on the graphite anode.
It grows slowly throughout the cell's life, and that growth consumes both
lithium and electrolyte. Faster SEI growth means faster capacity fade means
shorter cycle life. And SEI growth follows the Arrhenius relationship — it
roughly doubles for every ten degrees of temperature increase.

BLAKE: So at thirty-five degrees — the top of the optimal range — you have
baseline SEI growth rate. Call that one-times.

ALEX: At forty-five degrees, it's about two-point-one times baseline. That
means the cell ages at double the rate if you charge it continuously at
forty-five degrees. That's why it's a warning — you derate the charge current
to reduce heat generation and keep the temperature from climbing further.

BLAKE: At fifty-five degrees?

ALEX: Four-point-three times baseline. SEI is growing more than four times
faster than it should. At that rate, you're burning through cycle life so fast
that continued charging is simply not acceptable. Hard cutoff.

BLAKE: And why is the discharge cutoff higher — sixty degrees?

ALEX: Because charging and discharging have different effects at the anode.
During charging, lithium ions are being pushed into the graphite — right to the
surface where the SEI forms. That's when SEI growth accelerates with temperature.
During discharging, lithium leaves the graphite — the SEI isn't growing because
the conditions for its formation aren't present. The limiting concern during
discharge is electrolyte stability, which has a higher thermal limit.

BLAKE: So you can discharge at higher temperatures than you can charge.

ALEX: Right. The asymmetry is physically justified. Forty-five-degree charge
warning, sixty-degree discharge limit.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE ABSOLUTE RULE — NO CHARGING BELOW ZERO DEGREES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: And now the other end of the temperature spectrum — the cold cutoff.
This one is absolute.

ALEX: This is the hardest rule in thermal firmware, and it has zero exceptions.
Below zero degrees Celsius, charge current must be completely blocked — not
reduced, not limited to point-one C, but zero.

BLAKE: Why? What physically happens?

ALEX: At low temperatures, the graphite anode becomes kinetically sluggish.
Lithium ions arrive at the surface during charging but can't insert into the
crystal lattice fast enough. They pile up on the surface and can't find a
site to enter. So instead of inserting into the graphite, they deposit as
metallic lithium on top of the graphite surface.

BLAKE: And metallic lithium on the surface is bad.

ALEX: Very bad. Metallic lithium doesn't stay as a smooth layer — it grows
as dendrites. Needle-like crystalline spikes that keep growing with each
charge cycle. The separator between the anode and cathode is only twenty to
thirty microns thick — less than the diameter of a human hair. A dendrite
that grows even slightly can puncture through it.

BLAKE: And then you have an internal short circuit.

ALEX: The full energy of the cell — all eight-hundred-and-six watt-hours at
full charge for our reference cell — dumps as heat internally. Dendrite-induced
internal shorts are particularly dangerous because they can happen suddenly,
without any warning from the BMS, days or weeks after the cold charging event
that caused the dendrite to form.

BLAKE: So you charge below zero degrees, the dendrite forms but doesn't short yet.
Then later, during normal cycling, it grows one more tiny amount and punctures
the separator.

ALEX: Yes. The cause and effect can be separated by weeks. Which is why there
is absolutely no acceptable "gentle charge at zero point one C in cold weather"
strategy. You must wait for the cells to warm up.

BLAKE: The BMS temperature derating table.

ALEX: Yes. The BMS stores a lookup table. Below zero degrees — zero current,
no exceptions. Zero to five degrees — maximum zero-point-one C charge rate.
Five to ten degrees — zero-point-two C. Ten to fifteen degrees — zero-point-five
C. Fifteen to thirty-five degrees is the optimal zone — full one C charge
rate allowed. Above thirty-five degrees, you start derating: zero-point-seven
C at thirty-five to forty-five degrees, zero-point-three C at forty-five to
fifty-five degrees. Above fifty-five degrees — zero, hard cutoff.

BLAKE: And the BMS enforces this — the charger doesn't get to override it.

ALEX: The BMS sends the allowed current as a command to the charger, usually
via CAN bus. The charger must comply. The BMS is the authority.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
DERATING — LINEAR REDUCTION BETWEEN THRESHOLDS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: And between those thresholds, the reduction isn't a sudden step —
it's a smooth curve.

ALEX: Linear derating. Between the warning threshold and the hard cutoff, the
BMS linearly interpolates the allowed current. So at forty-five degrees, you
have full rate. At fifty-five degrees, zero rate. At fifty degrees — exactly
halfway — you're allowed fifty percent of full rate. The charger ramps down
smoothly rather than chopping on and off.

BLAKE: Why is smooth derating better than a simple on-off cutoff?

ALEX: Two reasons. First, it's gentler on the load — abrupt current steps
cause voltage transients that stress the cells. Second, it allows the system
to keep the cell just below the hard cutoff rather than alternating between
full charge and complete stop. The BMS is trying to extract as much useful
charging as possible while staying safe.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
dT/dt MONITORING — THE THERMAL RUNAWAY EARLY WARNING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Now the most sophisticated temperature feature — dT/dt monitoring.
This is where the BMS goes beyond just reading a number and starts watching
for patterns.

ALEX: Right. dT/dt is the rate of temperature rise — how many degrees per
second the temperature is climbing. And this is the definitive early warning
signal for thermal runaway.

BLAKE: Why is the rate more useful than the absolute temperature?

ALEX: Because normal Joule heating produces a predictable, current-proportional
temperature rise. If you double the current, you expect roughly four times
the heating rate — because of that I-squared relationship. But when an
exothermic chemical reaction starts inside the cell — like SEI decomposition
at Stage One — the temperature rises faster than the current alone can explain.

BLAKE: The temperature is climbing without a matching increase in current.

ALEX: That's the signature. A healthy cell running at two C at room temperature
produces about zero-point-one degrees Celsius per second of temperature rise.
When Stage One SEI decomposition begins, you see zero-point-five to one degree
per second — without any increase in current. That mismatch is the thermal
runaway warning.

BLAKE: What does the BMS do with it?

ALEX: Three alarm levels. Level One: dT/dt above zero-point-five degrees per
second — reduce load, increase cooling, alert the host system. Something is
wrong and you're trying to arrest it.

BLAKE: Level Two?

ALEX: dT/dt above one degree per second — disconnect the pack immediately.
Open the main FETs. Latch a fault that requires manual reset. Send a
CAN alert. The situation is critical and you're cutting off the energy source.

BLAKE: Level Three?

ALEX: dT/dt above two degrees per second — thermal runaway is in progress.
In addition to everything at Level Two, if there's a fire suppression system
connected, you trigger it. And you log everything for post-event analysis.

BLAKE: Now, measuring dT/dt sounds simple — just take the temperature at
two consecutive moments and divide by the time between them. But there's
a catch.

ALEX: A big catch. Temperature sensors on a twelve-bit analog-to-digital
converter — which is typical for BMS designs — have quantisation noise.
That means even if the temperature isn't changing, the readings jump around
slightly due to electrical noise. At a sampling rate of ten times per second —
which is what you need for good dT/dt detection — those tiny noise jumps
look like very rapid temperature changes.

BLAKE: Creating false alarms.

ALEX: Right. You'd be triggering Level One or Level Two alarms randomly
from noise. So you filter the dT/dt with an exponential moving average.
In the firmware code, it's: new filtered dTdt equals zero-point-eight
times the previous filtered value, plus zero-point-two times the new
raw measurement.

BLAKE: That zero-point-two weighting — is that always right?

ALEX: You have to tune it. Reduce it and you get more smoothing but slower
response — you might miss a fast thermal event. Increase it and you get
faster response but more noise — you get false alarms. For the two-hundred-
and-eighty amp-hour prismatic LFP, zero-point-two gives a response time of
about one second while smoothing out the ADC noise effectively.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
MULTI-SENSOR VOTING — PROTECTING AGAINST SENSOR FAILURES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: And there's one more piece of firmware sophistication we should
cover — voting logic.

ALEX: Yes. Because a single temperature sensor can fail. Shorted sensors read
full-scale — which looks like the pack is extremely hot. Open sensors read
nothing or maximum. Either failure can trigger a false overtemperature alarm.

BLAKE: And in a UPS, a false overtemperature alarm means the UPS shuts down.

ALEX: At exactly the moment you need it. Power fails, UPS activates — and if
a spurious thermal alarm fires at that moment, the UPS goes offline too.
The very system that's supposed to protect you from power failures has just
contributed to one.

BLAKE: How does voting logic fix this?

ALEX: Simple but effective. Instead of triggering a protection action when
any single sensor exceeds the threshold, require at least two sensors to
agree. If one sensor reads a hundred-and-thirty degrees but the other five
sensors all read thirty degrees, you have a failed sensor, not a hot pack.
No action.

BLAKE: But what about thermal runaway? You don't want to require two sensors
to agree before responding to that.

ALEX: Exactly right, and this is the subtle part of the implementation.
For warning-level events — temperatures at forty or forty-five degrees,
mild dT/dt readings — use two-of-N voting. But for thermal runaway level
events — dT/dt above one or two degrees per second — use one-of-N voting.
Any single sensor detecting runaway-level temperature rise should immediately
trigger disconnection.

BLAKE: Because the cost of a false positive at the runaway level —

ALEX: Is the UPS going offline briefly. Annoying.

BLAKE: And the cost of a false negative at the runaway level —

ALEX: Is a cell fire spreading through the pack. Catastrophic. The
asymmetry of consequences tells you exactly where to set the voting threshold.

BLAKE: One-of-N for runaway detection. Two-of-N for everything else.

ALEX: That's the rule.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CHAPTER SUMMARY — TEN PHYSICAL PRINCIPLES EVERY BMS DESIGNER MUST KNOW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Let's pull it all together. What are the essential takeaways
from Chapter Three?

ALEX: Ten physical principles. These aren't rules someone made up — each one
traces directly back to a physical law or mechanism.

BLAKE: Number one.

ALEX: Heat scales with current squared, not linearly. Double the current,
quadruple the heat. This is why high C-rates are thermally dangerous, and
why adding cells in parallel reduces heat per cell so dramatically. The I-squared
relationship governs everything in thermal design.

BLAKE: Number two.

ALEX: The surface sensor reads five to fifteen degrees cooler than the cell
core at one C-rate. Your BMS is always measuring the outside and inferring
the inside. Set your protection thresholds conservatively to account for
this lag. Apply the core temperature correction formula in firmware.

BLAKE: Number three.

ALEX: Stage One — SEI decomposition at ninety to a hundred-and-twenty degrees —
is the last moment BMS intervention can prevent full thermal runaway. Catching
dT/dt above zero-point-five degrees per second while the cell is still below
ninety degrees means Stage One is just starting. Disconnect immediately.
Miss this window and you're in Stage Two.

BLAKE: Number four.

ALEX: LFP's olivine crystal holds oxygen with a five-hundred-and-forty-four
kilojoule-per-mole bond. Even at two-hundred-and-seventy degrees, those bonds
don't break. No oxygen release means no self-feeding fire. This is the physical
property that makes LFP the right chemistry for indoor installations.

BLAKE: Number five.

ALEX: Every ten degrees above the optimal range doubles the aging rate. The
economic case for thermal management is overwhelming — reducing average operating
temperature by just five degrees extends calendar life by about forty percent.
Over ten years and multiple pack replacement cycles, this is tens of thousands
of dollars.

BLAKE: Number six.

ALEX: Temperature gradients cause differential aging. Hot cells age two to four
times faster than cool cells. The cell balancer cannot compensate — it can only
balance state of charge, not capacity. Keep the temperature spread below five
degrees across all cells.

BLAKE: Number seven.

ALEX: Charge current must be zero below zero degrees Celsius. No exceptions.
Below freezing, lithium deposits as metallic spikes rather than inserting into
graphite. Those spikes grow into dendrites that can puncture the separator
weeks later. The cause-and-effect chain is not immediate — which makes it even
more dangerous to ignore.

BLAKE: Number eight.

ALEX: LFP prismatic cells expand about zero-point-nine millimetres per cycle.
Pack fixtures must include spring compliance to maintain controlled compression.
Irreversible swelling — cells that don't return to baseline thickness — indicates
gas generation and approaching end of life. A strain gauge in the compression
fixture gives you this information in real time.

BLAKE: Number nine.

ALEX: dT/dt — the rate of temperature rise — is a more powerful thermal runaway
signal than absolute temperature. A cell heating at two degrees per second without
a matching current increase is experiencing an internal exothermic reaction.
This requires ten-hertz sampling minimum and a filtered derivative in firmware.

BLAKE: Number ten.

ALEX: Sensor voting logic is essential. Two-of-N voting for warning-level events
protects against single sensor failures. One-of-N voting for thermal runaway
detection — the cost of a missed runaway is always greater than the cost of
a brief false alarm.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
QUICK REFERENCE — KEY NUMBERS TO REMEMBER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: Rapid-fire reference. These numbers belong in your memory if you're
working with LFP BMS.

ALEX: SEI decomposition onset — ninety to a hundred-and-twenty degrees Celsius.

BLAKE: Separator melt point.

ALEX: Forty-five degrees warning — derate starts. Fifty-five degrees hard cutoff.

BLAKE: Undertemperature charge cutoff.

ALEX: Zero degrees. Absolute. No charge. Not even a trickle.

BLAKE: Cell expansion per cycle for two-eighty amp-hour LFP.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLAKE: That's Chapter Three complete. And this chapter changed how I think

ALEX: How so?

BLAKE: Before this I thought of a battery as a black box. Electricity in,

It has a knife-edge between normal and catastrophic — and the BMS is the
