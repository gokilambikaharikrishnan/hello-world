Part 1 — Meet the Chip
01
What is the L9963E? The Big Picture
🧒 In plain English

Imagine a school nurse whose only job is to check every student's temperature and report back to the teacher. The L9963E is exactly that nurse — but for a battery pack. It watches up to 14 battery cells, measures their voltages and temperature, and reports everything to your microcontroller (the teacher).

The L9963E is an Analog Front-End (AFE) IC made by STMicroelectronics for automotive and industrial battery packs. At its heart, it is a battery-monitoring and balancing chip: it sits between the battery cells and your microcontroller, continuously measuring cell voltages, pack current, and temperatures, then handing all that data over via a serial interface.

Before chips like the L9963E existed, engineers had to build dozens of discrete op-amps, voltage references, analog multiplexers, and protection circuits to do the same job. The L9963E packs all of that into a single 64-pin TQFP package that is roughly 10 mm × 10 mm. One chip replaces what used to be an entire circuit board.

It is important to understand what the L9963E does not do. It does not make any decisions about charging or discharging — that is your firmware's job. It does not disconnect the battery — you need external contactors or MOSFETs for that. It does not compute State of Charge (SOC) or State of Health (SOH) — your microcontroller uses the data the chip provides to do that math. The L9963E is purely a sensor and reporter. Think of it as the eyes and ears of your Battery Management System (BMS), while your STM32 is the brain.

The chip is designed for automotive reliability: it is AEC-Q100 qualified (meaning it has passed rigorous automotive-grade testing), operates from −40°C to +105°C, and is compliant with ISO 26262, enabling ASIL-D safety systems. It can monitor anywhere from 4 to 14 cells wired in series, and multiple chips can be daisy-chained to handle up to 434 cells in a large battery system.

⚙️ Technical Detail

Supply range: VBAT = 9.6 V to 64 V (the full pack voltage). Package: TQFP 10×10 64L with exposed pad. Temperature range: −40°C to +105°C junction. Supports 4 to 14 cells in a single device, up to 31 devices in a daisy chain (434 cells total). Current consumption in Normal mode: ~5.5–9 mA from VBAT.

02
What's Inside the Chip?
🧒 In plain English

A Swiss Army knife has many tools folded inside one handle. The L9963E is the same idea — one chip with many specialist circuits inside, each doing one job really well. Let's open it up and see all the tools.

The L9963E contains several distinct functional blocks, all sharing a common digital control core. Understanding what each block does helps you understand every register and every pin — they all map back to one of these building blocks.

The Cell Voltage ADCs are the main measurement engines. There are actually two independent 16-bit ADCs inside, one handling odd-numbered cells and one handling even-numbered cells. This redundancy is key to the chip's safety architecture — you can cross-check their results. Each ADC samples the differential voltage between adjacent C-pins to measure individual cell voltages.

The Current Sense ADC (CSA) is a separate, dedicated high-precision ADC that measures the differential voltage across an external shunt resistor, giving you instantaneous current. It also feeds the on-chip Coulomb Counter, which integrates current over time to track how much charge has flowed in or out of the pack.

The GPIO block manages nine general-purpose I/O pins. Most of them can be configured as analog inputs to read NTC thermistors for temperature sensing, or as digital inputs/outputs for signaling. Two are permanently dedicated to the FAULT line.

The SPI Engine and ISO Communication Engine handle all the serial communication with the microcontroller. Which one is active depends on the state of the SPIEN pin at startup. The ISO engine uses transformer-isolated differential signaling for daisy-chain configurations.

The Balancing Drivers are fourteen internal power MOSFETs, one per cell, that can bleed charge from higher cells through an external resistor. The Power Regulators (VREG, VANA, VCOM, VTREF) convert the raw pack voltage into clean internal supplies. The Fault Logic monitors everything and can set flags, drive the FAULT line, and run the Hardware Self-Check (HWSC). Finally, a small NVM (Non-Volatile Memory) block stores 112 bits of configuration data that survive power loss.

🧠 Key Insight

Every feature of the L9963E — every register bit, every pin, every flag — belongs to one of these blocks. When you understand which block you're dealing with, the rest of the datasheet becomes much easier to navigate.

03
The Chip's 64 Pins
🧒 In plain English

Pins are like the chip's hands and ears — some listen to the battery cells, some talk to your microcontroller, some taste the voltage, some carry power. There are 64 of them, but they group neatly into just six categories.

The L9963E comes in a 64-pin TQFP package with an exposed thermal pad on the bottom. All 64 pins have specific roles, but grouping them makes them easy to remember.

Cell Input Pins (C0–C14): These are the 15 analog inputs that connect to the battery stack. C0 connects to the negative terminal of cell 1 (near battery ground), and each subsequent Cn pin connects to the positive terminal of cell n. The chip measures the differential voltage between consecutive C-pins to find each cell voltage.

Balancing Pins (S1–S14, B2_1, B4_3 ... B14_13): Each cell has an S-pin (switch output from the internal MOSFET) and shares a B-pin (the common terminal) with its neighbor. These are the outputs that drive balancing current through external resistors.

Power Pins (VBAT, VREG, VANA, VCOM, VTREF, CAP1, CAP2, NPNDRV): VBAT is the main supply from the pack. VREG, VANA, VCOM, and VTREF are regulated supply outputs — you connect decoupling capacitors here. CAP1 and CAP2 connect the bootstrap capacitor used to bring up VREG. NPNDRV drives an external NPN transistor that acts as the pass element for VREG.

Communication Pins (ISOLp/SDI, ISOLm/NCS, GPIO8/SCK, GPIO9/SDO, ISOHp, ISOHm): These handle all data exchange with your MCU. In direct SPI mode, ISOLp=SDI, ISOLm=NCS, GPIO8=SCK, GPIO9=SDO. In isolated mode, ISOHp/m connect upward in a chain and ISOLp/m connect downward.

GPIO Pins (GPIO1–GPIO9): GPIO1 is permanently FAULTH (fault input from upper chip), GPIO2 is permanently FAULTL (fault output to lower chip/MCU). GPIO3–7 are general-purpose analog/digital I/O. GPIO7 can also wake up the chip. GPIO8 and GPIO9 are locked to SPI SCK/SDO when SPIEN=1.

Current Sense Pins (ISENSEp, ISENSEm): These connect across the shunt resistor in the current path. The chip measures their differential voltage to calculate current.

Ground Pins (AGND, DGND, CGND, GNDREF, GNDEP): Multiple ground pins serve different internal circuits — analog, digital, communication, and reference grounds — plus the exposed pad (GNDEP) which must connect to the AGND plane.

Pin categories and key pin descriptions

⚠️ Watch Out

C10 through C14 have a minimum absolute voltage of 3 V above AGND (not 0 V like the lower cells). This is because the internal circuitry for the upper cells requires a minimum common-mode voltage. If your pack has fewer than 10 cells, make sure the cells you do use are connected to the correct C-pins starting from C0.

04
Power Supply: How the Chip Feeds Itself
🧒 In plain English

VBAT is the power plant — it takes raw battery voltage. VREG is the city grid — it creates a stable intermediate voltage. VANA, VCOM, and VTREF are the neighborhoods — each gets a clean, regulated supply for their specific job. Nothing turns on until the step above it is ready.

The L9963E is powered directly from the battery pack via the VBAT pin. This can be anywhere from 9.6 V to 64 V — the full pack voltage. Since the chip's internal logic runs at much lower voltages, it needs an internal power supply chain to step that down safely and cleanly.

The first stage is VREG, the main internal regulator. VREG is special because it uses a bootstrap startup circuit. During the very first power-up, there is no VREG yet, so an external bootstrap capacitor connected between CAP1 and CAP2 provides a temporary charge that kicks VREG into life. Once VREG is running, it provides a regulated ~6.5 V rail. An external NPN transistor is used as the series pass element — the chip controls its base via the NPNDRV pin. This external transistor is needed to handle the heat dissipation from dropping 64 V down to 6.5 V at the required current.

VANA is derived from VREG and provides a precise, low-noise supply specifically for the ADC analog circuits. Noise on VANA directly corrupts ADC measurements, so it gets its own dedicated regulator and filtering. VTREF is a buffered precision reference voltage (~5 V) derived from VREG, used to bias the external NTC voltage dividers for temperature measurement. It can supply up to 50 mA to external circuits. VCOM is another ~5 V rail from VREG, dedicated to powering the communication interfaces (SPI and ISO) and their external loads, up to 25 mA.

In low-power modes (Sleep, Silent Balancing), VREG is turned off, which also kills VANA, VTREF, and VCOM. This is why ADC measurements, GPIO readings, and SPI communication all stop during sleep — their power is gone.

Internal power supply tree: VBAT → VREG → VANA / VCOM / VTREF

🔧 For your firmware

You don't control these regulators directly in firmware, but you need to understand them for hardware design. Each of VREG, VANA, VCOM, and VTREF requires an external decoupling capacitor (values specified in the datasheet Application Table). Skipping or undersizing these capacitors causes regulator instability, ADC noise, and erratic behavior — even if everything else looks right.

05
The Chip's Power-Up Sequence
🧒 In plain English

Just like a computer that must boot the BIOS before the OS before your apps, the L9963E has a strict turn-on order. Each stage must be ready before the next can start. If you send SPI commands before the chip is ready, it simply ignores you — or worse, behaves unpredictably.

When power is first applied to VBAT, the L9963E does not instantly become ready for communication. It goes through an internal startup sequence that takes a minimum of about 2 ms, called T_WAKEUP. During this window, the internal regulators come online one by one, the oscillators stabilize, and the digital core initializes.

The sequence is: VBAT rises → bootstrap circuit charges CAP1/CAP2 → VREG turns on → VANA powers up → VTREF becomes available → VCOM powers up → digital logic initializes → the chip enters either RESET or INIT state. Until VCOM is stable, the SPIEN pin is not even sampled — which means if you try to communicate before VCOM is ready, the chip doesn't even know if it should use SPI or ISO mode yet.

After VCOM is up and SPIEN is sampled, if you are in direct SPI mode (SPIEN=1), you still need to send at least 37 SPI clock pulses with NCS high to wake the chip from its startup state. This is called the "wakeup clock" sequence. After that, you wait another T_WAKEUP period before sending any real SPI command frame.

Once the startup sequence completes, the chip enters INIT state, during which only the device address assignment is possible. You have up to 60 seconds (t_SHUT) to assign the chip's ID before it gives up and goes back to SLEEP. Once the ID is assigned, the chip transitions to NORMAL state and all registers become accessible.

Power-up sequence: VBAT rise → T_WAKEUP → wakeup clocks → INIT state

🔧 For your firmware

Your startup code must always include: (1) a delay of at least 2 ms after VBAT stabilizes, (2) send 37+ SPI clocks with NCS high (a dummy read works), (3) another 2 ms delay, then (4) begin normal SPI communication. Skipping step 2 or 3 will cause the chip to ignore your first real command.

06
The 5 Operating States
🧒 In plain English

The chip is like a light switch with five positions, not just on/off. Each position turns different things on and off to trade between functionality and power consumption. You control which state it's in, mostly through SPI commands.

The L9963E has five distinct operating states, each with different capabilities and current draw. Understanding the state machine is essential because certain operations are only possible in certain states.

RESET: The chip has just powered on or received a reset command. All internal registers return to their default (NVM) values. The chip immediately transitions out of RESET automatically — you never "stay" in RESET.

INIT: The first real state after power-on. Only the device address (chip_ID) and a handful of communication configuration bits can be written here. The chip waits for your MCU to assign it a unique ID. If no ID is assigned within 60 seconds (t_SHUT timeout), the chip goes back to SLEEP. Once the ID is assigned, the chip automatically transitions to NORMAL.

NORMAL: Full operation. All registers are readable and writable. ADCs run, SPI is active, balancing can operate. Current draw is the highest here: approximately 5.5–9 mA from VBAT depending on configuration and measurement activity. This is the state you will spend most of your time in.

SLEEP: Ultra-low power mode. VREG is off, which means VANA, VCOM, and VTREF are all off. Only the standby oscillator and minimal logic stay alive to watch for wakeup triggers. Current draw drops to approximately 10–50 µA. The chip can be woken by a SPI activity, GPIO7 wakeup signal, or the cyclic wakeup timer. Balancing stops unless you transition to Silent Balancing instead.

SILENT BALANCING: A special hybrid state where the chip is mostly asleep (VREG off, no SPI) but the balancing predriver circuits remain active. If balancing was running when you sent the sleep command and the slp_bal_conf bit was set, the chip enters this state instead of full sleep. Current draw is higher than full sleep (approximately 1.2–2.8 mA) because the cells are actively being drained through the balance resistors.

CYCLIC WAKEUP: Not exactly a standalone state but a mode in which the chip periodically wakes from SLEEP, runs a quick diagnostic or measurement, then goes back to SLEEP. The cycle period is programmable from 100 ms to 12.8 seconds. If a fault is detected during a cyclic wakeup, the chip stays awake and enters NORMAL.

State machine: RESET → INIT → NORMAL ↔ SLEEP / SILENT BAL / CYCLIC

⚙️ Technical Detail

State transitions triggered by SPI commands: Go_to_sleep command moves NORMAL → SLEEP (or SILENT BALANCING). Wakeup command or GPIO7 wakeup moves SLEEP → NORMAL. t_SHUT = 60 s (max) in INIT before auto-sleep. Cyclic period: programmable from 100 ms to 12.8 s via TCYCLE_SLEEP register.

Part 2 — SPI Communication
07
How the Chip Communicates: Two Options
🧒 In plain English

The L9963E is like a walkie-talkie that can work two ways: direct radio (SPI) when you're close, or relay station (ISO) when you're far away in a chain. Which mode it uses is decided once at power-on and never changes until reset.

The L9963E supports two fundamentally different communication physical layers, selected by the state of the SPIEN pin at the moment VCOM first powers up.

Direct SPI mode (SPIEN = 1, tied to VCOM): The chip uses a standard 4-wire SPI interface directly connected to your microcontroller. The pins are ISOLp (acting as SDI/MOSI), ISOLm (acting as NCS/chip select), GPIO8 (acting as SCK), and GPIO9 (acting as SDO/MISO). This is the simpler option and the right choice for a single L9963E connected directly to an STM32. All four pins are locked to their SPI functions — they cannot be used for anything else.

Isolated SPI mode (SPIEN = 0 or floating): The chip uses a transformer- or capacitor-isolated differential serial interface on the ISOHp/ISOHm (high-side port) and ISOLp/ISOLm (low-side port) pins. This mode enables daisy-chaining multiple L9963E devices across different ground potentials — essential for multi-module battery systems where each module floats at a different voltage. The chain communicates through the L9963T transceiver IC which handles the isolation between the MCU and the first L9963E.

The critical thing to remember: SPIEN is latched at first power-up and cannot be changed by software. If your board routes SPIEN to VCOM, the chip will always be in direct SPI mode. If it is tied low or floating, it will always be in ISO mode. You cannot change this dynamically. This choice is a hardware design decision, not a firmware decision.

For a single-chip setup with an STM32H743ZI (like a typical evaluation setup), SPIEN=1 (direct SPI) is the natural choice. It is simpler, faster to debug, and perfectly adequate when you do not need daisy-chaining.

Left: direct SPI (single chip). Right: isolated daisy chain (multi-chip).

⚠️ Watch Out

If SPIEN is left floating, it has an internal 100 kΩ pull-down, defaulting to ISO mode. If your hardware is wired for direct SPI but SPIEN is accidentally floating, the chip will be in ISO mode and will appear completely unresponsive to SPI commands. Always explicitly tie SPIEN to VCOM for SPI mode.

08
SPI Pins and Configuration
🧒 In plain English

Think of SPI as a conversation where both sides talk at exactly the same time, perfectly in sync with a shared clock. There are specific rules about timing: how fast the clock can go, how long the chip needs to "breathe" between commands, and which way the clock idles. Get these wrong and every message is garbled.

In direct SPI mode (SPIEN=1), the L9963E uses four specific pins for its SPI interface. The pin names on the chip are shared with their ISO counterparts, so it is important to remember the mapping: ISOLp/SDI = MOSI (data into chip), ISOLm/NCS = chip select (active low), GPIO8/SCK = serial clock, GPIO9/SDO = MISO (data out of chip).

The SPI mode is CPOL=0, CPHA=0 (Mode 0). This means the clock idles low and data is sampled on the rising edge. This is the most common SPI mode. Maximum clock frequency is 5 MHz. Going faster than this can cause bit errors — the chip simply cannot keep up.

Timing constraints are strict and must be met at the hardware level. The minimum NCS high time between transactions is 300 ns — you must not immediately pull NCS low again after releasing it. The minimum clock low time is 75 ns and minimum clock high time is also 75 ns (matching the 5 MHz max). NCS must be held low for the entire 40-bit transaction — if you release it in the middle of a frame, the chip discards the partial frame.

Every transaction is exactly 40 bits — no more, no less. There is no variable-length SPI here. You clock out exactly 40 bits on MOSI while simultaneously clocking in 40 bits on MISO. After the 40th bit, you release NCS, wait at least 300 ns, and the transaction is complete. The chip processes the command internally and prepares its response for the next transaction.

SPI timing: NCS active low, 40-bit frame, CPOL=0 CPHA=0, max 5 MHz

⚙️ Technical Detail

SPI mode: CPOL=0, CPHA=0. Max FSCK = 5 MHz. Min tNCS_HIGH = 300 ns. Min tCLK_LOW = tCLK_HIGH = 75 ns. Frame length: exactly 40 bits per transaction. NCS must remain low for all 40 bits. STM32 HAL SPI config: SPI_MODE_MASTER, SPI_DATASIZE_8BIT × 5 transfers, SPI_POLARITY_LOW, SPI_PHASE_1EDGE.

09
The 40-Bit SPI Frame: Master to Chip (MOSI)
🧒 In plain English

Every message you send to the L9963E is like a standardized form: who it's for, what you want to do, and what data to write — all packed into exactly 40 bits. Fill it out wrong and the chip ignores the whole form.

Every single transaction from your MCU to the L9963E uses exactly the same 40-bit MOSI frame format. There are no exceptions. Understanding this frame is the most important technical thing you will learn about this chip, because every register access — read or write — goes through it.

The frame is organized from bit 39 (MSB, sent first) to bit 0 (LSB, sent last). Starting from the top: bit 39 is the P.A bit — "Physical Address" indicator. For frames from the MCU to the chip, this is always 1. Bit 38 is R/W: set to 1 for a write, 0 for a read. Bits 37–33 are the DevID (5 bits): which L9963E in the chain you're addressing (1–31; use your assigned chip ID here; 0 is broadcast). Bits 32–26 are the Register Address (7 bits): which of the chip's internal registers you want to access (register map goes from 0x00 to 0x5F).

Bits 25–24 are the GSW Echo (2 bits): the MCU echoes back the Global Status Word bits it received in the previous MISO frame. This lets the chip know the MCU understood the last status. Bits 23–6 are DATA_WRITE (18 bits): the value you want to write into the register. For a read command, these 18 bits are still transmitted but are ignored by the chip — send zeros or anything. Bits 5–0 are the CRC (6 bits): a checksum computed over bits [39:7]. If the CRC doesn't match what the chip calculates, the entire frame is silently discarded.

That's the complete MOSI frame: P.A(1) + R/W(1) + DevID(5) + Addr(7) + GSW_echo(2) + DATA(18) + CRC(6) = 40 bits exactly.

40-bit MOSI frame layout: P.A(1) + R/W(1) + DevID(5) + Addr(7) + GSW(2) + Data(18) + CRC(6)

🔧 For your firmware

Build a helper function: uint64_t build_mosi_frame(uint8_t rw, uint8_t dev_id, uint8_t addr, uint32_t data, uint8_t gsw_echo). Pack the bits in the right order, then compute and append the 6-bit CRC. Transfer as 5 bytes MSB-first. Always zero-pad data to 18 bits.

10
The 40-Bit SPI Frame: Chip to MCU (MISO)
🧒 In plain English

Imagine asking a librarian for a book. She hears your request, walks to the shelf, and hands you a book — but the book she's handing you right now is the one you asked for LAST time, not this time. The L9963E works exactly the same way: you always get the answer to your previous question.

While you clock out your 40-bit MOSI command, the L9963E simultaneously clocks in its MISO response. But here is the critical insight that trips up almost every engineer new to this chip: the MISO data you receive during transaction N is the response to transaction N-1. This is called the "out-of-frame" protocol.

The MISO frame is also 40 bits and has a specific layout. Bit 39 is P.A — but here it is 0, meaning "this is a response from the chip, not a command." Bit 38 is Burst — indicates if this is part of a burst read sequence. Bits 37–33 are DevID — which chip is responding. Bits 32–26 are Address_feedback — the register address of the command being answered (so you can verify you got the right response). Bits 25–24 are GSW — the current Global Status Word (2-bit health summary — more on this in Chapter 14). Bits 23–6 are DATA_READ — the 18-bit register value you asked for. Bits 5–0 are CRC — checksum of the MISO frame that you should verify.

This out-of-frame timing means that to read one register, you actually need two SPI transactions: the first sends the read command (and you receive old/dummy MISO data), and the second sends any dummy command (you can send another read for the next register, or just a dummy frame) — and during that second transaction, you receive the answer to your read command in the MISO stream.

For a single-chip setup, the "dummy" second frame can simply be another useful command — so you can pipeline your transactions: send command N while receiving response to command N-1.

40-bit MISO frame: P.A=0, Burst, DevID(5), Addr_feedback(7), GSW(2), DATA_READ(18), CRC(6)

🧠 Key Insight

The out-of-frame protocol is not a quirk — it is a deliberate design for daisy-chain latency. The chip needs time to process your command and fetch the register value. By returning the answer in the next frame, it guarantees there is always enough time, even when your command had to travel through 30 chips before reaching its target.

11
Why Out-of-Frame? The Propagation Delay Reason
🧒 In plain English

Imagine a game of telephone with 31 people in a line. You whisper a question to person 1, it travels all the way to person 31, who whispers the answer back. By the time it returns, you've already moved on to the next question. The out-of-frame protocol is designed exactly for this relay race.

In a single-chip setup, the out-of-frame protocol might seem like unnecessary complexity. But it exists for a very good reason: daisy-chain latency. When you have 31 L9963E devices chained together, a command you send to device #31 must travel through devices #1 through #30 before reaching its destination. Each chip in the chain receives, decodes, and re-transmits the frame — adding a small propagation delay at each hop.

By the time device #31 has received your full 40-bit command and prepared its answer, you are already clocking out the next SPI frame from the MCU. The out-of-frame design elegantly handles this: the answer always comes back one frame later, regardless of how many chips are in the chain. This keeps the protocol timing consistent and predictable.

For a single-chip setup, this means you simply send a "dummy" second frame to retrieve the answer to your first command. The dummy frame can be anything — a null read, a status check, whatever is convenient. Many firmware drivers pipeline commands: send command N+1 while harvesting the response to command N from the MISO stream. This is efficient and keeps the SPI bus busy with useful work.

There is one practical implication: you should never assume the first MISO frame after power-on contains valid data. The very first transaction returns whatever the chip's MISO output was initialized to (typically 0). Always send at least one dummy frame first and discard that MISO data.

Two-transaction sequence to read one register. In chains, the out-of-frame design hides propagation latency.

🔧 For your firmware

Pattern for a clean register read: (1) spi_transfer(read_frame) — discard MISO. (2) spi_transfer(dummy_frame) — parse MISO, check Addr_feedback matches step 1's address, check CRC, extract DATA_READ. For pipelining: combine step 2 of the previous read with step 1 of the next read into one transfer.

12
Reading and Writing Registers: Step by Step
🧒 In plain English

Writing a register is like dropping a letter in a mailbox — you write the address, put in the content, seal it with a CRC stamp, and drop it. Reading a register is like sending a request letter and then collecting the reply in your next trip to the mailbox.

Now that you understand the frame format and the out-of-frame protocol, let's put them together into the exact sequence for reading and writing registers.

To write a register: Build a MOSI frame with R/W=1, the target DevID, the register address, and the 18-bit data value you want to write. Compute and append the 6-bit CRC. Perform the SPI transfer. The MISO data during this first frame is the response to whatever command came before — inspect it if useful, but it does not contain the write acknowledgment. To confirm the write was accepted, send any second frame — typically a read of the same register — and inspect the MISO of that second frame, which will contain the chip's acknowledgment of your write (Address_feedback will match, and DATA_READ will show the new register value if it was a readback).

To read a register: Build a MOSI frame with R/W=0, the DevID, the register address. DATA_WRITE bits are ignored — you can set them to zero. Compute and append CRC. Perform the first SPI transfer. Now send a second frame (dummy or another command). During the second transfer, MISO carries the response to your read: check that Addr_feedback matches your requested address, extract the 18-bit DATA_READ value, verify the CRC.

A good firmware driver always verifies the CRC of the received MISO frame and checks Addr_feedback to ensure the response corresponds to the expected command. It also checks the 2-bit GSW field on every received frame — if GSW is non-zero, there is a fault condition that needs attention regardless of the register data.

Write + read-back pattern: 3 frames total. Frame 2 ACKs the write; Frame 3 returns read data.

⚙️ Technical Detail

Register map width: 18 bits per register. Addresses: 0x00–0x5F. Read-only registers: writing to them is silently ignored. Some registers have write-protect bits that must be cleared before writing. After a reset, all registers return to NVM defaults. Registers are accessible only in NORMAL state (except chip_ID which is INIT-only).

Burst Read: Reading Many Registers Fast
🧒 In plain English

Normally, each question and answer requires separate trips to the mailbox. Burst read is like asking "just give me all 20 pieces of mail at once" — the chip hands you a stack without you needing to request each one individually.

A normal register read requires two SPI transactions per register (one to request, one to receive). To read all 14 cell voltages individually, you would need 28+ transactions. Burst read solves this by allowing you to trigger a single command that causes the chip to stream back a pre-defined set of registers automatically.

There are three burst commands, each returning a specific set of 20 registers in sequence. Burst 0x78 is the most useful for day-to-day operation: it returns all 14 cell voltages, the stack voltage sum, the VBAT divider measurement, the instantaneous current, and the balancing status. This is the command you'll use in your main measurement loop. Burst 0x7A returns all fault and diagnostic registers — useful when GSW indicates a problem. Burst 0x7B returns the Coulomb counter value and GPIO measurements.

To start a burst, you send a single 40-bit frame using the burst address (e.g., 0x78) in the Addr field and R/W=0. Then you wait: for ISO_FAST mode, wait at least 400 µs; for ISO_SLOW mode, wait at least 3 ms. After the wait, you clock out 20 more dummy SPI frames, collecting the MISO data on each one. Each MISO frame contains one register's data, with Addr_feedback telling you which register it is.

Burst read is by far the most efficient way to gather cell voltages and is the recommended approach for any BMS that needs to update measurements faster than once per second.

Burst 0x78: 1 trigger frame + 400µs wait + 20 read frames = all cell data in one shot

🔧 For your firmware

In your measurement loop: (1) trigger SOC (start conversion), (2) wait for DUTY_ON=0, (3) send burst 0x78 frame, (4) wait 400 µs, (5) clock out 20 dummy frames collecting MISO, (6) parse each frame using Addr_feedback to identify which cell's data it carries. Total SPI time for 20 frames at 5 MHz ≈ 160 µs.

The Global Status Word (GSW)
🧒 In plain English

On every single response the chip sends you, there are two bits that act like a traffic light: green means all good, yellow means take a look, red means something is wrong, flashing red means emergency. These two bits are the Global Status Word, and you should read them on every single SPI transaction.

The Global Status Word is a 2-bit field embedded in every single MISO frame at bits [25:24]. It provides an instant, at-a-glance health summary of the entire chip without requiring you to read any fault registers. Think of it as a consolidated alarm indicator.

The four possible values are: 00 = all OK, no issues detected; 01 = warning level event (non-critical, check fault registers when convenient); 10 = fault condition (action required, check fault registers soon); 11 = critical fault (immediate attention required, pack safety may be at risk). Each of these levels has specific fault types that trigger it — critical faults include things like cell OV/UV, ADC failure, or communication errors, while warnings might be near-threshold conditions or non-safety diagnostics.

The GSW is set by the chip's fault logic and cleared automatically when the underlying fault condition is resolved and the fault latches are reset by firmware. The key insight is that you do not need to read dedicated fault registers on every SPI transaction — you just check the 2-bit GSW. Only when GSW is non-zero do you need to spend the extra SPI transactions reading the detailed fault registers (using burst 0x7A) to find out what went wrong.

In your main loop, a good pattern is: execute measurement → read burst 0x78 → check GSW on every MISO frame. If any frame shows GSW ≠ 00, trigger your fault-handling routine. This keeps the normal path fast while catching faults immediately.

⚙️ Technical Detail

GSW field: bits [25:24] of every MISO frame. Values: 2'b00=OK, 2'b01=warning, 2'b10=fault, 2'b11=critical. The MCU echoes the last received GSW back in the next MOSI frame (bits [25:24] of MOSI = GSW_echo). This echo mechanism lets the chip track whether the MCU has seen the latest status. GSW is a live summary — it reflects the current state of all fault latches.

🔧 For your firmware

After every spi_transfer(): extract bits [25:24] from the 40-bit MISO value. If non-zero, set a fault_pending flag. Store the last seen GSW and echo it in the next MOSI frame. When fault_pending is set, queue a burst 0x7A read to get the full fault details.

CRC: Making Sure Data Isn't Corrupted
🧒 In plain English

CRC is like a fingerprint of the message. Before sending, you compute the fingerprint and attach it. When received, you compute the fingerprint again from the data — if it matches what was attached, the message is intact. If it doesn't match, something got corrupted in transit.

Every SPI frame — both MOSI and MISO — ends with a 6-bit CRC field. This is not optional: the L9963E checks the CRC on every incoming MOSI frame and will silently discard any frame whose CRC is wrong. It will not send an error back to you. It simply ignores the entire frame as if you never sent it. This "silent ignore" behavior means if your CRC calculation is buggy, your register writes will appear to succeed (no error) but actually do nothing — a maddening failure mode.

The CRC is computed over bits [39:7] of the frame — everything except the 6-bit CRC field itself (bits [5:0]). The polynomial used is x⁶ + x⁴ + x² + x¹ + 1, which in binary is 1010011 or hex 0x43. The initial value (seed) is 0b111111 (all ones). The algorithm processes bits from MSB to LSB of bits [39:7], XOR-ing with the generator polynomial when the topmost bit is 1.

On the MISO side, you should also verify the 6-bit CRC of every received frame. If it fails, the frame should be discarded and the transaction retried. CRC failures on MISO indicate electrical noise, EMI issues, or PCB layout problems — and in an automotive battery system, these matter.

In practice, you implement the CRC in firmware as a lookup table or a simple bit-by-bit loop. A 64-entry lookup table (6-bit CRC) is fast and small. Many open-source L9963E drivers include a pre-computed CRC table — use one, but verify it against the polynomial above.

⚙️ Technical Detail

CRC-6, polynomial: x⁶ + x⁴ + x² + x + 1 = 0x43. Seed: 0x3F (all ones). Input: bits[39:7] of frame (33 bits). Output: 6-bit CRC placed in bits[5:0]. L9963E rejects MOSI frames with wrong CRC silently — no error flag, no response. MISO CRC uses the same polynomial over the MISO bits[39:7].

🔧 For your firmware

Implement a uint8_t compute_crc6(uint64_t frame_bits_39_to_7) function. Start with crc = 0x3F. For each bit from bit 39 down to bit 7: if ((crc >> 5) ^ (bit)) & 1 is true, crc = (crc << 1) ^ 0x43; else crc = crc << 1. Mask to 6 bits. Test against: frame 0x8041800000 should produce CRC 0x3A.

Device Addressing in a Daisy Chain
🧒 In plain English

Imagine 31 employees sharing a single phone line with no name badges yet. Your first job is to assign each one a unique number so you can call them individually. L9963E addressing works exactly this way — unassigned chips all start with ID=0, and you hand out IDs one by one.

When multiple L9963E chips are connected in a daisy chain, each one needs a unique Device ID (DevID) so the MCU can address them individually. At power-on, all chips have DevID=0 (unassigned). The ID assignment process happens during the INIT state and uses a clever sequential scheme.

The assignment algorithm works from the farthest device back toward the MCU. You send a broadcast WRITE command (DevID=0 in the MOSI frame) to assign ID to the device currently listening on the INIT-address channel. The farthest device in the chain (let's say there are 5 chips: #5 is farthest) responds to the first assignment command and takes ID=5. It then locks its ID and stops responding to DevID=0 commands. Now the second-farthest chip (#4) is the next to respond to a DevID=0 command, and so on, until all chips have IDs.

Once an ID is assigned via the chip_ID register write during INIT, it is locked and only a reset can clear it. Thereafter, you address individual chips using their specific DevID (1–31) in every MOSI frame, and only the chip with that ID will respond. To send a command to all chips simultaneously (for example, a global sleep command), use the broadcast address DevID=0.

For a single-chip setup, this is much simpler: you just assign DevID=1 to your one chip during INIT, and from then on use DevID=1 for all communications.

ID assignment: broadcast from farthest to nearest. Broadcast addr DevID=0 reaches all; DevID=1–31 targets specific chips.

Init State: The Address Assignment Window
🧒 In plain English

After waking up, the chip opens a 60-second window and waits for you to tell it its name (its ID). If you don't show up in time, it gives up, goes back to sleep, and you have to start the whole wake-up process over. Don't miss the window.

After every wakeup — whether from power-on, a wakeup command, or a cyclic wakeup — the L9963E enters INIT state. This is a restricted operating mode with a specific purpose: allowing the MCU to assign the chip's device address.

During INIT state, only three fields are writable: chip_ID (the 5-bit device address), isotx_en_h (enable the ISO high-side transmitter), and iso_freq_sel (select ISO communication speed). All other registers are read-only and return their NVM default values. Attempting to write any other register during INIT is silently ignored.

The INIT window is time-limited by t_SHUT, which has a maximum of 60 seconds. If the MCU does not write a valid non-zero chip_ID within this window, the chip automatically returns to SLEEP state. You then need to issue another wakeup sequence to try again.

Once a valid chip_ID (1–31) is written, the chip immediately and automatically transitions from INIT to NORMAL state. In NORMAL state, all registers become accessible, ADC conversions can be started, balancing can be enabled, and the full SPI protocol is active. The chip_ID is locked from this point until the next reset.

For firmware reliability, always perform the INIT step within the first few hundred milliseconds of wakeup, well within the 60-second window. A good practice is to include a check: after writing chip_ID, verify by reading it back. If the read-back matches, you're in NORMAL state and can proceed with full initialization.

⚙️ Technical Detail

INIT state register access: only chip_ID [bits 4:0], isotx_en_h [bit], and iso_freq_sel [bit] are writable. All others: read-only, return NVM defaults. t_SHUT timeout: max 60 s. Transition to NORMAL: automatic after valid chip_ID write (chip_ID 1–31). Transition back to SLEEP: on t_SHUT expiry without valid ID write.

⚠️ Watch Out

If your firmware crashes or stalls between wakeup and chip_ID assignment, the chip will silently go back to SLEEP after 60 seconds. Add a watchdog timer in your firmware's initialization sequence, and always verify the chip_ID read-back before proceeding to full configuration.

ISO (Isolated) SPI: The Daisy Chain System
🧒 In plain English

Imagine 14-story building where each floor is at a different electrical potential. You can't run a normal wire between floors because of the voltage difference. The ISO interface uses magnetic pulses through transformers — like sending messages by drum beat — that cross the voltage barrier safely.

In a multi-module battery system, each L9963E module is physically connected to a different section of the battery pack, which means each chip's ground (AGND) is at a different voltage — potentially hundreds of volts apart. A direct SPI bus cannot cross this voltage barrier. The ISO interface solves this by using transformer- or capacitor-isolated differential signaling.

Each L9963E has two ISO ports: the high-side port (ISOHp and ISOHm) connects upward toward the MCU, and the low-side port (ISOLp and ISOLm) connects downward toward the next chip in the chain. Between each pair of chips sits an isolation barrier — either a small transformer (XFMR) or a coupling capacitor (CAP). The signal is differential, which means one pin carries the signal and the other carries its inverse, giving excellent noise immunity.

The ISO interface has two speed modes: FISO_SLOW at 333 kbps (default, more robust, better range) and FISO_FAST at 2.66 Mbps (high speed for fast measurement loops). The speed is selected via the iso_freq_sel bit, which must be written during INIT state before transitioning to NORMAL.

The practical benefit is remarkable: the datasheet specifies less than 4 ms to convert and read 96 cells from 8 devices, and less than 16 ms for 434 cells from 31 devices at fast speed. The chain regenerates the signal at each hop (regenerative buffer), so signal quality does not degrade with chain length.

ISO daisy chain: transformer barriers isolate each chip's ground. Signal regenerated at each hop.

Cell Voltage Pins: C0 Through C14
🧒 In plain English

Imagine a stack of 14 coins. The chip measures the thickness of each coin by placing probes between adjacent gaps. C0 is at the bottom (negative terminal), C14 is at the top (positive terminal), and each Cn pin is a probe at the interface between cell n and cell n+1.

The L9963E measures individual cell voltages using 15 analog input pins: C0 through C14. Cell 1's voltage is the difference between C1 and C0, cell 2 is C2 minus C1, and so on. This differential measurement technique is fundamental — the chip measures the voltage across each cell, not each cell's absolute voltage relative to ground.

C0 connects to the negative terminal of the bottom cell in the stack, which is near the battery's AGND (ground). C14 connects to the positive terminal of the top cell, which is near VBAT. For a 14-series pack, VBAT − C14 should be very small (essentially 0 V minus wire losses). For packs with fewer than 14 cells, connect the unused upper C-pins to the highest C-pin actually used.

Each C-pin connection on the PCB requires a low-pass filter to remove switching noise from the battery before it reaches the ADC. The recommended filter uses a 1 kΩ resistor (RLPF) in series with the C-pin trace, and a 10 nF capacitor (CLPF) from the pin to AGND. This creates an RC low-pass filter with a cutoff around 16 kHz, sufficient to filter out switching noise from inverters and chargers.

An important constraint: C10 through C14 have a minimum absolute voltage of 3 V with respect to AGND. This is because the internal measurement circuitry for the upper cells needs a minimum common-mode voltage to function. The lower cells (C1–C9) can be as low as 0 V absolute. This is not an issue in a fully-charged 10+ cell pack, but matters in edge cases.

C-pins connect at cell boundaries. RLPF (1 kΩ) + CLPF (10 nF) filter on each. Differential measurement.

The Cell Voltage ADC
🧒 In plain English

The ADC is like a very precise ruler. It looks at the voltage across a cell and tells you the measurement in tiny steps — each step is about 89 microvolts. A cell at 3.3 V returns a number that, when multiplied by 89 µV, gives you back 3.3 V.

The L9963E contains two independent 16-bit sigma-delta ADCs dedicated to cell voltage measurement. Having two independent ADCs is both an efficiency feature (they can measure simultaneously) and a safety feature (they provide redundant measurements for cross-checking).

The two ADCs are organized by cell parity: the odd-number ADC handles cells 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 and the even-number ADC handles cells 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14. Both ADCs run simultaneously during a conversion cycle, so all 14 cells are measured in parallel rather than sequentially. This is why the measurement time is independent of the number of cells — the conversion time is set by TCYCLEADC, not by the cell count.

The specified accuracy is ±2 mV maximum error across the voltage range of 0.5 V to 4.3 V, after PCB soldering, over the full automotive temperature range of −40°C to +105°C. For LFP (lithium iron phosphate) cells operating between 2.5 V and 3.65 V, this puts worst-case error at ±0.06% — excellent for SOC estimation. The LSB resolution is approximately 89 µV (16-bit range over ~5.8 V full scale).

The voltage conversion formula is simply: V_cell (in millivolts) = register_value × 0.089 mV. Or equivalently: V_cell (in volts) = register_value × 89 × 10⁻⁶. Register values are unsigned 16-bit codes; a zero code means 0 V (cell below measurement range) and the maximum code means approximately 5.8 V.

⚙️ Technical Detail

ADC type: 16-bit sigma-delta. Accuracy: ±2 mV max in [0.5–4.3] V, after soldering, −40 to +105°C. Resolution: ~89 µV/LSB. Two independent ADCs: odd cells (ADC_ODD), even cells (ADC_EVEN). Formula: V_cell = code × 89 µV. Measurement range: 0 V to ~5.8 V. Simultaneous measurement of all enabled cells in one conversion cycle.

Enabling Individual Cells
🧒 In plain English

Not every board uses all 14 channels. Telling the chip which cells are actually connected is like telling a teacher how many students are in the class — the teacher won't call on students who aren't there.

The L9963E supports between 4 and 14 cells, and you must explicitly tell it which cells are connected. This is done through the VCELL_EN register, which contains individual enable bits for each cell channel: VCELL1_EN through VCELL14_EN.

When a cell is disabled (its enable bit is 0), the chip skips that channel entirely during voltage conversions. The corresponding VCell register will not be updated, the data-ready bit (d_rdy_VcellN) will not be set, and the channel is excluded from diagnostics like open-wire detection and OV/UV threshold checking. This means you must match the enabled cells exactly to your physical cell count — enabling more cells than you have connected will cause false open-wire faults; enabling fewer will cause you to miss real measurements.

For a 14-series pack, you set all 14 bits high: VCELL1_EN through VCELL14_EN = 1. For a 10-cell pack, you set VCELL1_EN through VCELL10_EN = 1 and leave VCELL11_EN through VCELL14_EN = 0. Also make sure the physical C-pins for disabled cells are connected to the highest enabled C-pin (e.g., for 10 cells, tie C11 through C14 to C10).

This configuration must be done before starting any ADC conversion. If you start a conversion with incorrect cell enable settings, you will get wrong results that are very hard to debug.

🔧 For your firmware

In your initialization sequence, right after exiting INIT and entering NORMAL state, the first register write should be to VCELL_EN. For 14 cells: write 0x3FFF (all 14 bits set). Read it back to verify. Then write the VCELL_BAL_EN bits (balancing enable, separate register). Never start a conversion before setting cell enables.

How a Conversion is Started: SOC Bit
🧒 In plain English

SOC stands for Start Of Conversion — it's the "go" button for the ADC. You press it, the chip runs through its measurement routine, and when it finishes it sets a flag saying "done, come read your data." You must wait for the "done" flag before reading — looking at the result before it's ready gets you stale data.

To trigger a voltage measurement, you write a 1 to the SOC (Start of Conversion) bit in the ADCV_CONV register. This starts the conversion routine. The chip immediately sets the DUTY_ON flag to indicate that a conversion is in progress. While DUTY_ON is 1, the ADC is actively measuring and you should not attempt to read cell voltage registers — the values are in the middle of being updated.

Once the conversion completes, DUTY_ON drops to 0 and the chip sets individual data-ready bits for each cell: d_rdy_Vcell1 through d_rdy_Vcell14. Each d_rdy bit is set to 1 when fresh data is available for that cell, and it is automatically cleared when you read the corresponding cell register — so you always know whether the data you're about to read is fresh or previously cached.

The conversion routine includes several optional steps beyond just measuring cells. You can configure it to also measure VBAT (always included), GPIO temperatures, and run diagnostics. The optional steps are controlled by configuration bits in the conversion control registers. More active steps mean a longer conversion time (longer DUTY_ON period), so there is a tradeoff between measurement thoroughness and conversion speed.

The total conversion time depends on the ADC filter setting (TCYCLEADC) and which optional steps are enabled. For a minimal cell-only conversion with the fastest filter, you can expect the DUTY_ON period to be a few milliseconds. For a full conversion with all steps and the longest filter, it can be 50+ milliseconds.

⚙️ Technical Detail

SOC bit: in ADCV_CONV register, bit [0]. Write 1 to start; auto-clears. DUTY_ON: in status register, indicates conversion in progress. d_rdy_VcellN: per-cell data ready bits, set on fresh conversion, cleared on register read. Poll DUTY_ON until 0 before reading any cell register.

🔧 For your firmware

Pattern: (1) write SOC=1 to ADCV_CONV, (2) poll DUTY_ON flag until it reads 0 (with a timeout of ~100 ms for safety), (3) read cell voltages via burst 0x78. Never read cells while DUTY_ON=1. Set a timeout — if DUTY_ON stays high indefinitely, the chip may be in a fault state.

On-Demand vs. Cyclic Conversions
🧒 In plain English

On-demand is like asking the nurse to check temperature only when you press a button. Cyclic is like setting a timer so the nurse checks automatically every 5 minutes. Both approaches work, but cyclic is better for steady monitoring without constantly tying up the MCU.

The L9963E supports two conversion trigger modes, and you choose which one fits your application best.

On-demand mode is exactly what it sounds like: your firmware writes SOC=1 whenever it needs a fresh measurement, waits for DUTY_ON to clear, then reads the results. This gives your firmware complete control over timing and is useful for testing, calibration, or scenarios where you only need measurements occasionally. The downside is that your MCU must actively drive the measurement timing.

Cyclic mode is enabled by writing CONF_CYCLIC_EN=1 and setting the conversion period via the TCYCLE field (programmable from 100 ms to 12.8 s). In cyclic mode, the chip autonomously starts a new conversion at the end of each period — no MCU intervention needed. Your firmware simply reads the latest data at its convenience, knowing it's always fresh within one cycle period.

A related configuration bit is CYCLIC_UPDATE. When set to 1, cyclic conversion results are written into the readable cell voltage registers (so your MCU can read them). When 0, results are used only for internal threshold checking (OV/UV comparisons) but the registers are not updated — useful if you want the chip to autonomously watchdog the battery without the MCU reading individual voltages every cycle.

In cyclic mode, the chip continues conversions autonomously even during a Cyclic Wakeup state, enabling a low-power watchdog scenario where the MCU sleeps most of the time while the L9963E quietly monitors cells and only wakes the MCU if a threshold is crossed.

Cyclic mode: chip auto-starts each TCYCLE period. DUTY_ON pulses during conversion. MCU reads after.

The ADC Filter (TCYCLEADC): Speed vs. Accuracy
🧒 In plain English

The ADC filter is like how long you hold a measuring tape still before reading it. Hold it for 1 ms and a tiny vibration might throw off your reading. Hold it for 10 ms and the reading averages out any vibration. More time = more accurate, but also slower.

The TCYCLEADC field controls the integration time (effectively the filter bandwidth) of the sigma-delta ADC. A longer integration time means the ADC averages more samples, reducing noise and improving effective resolution, but it also means each conversion takes longer. A shorter time means faster conversions but potentially more noise in the reading.

There are eight settings, from TCYCLEADC_000 (shortest, approximately 1.25 ms per cell measurement) to TCYCLEADC_111 (longest, approximately 9.9 ms). The actual conversion time depends on the combination of this setting and the number of enabled steps in the conversion routine.

Two separate TCYCLEADC settings exist: ADC_FILTER_SOC applies to on-demand (SOC-triggered) conversions, and ADC_FILTER_CYCLE applies to cyclic conversions. This lets you, for example, use a fast filter for cyclic monitoring (to minimize DUTY_ON duty cycle) and a slower, more accurate filter for on-demand precision measurements.

For a typical BMS application with LFP cells, a mid-range filter setting like TCYCLEADC_010 (approximately 2.5 ms) provides a good balance. The ±2 mV accuracy specification is already achieved at the minimum filter setting — longer filters help reduce noise further, which matters for tight SOC estimation algorithms.

TCYCLEADC options. Two independent fields: ADC_FILTER_SOC (on-demand) and ADC_FILTER_CYCLE (cyclic).

Reading a Cell Voltage Register
🧒 In plain English

Each cell has its own pigeonhole in the chip's memory. After a conversion, the chip drops the new measurement into the pigeonhole. You collect it by reading the register. A little flag on the pigeonhole says "fresh mail" — that's the d_rdy bit, and it drops after you read.

Each enabled cell has a dedicated 18-bit register in the L9963E's register map: VCell1 through VCell14. The layout of each register is similar: the upper bits contain the 16-bit voltage code, and two control bits provide status information — the enable bit and the data-ready bit.

The data-ready bit (d_rdy_VcellN) is 1 when a fresh conversion result has been written to that register since the last time you read it. It is automatically cleared when you perform a register read. This lets your firmware confirm it is reading fresh data rather than a stale value from the previous conversion cycle.

The conversion formula is straightforward: to get voltage in millivolts, multiply the 16-bit code by 0.089 mV. For example, a code of 37,079 gives 37,079 × 0.089 mV = 3,300 mV = 3.300 V. For LFP cells, you can expect codes in the range of approximately 28,090 (2.5 V) to 40,990 (3.65 V).

In your firmware, after triggering an SOC conversion and waiting for DUTY_ON to clear, you have two options for reading: read each cell register individually (14 separate 2-frame SPI transactions = 28 total frames), or use the burst 0x78 command which returns all cell voltages in 20 frames. The burst approach is always preferred for normal operation.

VCell1 register: d_rdy (fresh data flag) + EN (cell enabled) + 16-bit voltage code

Stack Voltage: Sum of All Cells
🧒 In plain English

Instead of adding up 14 numbers yourself, the chip does it for you during every conversion cycle. It's like a cashier ringing up 14 items and giving you the subtotal automatically — you can check it against your own sum to catch any errors.

During every cell voltage conversion, the L9963E automatically accumulates the sum of all enabled cell voltages into a 20-bit register called Vsum_batt. This gives you the total pack voltage calculated from individual cell measurements — no MCU-side arithmetic needed.

The 20-bit width is necessary because 14 cells × maximum 5.8 V per cell = up to 81 V, which requires more bits than the 16-bit individual cell registers. The conversion formula for Vsum_batt is slightly different from individual cells; you'll find the exact scaling factor in the register map section of the datasheet, but it is essentially the sum of all individual cell codes multiplied by 89 µV.

In addition to the sum-based measurement, the chip also provides a completely independent direct measurement of the VBAT pin through an internal resistor divider. This result is stored in the VBATTDIV register. Having two independent methods to measure total pack voltage is key to the chip's ASIL-D safety architecture — you can cross-check Vsum_batt against VBATTDIV, and if they disagree by more than a threshold, something is wrong with either the individual cell measurements or the VBAT divider measurement.

For a 14-cell LFP pack at nominal charge (3.3 V per cell), you would expect Vsum_batt ≈ 14 × 3.3 V = 46.2 V. A healthy system should show Vsum_batt and VBATTDIV agreeing to within a few hundred millivolts (accounting for any resistance drops in series with the VBAT connection).

⚙️ Technical Detail

Vsum_batt: 20-bit register, auto-accumulated sum of all enabled cell ADC codes during each conversion. VBATTDIV: direct VBAT pin measurement via internal resistor divider, separate ADC path. Both updated simultaneously with cell measurements. Use for cross-check: |Vsum_batt − VBATTDIV| should be < 200 mV in a healthy system.

Burst Read for All Cell Voltages (0x78)
🧒 In plain English

Burst 0x78 is your one-stop-shop command. Instead of 28 separate SPI transactions to read 14 cells, you send one trigger and the chip queues up all the data. You then collect 20 consecutive answers. It's like ordering a combo meal instead of every item separately.

In any real BMS firmware, you'll be reading cell voltages hundreds of times per second in the main loop. The burst 0x78 command is the cornerstone of this operation, and understanding it in detail is critical for efficient firmware.

When you send the burst command, you set the address field to 0x78 in the MOSI frame with R/W=0. The chip processes this command and begins internally preparing 20 sequential register responses. You must then wait at least 400 µs (in ISO_FAST mode) or 3 ms (in ISO_SLOW mode) — this delay is mandatory because the chip needs time to assemble all the data internally before it can start streaming it back.

After the wait, you send 20 dummy MOSI frames (anything valid with correct CRC) and collect the MISO response on each one. The chip sends back the registers in a fixed order: VCell1 through VCell14, followed by Vsum_batt, VBATTDIV, instantaneous current (CUR_INST), and balancing status. The BST bit (bit 38) in the MISO frame is set to 1 for all frames that are part of a burst response, helping you track that you're still in burst mode.

The Addr_feedback field in each MISO frame tells you exactly which register that frame contains, so even if you miss-count frames, you can always recover. After the 20th response frame, the burst is complete and normal (non-burst) operation resumes.

🔧 For your firmware

Suggested burst read function: void read_all_cells(uint16_t* vcell_mv): (1) send burst 0x78 frame, (2) HAL_Delay_us(400), (3) for i in 0..19: transfer dummy frame, parse MISO — use addr_feedback to map to cell array index, multiply code by 89 µV, store in vcell_mv. Check CRC and GSW on every frame.

Synchronized Voltage and Current Measurement
🧒 In plain English

If you want to calculate power (P = V × I), you need voltage and current measured at exactly the same instant. Measuring them 5 ms apart during a dynamic load change would give you a wrong power calculation. Synchronization makes the chip take both readings simultaneously — like pressing the shutter on a camera that captures both values in a single snapshot.

Accurate power calculation and State-of-Charge estimation both require that voltage and current are measured at the same instant. The L9963E addresses this with an optional synchronization feature: when the Coulomb Counter is enabled (coulombcounter_en=1), the SOC-triggered voltage conversion is automatically held back until the next current ADC sample occurs.

In practice, this means when you write SOC=1, the chip does not immediately start the cell voltage measurement. Instead, it waits for the next current sample interval (set by TCYCLEADC_CUR) and then starts both measurements simultaneously — the cell voltage ADC and the current ADC sample at precisely the same moment. The resulting voltage and current readings are guaranteed to correspond to the exact same instant in time.

The implication for firmware is that the DUTY_ON period may be slightly longer than expected when Coulomb Counting is enabled — you must add up to one TCYCLEADC_CUR to your polling timeout when waiting for DUTY_ON to clear. Failing to account for this extra delay could cause a false timeout in your firmware.

This synchronization is transparent — you do not need to do anything special to receive it. Just ensure the Coulomb Counter is enabled before triggering conversions, and the chip handles the rest. The burst 0x78 read always returns both cell voltages and instantaneous current in the same burst, naturally delivering the synchronized pair to your firmware.

🧠 Key Insight

Synchronized sampling eliminates a subtle but significant error source: in dynamic load conditions, a 5 ms offset between V and I measurements can create a false power spike or hole in your SOC calculation. This is especially important for fast-charging LFP cells where current can change rapidly.

Part 4 — Measuring Current & Coulomb Counting

The Current Sense Input (ISENSEp / ISENSEm)
🧒 In plain English

Measuring current directly is tricky — you can't easily "see" electrons flowing. Instead, you put a tiny, precise resistor in the current path and measure the tiny voltage drop across it. More current = more voltage drop. The ISENSEp and ISENSEm pins are the two probes that measure that voltage drop.

The L9963E measures pack current using a shunt resistor method. A small, precision resistor (the shunt, value RISENSE) is placed in series with the battery pack's current path. When current flows, Ohm's Law creates a small voltage drop across the shunt: V = I × RISENSE. By measuring this voltage drop, the chip calculates the current.

The ISENSEp and ISENSEm pins connect across this shunt resistor. ISENSEp connects to the higher-potential side (positive terminal) and ISENSEm connects to the lower-potential side (negative terminal). The chip measures the differential voltage: V_diff = V(ISENSEp) − V(ISENSEm).

The maximum allowed differential voltage is ±150 mV. This means your shunt resistor value must be chosen so that at maximum pack current, the voltage across the shunt does not exceed ±150 mV. For a 200 A maximum current pack, this limits RISENSE to at most 0.75 mΩ. For a 314 Ah LFP cell pack (typical maximum continuous current ~200–300 A depending on C-rating), a shunt of 500 µΩ (0.5 mΩ) to 1 mΩ is typical.

The common-mode range of the ISENSEp/m inputs is ±225 mV around GNDREF. This means both pins must stay within 225 mV of GNDREF, which is why the shunt is placed on the negative side (near GNDREF) rather than the positive side (which would be at pack voltage) in most designs.

Shunt resistor in current path. ISENSEp/m measure differential drop. Place shunt near GNDREF.

⚠️ Watch Out

If RISENSE × I_max exceeds 150 mV, the current ADC will saturate and report wrong values — without any error flag. Always size your shunt for the absolute maximum current your pack can produce (including fault conditions). For LFP cells, consider 2–3× the rated current as the shunt sizing current to handle short-circuit scenarios.

The Current Sense ADC
🧒 In plain English

The current sense ADC is a dedicated converter just for the shunt voltage — it never shares time with cell voltage measurements. It's like having a separate checkout lane only for current measurement, running continuously in the background.

The L9963E contains a dedicated Current Sense Amplifier (CSA) and ADC for measuring the shunt voltage, separate from the cell voltage ADCs. This dedicated path ensures that current measurement is continuous and does not interfere with cell voltage measurement timing.

The current ADC is enabled by setting the coulombcounter_en bit to 1. When enabled, it runs continuously, taking current samples at regular intervals defined by TCYCLEADC_CUR. The instantaneous calibrated current reading is available in the CUR_INST_calib register, updated after every sample.

The current ADC has a built-in offset calibration mechanism. The chip measures the offset error of the CSA when no current is flowing (zero differential input) and stores this calibration offset, which is then subtracted from all subsequent measurements — this is the "calib" in CUR_INST_calib. This reduces the offset error that would otherwise appear as a false current reading when the pack is at rest.

The current ADC result is a signed value (current can flow in or out of the pack). Positive values indicate discharge current (current leaving the pack), and negative values indicate charge current (current entering the pack), or vice versa depending on the physical shunt orientation in your design. The resolution depends on your shunt value: with RISENSE = 500 µΩ and a 16-bit CSA, you get approximately 0.45 mA per LSB.

⚙️ Technical Detail

Enable: coulombcounter_en = 1 in conversion config register. Registers: CUR_INST_calib (instantaneous, with offset correction), CUR_INST (raw without correction). Sample interval: TCYCLEADC_CUR (same setting as cell ADC filter for current). Result: signed 18-bit. Range: ±150 mV / RISENSE. Example: 500 µΩ shunt → ±300 A range, ~0.45 mA resolution.

Coulomb Counting: Measuring How Much Charge Has Flowed
🧒 In plain English

A Coulomb counter is like a water meter. Current is the flow rate, time is how long the tap was open, and the counter accumulates the total volume flowed. Every time you deliver a known charge (in coulombs), you know the battery's SOC has changed by that amount.

The L9963E's Coulomb counter continuously integrates the current measurement over time, producing a running total of charge that has flowed into or out of the battery pack. Mathematically, charge Q = ∫I dt, where I is the measured current and dt is the sample period.

In hardware, the chip implements this as a 32-bit accumulator: CoulombCnt += current_sample × TCYCLEADC_CUR on every current sample. The result is stored in the CoulombCnt register (accessed via burst 0x7B). Reading the register clears the accumulator back to zero, so your firmware is responsible for adding up successive readings to maintain a running total.

The 32-bit accumulator will eventually overflow if not read frequently enough. The chip sets the CoCouOvF (Coulomb Counter Overflow) flag when this happens. This flag propagates to the GSW as a fault condition, so you'll notice it even without reading the CoulombCnt register directly. To prevent overflow, read the Coulomb counter at a rate that ensures the accumulator never reaches its maximum value between reads — for a 300 A pack with a 500 µΩ shunt and 2.5 ms sample period, you need to read at least every 30 seconds.

The calibrated current value is used for the accumulation, so the same offset correction that applies to CUR_INST_calib also applies to the Coulomb counter, reducing accumulated drift from ADC offset errors.

🔧 For your firmware

Maintain a 64-bit accumulator in your firmware: int64_t total_coulombs = 0. Every time you read CoulombCnt (which clears the chip's counter), add the signed 32-bit value to your total: total_coulombs += coulomb_cnt_signed. Convert to Ah: soc_ah = total_coulombs × (TCYCLEADC_CUR_sec / RISENSE_ohm) / 3600.

Overcurrent Detection
🧒 In plain English

Overcurrent detection is like a circuit breaker for your firmware alarm system. Instead of waiting for your MCU to poll the current value, the chip is always watching — even while asleep — and immediately raises an alarm if current spikes too high.

The L9963E provides hardware overcurrent detection that operates independently of the microcontroller, using the current sense ADC output. Two separate thresholds exist for different operating modes: adc_ovc_curr_threshold_sleep applies when the chip is in Sleep or Cyclic Wakeup mode, and adc_ovc_curr_threshold_norm applies in Normal mode.

When the instantaneous current exceeds the configured threshold in either direction (charge or discharge), the chip sets the OC_curr fault flag. In Sleep mode, an overcurrent event additionally triggers a wakeup — the chip exits Sleep and enters Normal mode to allow the MCU to handle the fault. This is essential for scenarios like a charged battery that is accidentally short-circuited while the BMS is sleeping: the chip wakes up and can alert the MCU via the FAULT line.

The thresholds are programmed in units that correspond to the current ADC's output, so the actual current threshold in amps depends on your shunt resistance. For a 314 Ah LFP cell rated at 1C continuous discharge (314 A), you might set the normal-mode threshold at 1.5× rated current (approximately 471 A) and the sleep threshold lower (to catch any unexpected current during standby).

Overcurrent detection works even during the cyclic wakeup sleep phase — this is one of the few monitoring functions that remains active when the chip is mostly powered down. This makes the L9963E suitable for applications where the BMS must protect the battery pack even while the main system is off.

⚙️ Technical Detail

In sleep/cyclic: additionally wakes chip to Normal. Threshold format: same scaling as current ADC output. Active during: Normal, Sleep, Cyclic Wakeup.

Current Sense Open Wire Diagnostic
🧒 In plain English

If your thermometer isn't plugged in, it reads 0°C no matter what — and you'd make wrong decisions based on a fake reading. Similarly, if the ISENSEp or ISENSEm wire is broken, the current ADC reads zero — and your Coulomb counter silently stops working. The open-wire diagnostic detects this broken wire.

An open circuit in either the ISENSEp or ISENSEm connection is a silent failure mode: the current ADC will simply read zero differential voltage and report zero current, while in reality any amount of current could be flowing. This would cause the Coulomb counter to stop accumulating charge, leading to SOC estimation errors that only worsen over time.

The L9963E detects this condition through dedicated flags: sense_plus_open (ISENSEp connection broken) and sense_minus_open (ISENSEm connection broken). The detection mechanism uses a small bias current injected into the ISENSEp and ISENSEm pins. If the pin is open (disconnected), the bias current causes the pin voltage to rail to a detectable extreme — confirming the break.

These diagnostic flags are checked as part of the conversion routine and propagate to the GSW as a fault condition. If either flag is set, the Coulomb counter results should not be trusted. Your firmware should treat a current sense open-wire fault as a serious error and either stop SOC-based state estimation or flag the data as invalid until the hardware is repaired.

In addition to the open-wire check, the current sensing path is also validated by a BIST (Built-In Self-Test) as part of the HWSC step, which verifies that the CSA and its associated comparators are functioning correctly.

🔧 For your firmware

After any fault burst (0x7A) read, check sense_plus_open and sense_minus_open bits. If either is set: (1) set a CURRENT_SENSE_FAULT flag in your state machine, (2) invalidate all Coulomb counter data, (3) switch SOC estimation to voltage-only mode, (4) log the event for diagnostics, (5) alert the operator/log system if in a safety-critical mode.

Coulomb Counting Routine
🧒 In plain English

The Coulomb counter runs in the background, independently of everything else — like an odometer that keeps ticking even while you're checking the speedometer. You can pause it, but while it's running it silently accumulates every bit of charge that flows.

The Coulomb counting routine operates as a completely separate background process from the voltage measurement routine. While the cell voltage ADC runs periodically (either on-demand or cyclic), the current ADC runs continuously at its own fixed sample rate determined by TCYCLEADC_CUR.

Each current sample is accumulated into CoulombCnt without any MCU intervention required. The accumulation is hardware-based, meaning it continues even if the MCU is busy processing other data, handling SPI communication, or executing non-measurement firmware tasks. This ensures no charge "goes missing" during MCU processing gaps.

The Coulomb counting routine can be disabled by writing coulombcounter_en = 0. When disabled, both CUR_INST and CoulombCnt registers stop being updated. Disabling saves approximately 1 mA of current consumption — useful in low-power scenarios where current measurement is not needed. Note that disabling also means the current ADC is off, so overcurrent detection thresholds also stop working.

When the Coulomb counter is enabled and the chip enters Sleep or Cyclic Wakeup mode, there is a nuance: the current ADC itself may be powered down during the sleep phase of a cyclic wakeup, depending on the configuration. Check the datasheet's power mode table to understand exactly which current-sense functions remain active in each power mode for your specific configuration.

The GPIO Analog Inputs and NTCs
🧒 In plain English

An NTC thermistor is a resistor that changes its resistance based on temperature — it resists more when cold and less when hot. By measuring the voltage across it in a divider circuit, the chip indirectly measures temperature. GPIO3–9 are the measurement probes for these thermistors.

Temperature measurement in the L9963E uses external NTC (Negative Temperature Coefficient) thermistors connected to GPIO pins 3 through 9 — up to 7 temperature sensors per chip. Each NTC thermistor is connected in a voltage divider configuration: the VTREF pin (a stable 5 V reference) through a precision pull-up resistor (RVTREF), down to the GPIO pin, then through the NTC to AGND. As temperature changes, the NTC resistance changes, moving the voltage at the GPIO pin.

The VTREF pin is the key to accurate temperature measurement. It provides a precise, stable reference voltage that sources current through the voltage divider. Since both VTREF and the pull-up resistor are stable and well-characterized, any change in GPIO voltage directly reflects a change in NTC resistance, which reflects a change in temperature.

On the PCB, each GPIO-NTC connection also includes a small filter resistor (RGPIO, typically 1–10 kΩ) in series with the GPIO pin to limit pin current and filter high-frequency noise, and a small capacitor (CNTC, typically 100 nF) from the GPIO pin to AGND to further filter noise. These components form a low-pass filter similar to the cell input filter.

GPIO8 and GPIO9 are locked as SPI SCK and SDO when SPIEN=1, so in direct SPI mode, you only have 5 NTC channels available (GPIO3–7). GPIO7 also has a secondary wake-up function that you may need to account for.

NTC voltage divider: VTREF → RVTREF → GPIO pin → NTC → AGND. GPIO voltage ∝ 1/temperature.

How GPIO Voltage Converts to Temperature
🧒 In plain English

The chip measures the voltage at the GPIO pin, but you want the temperature. You need a translation table — the NTC's datasheet gives you a resistance-vs-temperature curve, and knowing VTREF and RVTREF lets you turn that into a voltage-vs-temperature curve. Your firmware does the lookup.

The L9963E's GPIO measurement provides a voltage reading — not a temperature directly. Converting this to temperature is a firmware responsibility, using the NTC's known characteristics. The chip provides two measurement modes for the GPIO ADC to help with accuracy.

Absolute mode (ratio_abs_x_sel = 0): the GPIO voltage is measured as an absolute voltage using the same 89 µV/LSB formula as cell voltages. V_GPIO = code × 89 µV. This is straightforward but can introduce error if VTREF drifts slightly from nominal.

Ratiometric mode (ratio_abs_x_sel = 1): the GPIO voltage is measured as a ratio to VTREF. The result is a dimensionless ratio: V_GPIO / V_VTREF = code × 2⁻¹⁶. Because both the numerator and denominator track any VTREF changes together, any variation in VTREF cancels out — making this mode significantly more accurate. For precise temperature measurements, ratiometric mode is strongly recommended.

Once you have the GPIO voltage (or voltage ratio), you calculate the NTC resistance: R_NTC = RVTREF × (V_GPIO / (VTREF − V_GPIO)) in absolute mode, or R_NTC = RVTREF × (ratio / (1 − ratio)) in ratiometric mode. You then use the NTC manufacturer's B-constant formula or a lookup table to convert R_NTC to temperature in °C.

The accuracy of the temperature measurement depends strongly on the accuracy of your RVTREF resistor (use 1% tolerance or better) and the quality of the NTC itself.

🔧 For your firmware

Use ratiometric mode for all NTC measurements. Formula: ratio = gpio_code / 65536.0f, then R_ntc = R_pullup * ratio / (1.0f - ratio). Then use the NTC B-constant formula: T_K = 1 / (ln(R_ntc / R_25) / B + 1/298.15). T_C = T_K - 273.15. Use R_25 and B from your NTC datasheet.

On-Chip Die Temperature
🧒 In plain English

The chip can feel its own temperature — like a person who knows when they have a fever. If the chip gets too hot (from balancing or high ambient temperature), it automatically stops balancing to protect itself before it gets damaged.

The L9963E contains an internal temperature sensor that monitors the chip's own silicon junction temperature (Tj). This is separate from the external NTC measurements — it measures how hot the chip itself is, not the battery cells. The reading is available in the TempChip register.

The conversion formula for the die temperature is: Tj (°C) = 1.3828 × binary_code − 99.733. The binary_code is a 2's complement signed integer from the TempChip register. For example, a code of 145 gives Tj = 1.3828 × 145 − 99.733 = 200.5 − 99.7 = 100.8°C. This formula is hardcoded into the chip and is valid across the specified junction temperature range.

If the die temperature exceeds the Thermal Shutdown threshold (TSD), which is typically between 175°C and 200°C, the chip automatically stops all balancing activity to prevent self-damage. It sets the OTchip flag in the fault registers, which propagates to the GSW and FAULT line. Normal operation can resume after the chip cools down and the fault latch is cleared.

The die temperature is especially relevant during heavy balancing. Each balancing channel that is active generates heat in the chip's internal MOSFETs. For a 14-cell pack with all channels balancing at 200 mA simultaneously, the thermal dissipation inside the chip can be substantial, and you should monitor TempChip during extended balancing sessions.

GPIO OT/UT Thresholds for Automatic Temperature Alarms
🧒 In plain English

Instead of your firmware constantly comparing "is it too hot?", you can program the chip to do this comparison in hardware. When a cell gets too hot or too cold, the chip raises an alarm automatically — no firmware polling needed.

Each GPIO configured as an analog input (for NTC measurement) can have programmable Over-Temperature (OT) and Under-Temperature (UT) thresholds. The chip automatically compares every GPIO voltage measurement against these thresholds and sets fault flags if they are crossed.

Here is the critical insight: because the NTC voltage divider is inverted (higher temperature = lower GPIO voltage), the threshold polarity is also inverted. An OT (too hot) event corresponds to the GPIO voltage falling below the threshold — in other words, OT triggers on undervoltage of the GPIO pin. A UT (too cold) event corresponds to the GPIO voltage rising above the threshold — UT triggers on overvoltage.

Thresholds are configured as GPIO voltage values (not temperatures) using the VGPIOAN_OT and VGPIOAN_UT registers for each GPIO channel. You calculate what GPIO voltage corresponds to your desired temperature threshold using the NTC curve, and program that voltage value. For example, if your OT limit is 60°C and at 60°C the NTC gives 2.1 V at the GPIO pin, you program VGPIOAN_OT = 2.1 V (expressed in the register's LSB units).

An additional feature is the Fast Charge OT threshold: a second, tighter OT limit that can be activated specifically during fast charging. LFP cells are more sensitive to overtemperature during high-current charging, so having a dedicated fast-charge OT threshold (typically 10–15°C lower than normal OT) allows the charger to be automatically limited without requiring MCU intervention.

🔧 For your firmware

Before enabling cyclic conversions, always program all 7 GPIO OT/UT thresholds. Convert your temperature limits to GPIO voltages using your NTC characterization. For LFP: typical OT = 60°C (charging), 70°C (discharging); UT = −20°C (charging) or −30°C (discharging). Set the fast-charge OT ~10°C lower than discharge OT. Check GPIOx_OT and GPIOx_UT flags in the fault register burst.

Why Balancing? The Cell Drift Problem
🧒 In plain English

Imagine 14 buckets of water connected by pipes. If they all start equal but one leaks slightly faster, it will always hit empty first and stop the system — even though the other buckets are still full. Balancing is the process of equalizing the buckets so they all hit empty (or full) at the same time.

In a series-connected battery pack, all cells experience the same current — they all charge and discharge together. Over hundreds of cycles, even tiny differences in cell capacity, self-discharge rate, or internal resistance cause some cells to reach full charge (OV limit) or empty (UV limit) before others. This mismatch is called cell imbalance.

The consequence is real and significant: the entire pack must stop charging when the highest-capacity cell hits its OV limit, even if the other cells aren't full yet. The pack must stop discharging when the weakest cell hits its UV limit, even if the others still have charge. As imbalance grows, the usable capacity of the pack shrinks — even though the individual cells are still healthy.

Passive balancing addresses this by deliberately draining charge from cells that are running "ahead" of the pack. A small resistor is connected across each high cell, allowing current to flow and burn off the excess charge as heat. This equalizes the cells at the top of charge (or any desired voltage target). It is called "passive" because it wastes energy as heat rather than transferring it to lower cells (which would require more complex active balancing circuitry).

The L9963E implements passive balancing with per-cell control: each of the 14 cells can be independently enabled or disabled for balancing. The balancing current is set by the external resistor RDIS (the "discharge" resistor), typically 15–20 Ω for approximately 200 mA balancing current per cell at 3.3 V.

🧠 Key Insight

Passive balancing doesn't add charge to weak cells — it removes charge from strong cells to match the weakest one. This means balancing always works at the top of the charge curve, where cells diverge most. At the bottom, you should stop balancing well above the UV threshold.

Balancing Pins: S1–S14 and B-Pins
🧒 In plain English

The S-pins are like light switches — the chip flips them on to connect a resistor across a cell, allowing current to flow and bleed off excess charge. The B-pins are shared ground rails between adjacent balancing switches.

The physical implementation of balancing uses three pin types per pair of cells: the S-pins (balancing switch outputs), the B-pins (common terminals), and the external RDIS resistors. Understanding this physical layout is essential for correct PCB design.

Each cell has one S-pin: S1 for cell 1, S2 for cell 2, and so on up to S14 for cell 14. Each pair of adjacent cells shares a B-pin: B2_1 is shared between cells 1 and 2, B4_3 between cells 3 and 4, and so on. The internal balancing circuit consists of a power MOSFET connected between Sn and the internal reference. When enabled, the MOSFET turns on and connects Sn to the balancing path.

The external discharge resistor RDIS is connected between the Sn pin and the Bn,n-1 pin (or between Sn and the cell's negative terminal, depending on the circuit variant). When the internal MOSFET closes, current flows from the positive terminal of the cell, through RDIS, through the MOSFET, back to the negative terminal — bleeding off charge as heat in RDIS.

The typical balancing current is 200 mA with a 15–20 Ω RDIS for a 3.3 V LFP cell. The heat generated in RDIS is P = I² × RDIS = (0.2)² × 15 = 0.6 W per cell. For 14 cells simultaneously balancing, that's 8.4 W of heat in the balance resistors — your PCB thermal design must accommodate this.

Balancing current path: cell+ → RDIS → S(N) → internal MOSFET → B(N,N-1) → cell−

Internal vs. External Balancing
🧒 In plain English

Internal balancing uses the chip's own transistors to switch the current — simple and compact. External balancing uses the chip's S-pins to drive bigger external transistors, allowing much higher current. Same idea, different power levels.

The L9963E supports two balancing topologies, and the choice between them depends primarily on the required balancing current for your application.

Internal balancing uses the L9963E's built-in power MOSFETs directly. The S-pins are the outputs of these internal MOSFETs, and the RDIS resistor is connected externally between Sn and Bn,n-1. Current flows through the internal FET, which handles the switching. The internal FETs are rated for up to approximately 200 mA per channel — sufficient for most smaller packs where slow balancing over hours is acceptable. This is the simpler circuit: fewer external components, less PCB area, straightforward design.

External balancing uses the S-pins as gate drivers for external MOSFETs, which carry the actual balancing current. The S-pin voltage swings to control the external FET gate. With external FETs, you can achieve balancing currents of 1 A, 2 A, or more — much faster balancing for large-capacity cells. For a 314 Ah LFP cell, higher balancing current means completing a balancing session in minutes rather than hours.

The register configuration is the same for both topologies — you still use the same BAL_CFG register bits to enable and disable individual cells. The physical circuit on the PCB is what differs. The chip does not need to know which topology you're using; it simply drives the S-pins high or low based on the software configuration.

The internal FETs also provide overcurrent protection: if balancing current exceeds approximately 400 mA (IBAL_OC threshold), the chip detects a short-circuit condition and shuts off that balancing channel, setting the BALx_SHORT fault flag.

🔧 For your firmware

No firmware changes are needed between internal and external balancing topologies — the register interface is identical. The only difference is at the hardware level. For large-format cells (>100 Ah), external FET balancing with 500 mA to 1 A per channel is recommended to complete balancing within a reasonable timeframe (1–4 hours for typical SOC spreads).

Manual Balancing: Turning It On Per Cell
🧒 In plain English

Manual balancing is like controlling each light switch yourself — you decide which cells to drain, turn them on, and turn them off yourself. You have full control, but you have to actively manage it.

In manual balancing mode, your firmware directly controls which cells are balancing at any given time. The control register is BAL_CFG, which contains individual enable bits: VCELL1_BAL_EN through VCELL14_BAL_EN. Writing a 1 to any of these bits enables the balancing switch for that cell; writing 0 disables it.

The typical manual balancing algorithm in firmware is: (1) measure all cell voltages after a full charge, (2) calculate the target voltage (usually the minimum cell voltage in the pack, or a fixed balance target), (3) for each cell that is above the target by more than a small threshold (to avoid endless micro-balancing), set its balance enable bit, (4) while balancing, periodically re-measure voltages and disable balancing for cells that have reached the target, (5) stop when all cells are within the balance tolerance window.

Multiple cells can balance simultaneously — all 14 can be active at the same time if needed. The chip's thermal and current capacity are the limiting factors. Monitoring die temperature (TempChip) during intense balancing is recommended to ensure you don't approach the thermal shutdown threshold.

Manual balancing continues until your firmware explicitly disables it (write 0 to the enable bits) or a fault occurs (balance short/open detection, OTchip, or a UV fault which disables balancing automatically to protect the cell from going further below the UV threshold).

⚙️ Technical Detail

Register: BAL_CFG. Bits: VCELL1_BAL_EN[0] through VCELL14_BAL_EN[13]. Set to 1 to enable, 0 to disable. All 14 can be active simultaneously. Balancing automatically stops on: OTchip, UV fault on that cell, BALx_OPEN or BALx_SHORT fault. Does not auto-stop on OV — firmware must stop it.

Timed Balancing: Let the Chip Handle Duration
🧒 In plain English

Instead of your firmware constantly checking "have we balanced enough yet?", you calculate the time needed, set a countdown timer, and the chip automatically stops when the timer runs out. Set it and forget it — the chip does the rest.

Timed balancing is an alternative to manual balancing that reduces MCU involvement. Instead of polling voltages and deciding when to stop, you pre-calculate how long to balance and set a timer. The chip counts down and stops balancing automatically when the timer expires, then sets the EOB (End of Balancing) flag.

The timer value is programmed into the TimedBalTimer register with a resolution of approximately 1 second. The maximum timer value allows for many hours of continuous balancing. To use timed balancing, you: (1) enable the desired balance channels in BAL_CFG, (2) write the desired duration in seconds to TimedBalTimer, (3) set the timed balance enable bit, (4) the chip starts counting and balancing simultaneously. When the timer reaches zero, balancing stops and EOB is set.

To calculate the correct timer value, use the energy balance equation: time = ΔQ / I_bal = (cell_voltage_difference × cell_capacity) / (I_bal × V_avg). More practically: if cell A is 50 mV above cell B, and each cell is 314 Ah with I_bal = 200 mA, the charge to remove is approximately: ΔQ = capacity × ΔV / V_cell = 314 × 0.05 / 3.3 ≈ 4.76 Ah, and time = 4.76 Ah / 0.2 A = 23.8 hours.

Timed balancing is particularly useful for Silent Balancing (the next chapter), where the MCU may be asleep and cannot actively poll voltage levels.

🔧 For your firmware

Poll the EOB bit after enabling timed balancing — it tells you when the session is complete. Or use it in silent balancing mode where you set the timer and put the chip to sleep; when the chip wakes, check EOB to confirm balancing completed. Formula: bal_time_s = (delta_voltage_V / cell_voltage_V) * cell_capacity_Ah / I_bal_A * 3600.

Silent Balancing Mode
🧒 In plain English

Silent Balancing is like the cleaning crew that works all night while the office sleeps. The MCU shuts down, the chip goes mostly to sleep — but the balancing resistors keep quietly draining the high cells overnight, ready for a balanced pack in the morning.

Silent Balancing is a special low-power state unique to the L9963E: the chip is effectively in a sleep mode (VREG is off, SPI is inactive, no ADC measurements), but the balancing predriver circuits remain powered and balancing continues on all enabled channels. This enables overnight or extended balancing without requiring the MCU to stay active.

To enter Silent Balancing: (1) enable the desired balance channels in BAL_CFG, (2) optionally set up timed balancing with TimedBalTimer, (3) set slp_bal_conf = 1 in the configuration register, (4) send the "Go to Sleep" SPI command. Instead of entering full sleep, the chip detects that slp_bal_conf = 1 and active balancing channels exist, and transitions to Silent Balancing state.

In Silent Balancing, current draw is approximately 1.2–2.8 mA, compared to 10–50 µA in true sleep. This is because the balancing predriver circuits need power, and the actual balancing current (200 mA per cell) flows through the cells and RDIS resistors. The chip monitors for the timed balance expiry (EOB) and for any fault conditions that would require waking up (short circuit, overcurrent on the pack).

To wake from Silent Balancing, the MCU can send a wakeup SPI sequence, or the chip can wake itself if the timed balance timer expires. After waking, the chip re-enters Normal state and the MCU can read measurement data. Note that cell voltages cannot be read during Silent Balancing — the ADC power is off.

⚠️ Watch Out

In Silent Balancing, there is no voltage monitoring — the chip cannot check for cell UV or OV while balancing silently. If a cell reaches the UV threshold during overnight balancing, there is no automatic protection. Always use timed balancing with a conservative timer when using Silent Balancing, and measure cell voltages immediately after waking to catch any over-discharged cells.

Balance Faults: Open and Short Detection
🧒 In plain English

What if a balancing resistor falls off the PCB (open) or something shorts across the cell while balancing (short)? The chip detects both — a missing resistor means no current flows when expected; a short means too much current flows. Both set fault flags and stop balancing to protect the cells.

The L9963E actively monitors the integrity of every balancing channel and can detect two types of faults: open-circuit and short-circuit conditions.

BALx_OPEN detection: To check for an open-circuit in the balancing path (e.g., a disconnected RDIS resistor or broken S-pin trace), the chip applies a small pull-down current (the open diagnostic current) to the S-pin with the balance switch closed. It then uses an internal comparator to check whether the S-pin voltage is in the expected range. If the balancing path is open, no current flows and the voltage at the S-pin will be at an unexpected level — the comparator detects this discrepancy and sets the BALx_OPEN fault flag.

BALx_SHORT detection: When the balance switch is enabled, the chip monitors the current through the switch using an internal overcurrent comparator. If current exceeds the threshold IBAL_OC (approximately 400 mA for internal balancing), the chip immediately turns off that balance channel and sets the BALx_SHORT flag. This protects against a shorted RDIS (which would allow unrestricted current from the cell) or a shorted external MOSFET.

Both types of faults propagate to the GSW and the FAULT line, alerting the MCU. Once a fault is detected, balancing on the affected channel stops automatically. The fault latch must be cleared by firmware before balancing can be re-attempted on that channel. If the fault reappears after clearing and re-enabling, it indicates a persistent hardware problem.

The balance comparators themselves are validated by the Hardware Self-Check (HWSC) step of the voltage conversion routine, ensuring that the fault detection mechanism is itself trustworthy.

Three scenarios: normal (200 mA), open (0 mA → BALx_OPEN), short (>400 mA → BALx_SHORT)

Balance Auto-Pause During ADC Measurement
🧒 In plain English

If you try to weigh yourself while jumping on the scale, you get a wrong reading. Balancing current through the RLPF resistor creates a voltage error during measurement — like jumping on the scale. Auto-pause stops balancing briefly while the ADC reads, then resumes automatically.

When balancing is active on a cell, the balancing current (typically 200 mA) flows through the external RLPF (low-pass filter resistor, 1 kΩ) on the C-pin trace. This current creates a voltage drop across RLPF: V_error = I_bal × RLPF = 0.2 A × 1000 Ω = 200 mV. This is an enormous error for a system trying to measure cell voltages to ±2 mV accuracy — it would completely invalidate the measurement.

To prevent this, the L9963E includes an automatic balancing pause feature. When BAL_AUTO_PAUSE = 1, the chip automatically stops all active balancing channels just before the cell voltage ADC starts sampling, waits for the cell voltages to settle (the T_CELL_SET settling time), takes the ADC measurements, then automatically re-enables balancing after the cells step of the conversion is complete.

A related bit is BAL_TIM_AUTO_PAUSE = 1: this causes the timed balance timer to freeze during the pause period. Without this, the timer keeps counting down while balancing is paused, under-counting the actual balancing time and potentially under-balancing the cells. With this bit set, the timer only counts actual balancing time — fair accounting.

The T_CELL_SET (cell settling time) is a configurable delay between stopping balancing and starting the ADC measurement. It allows the cell terminal voltage to stabilize after the balancing current stops. This settling time should account for the RC time constant of the RLPF/CLPF filter: τ = 1 kΩ × 10 nF = 10 µs. A settling time of 5–10 time constants (50–100 µs) is typically sufficient.

⚠️ Watch Out

ALWAYS set BAL_AUTO_PAUSE = 1 and BAL_TIM_AUTO_PAUSE = 1 in your initialization sequence if you plan to run balancing and voltage measurements simultaneously. If you forget, your cell voltage readings will be wrong by up to 200 mV whenever balancing is active — which is during most of the charging phase. This error could trigger false OV/UV faults.

The Safety Architecture: Three Layers
🧒 In plain English

Think of airport security: there's a fast scanner at the entrance (hardware comparators), a more thorough bag check (ADC thresholds), and a periodic equipment inspection to make sure the machines work (HWSC). Three independent layers catch more failures than one.

The L9963E implements a three-layer safety architecture, each layer with different speed and accuracy characteristics. Together they provide ASIL-D compliant fault coverage — the highest level of automotive functional safety.

Layer 1 — Analog Comparators (always-on, hardware-speed): These are dedicated hardware comparators that continuously watch cell voltages, stack voltage, and supply voltages against fixed or programmable thresholds. They respond in microseconds, independent of any ADC conversion in progress or MCU communication. They cannot be turned off and do not depend on the digital core. The tradeoff is lower accuracy — they use bandgap-referenced thresholds with broader tolerances than the ADC.

Layer 2 — ADC-Based Digital Thresholds (programmable, ADC-accurate, slower): After each ADC conversion cycle, the digital core compares all measured values against software-programmable thresholds in the configuration registers. These comparisons use the full ADC accuracy (±2 mV) and can be configured precisely for your cell chemistry and operating conditions. They are slower than the comparators (limited by conversion cycle time) but far more accurate and flexible.

Layer 3 — Hardware Self-Check / HWSC (periodic, validates layers 1 and 2): The HWSC step of the voltage conversion routine runs Built-In Self-Tests (BISTs) on the ADCs and all analog comparators. It injects known test signals and verifies the expected outputs. If any comparator or ADC fails the BIST, the chip knows its own fault detection system is compromised and flags this — so the MCU knows it cannot trust the Layer 1 or Layer 2 results. This "checking the checkers" is the hallmark of ASIL-D compliant design.

Three layers: fast analog comparators → accurate ADC thresholds → HWSC validates both

Cell OV/UV Detection
🧒 In plain English

OV (Over-Voltage) is like overfilling a glass — the cell gets damaged. UV (Under-Voltage) is draining it too empty — also damages the cell. The chip has programmable red lines for both. Cross either line and an alarm fires immediately.

Cell OV and UV detection are among the most critical safety functions of the L9963E. The chip compares every measured cell voltage against programmable threshold registers and sets fault flags if any cell exceeds the limits.

The VCELL_OV threshold: if any enabled cell's voltage exceeds this value after conversion, the VCELL_OV fault flag is set. For LFP (Lithium Iron Phosphate) cells, a typical OV threshold is 3.65 V. Going above this can cause lithium plating, gas generation, and thermal runaway risk.

The VCELL_UV threshold: if any enabled cell's voltage falls below this value, the VCELL_UV fault flag is set and balancing on that cell is automatically disabled (to prevent further cell drain). For LFP cells, a typical UV threshold is 2.5 V. Deep discharge causes irreversible capacity loss and potential copper dissolution in the anode.

There is also a third related threshold: VBAL_UV = VCELL_UV + VCELL_BAL_UV_DELTA. This is a softer UV limit specifically for balancing — when a cell drops below VBAL_UV (which is slightly above VCELL_UV), balancing on that cell stops preemptively. This prevents balancing from accidentally draining a cell all the way to the hard UV fault.

All three thresholds are fully programmable via SPI registers. For a 14-cell LFP pack: OV = 3.65 V, UV = 2.5 V, BAL_UV delta = 0.3 V (so balancing stops at 2.8 V). Always set BAL_UV above UV by a comfortable margin.

⚙️ Technical Detail

Registers: VCELL_OV (programmable, per-pack), VCELL_UV, VCELL_BAL_UV_DELTA. OV fault: VCELL_OV flag, propagated to GSW. UV fault: VCELL_UV flag + automatic balancing stop on that cell. VBAL_UV = VCELL_UV + VCELL_BAL_UV_DELTA — balancing stop threshold. For LFP: OV = 3.65 V, UV = 2.5 V, BAL_UV delta = 0.3 V (balancing stops at 2.8 V).

Stack Voltage OV/UV: Three Independent Checks
🧒 In plain English

For the total pack voltage, the chip doesn't trust just one measurement — it uses three completely independent watchdogs simultaneously. It's like having three separate smoke detectors in one room. Any one of them can raise the alarm, and each uses a different sensing method.

The total battery pack voltage (stack voltage) is monitored by three independent systems running in parallel. This redundancy is required by ASIL-D safety standards — no single point of failure can disable all stack voltage protection.

Check 1 — Vsum_batt (ADC-based sum): The sum of all individual cell ADC readings, accumulated hardware-automatically during each conversion. Compared against VSUM_OV and VSUM_UV threshold registers. Full ADC accuracy (±2 mV × 14 cells). Fault flags: VSUM_OV, VSUM_UV.

Check 2 — VBAT pin direct measurement: An independent resistor divider on the VBAT pin feeds a separate ADC conversion. This path is completely independent of the cell voltage ADC chain — if the cell ADC has a systematic error, this check can still catch a stack OV/UV event. Fault flags: VBATTCRIT_OV, VBATTCRIT_UV.

Check 3 — Analog comparator on VBAT: A continuously-running analog comparator watches the VBAT pin against a hardware threshold, independent of any ADC conversion timing. It responds in microseconds — the fastest of all three checks. This is the first line of defense for a sudden voltage spike that might happen between conversion cycles. Fault flags: VBATT_WRN_OV, VBATT_WRN_UV.

All three checks can set their respective fault flags independently. Your firmware should inspect all three when diagnosing a stack voltage fault to determine which watchdog triggered first and why.

🧠 Key Insight

A disagreement between the three checks is itself informative. If Vsum says OV but VBAT pin says normal, a cell measurement may be wrong. If VBAT says OV but Vsum says normal, the VBAT divider or cell connections may be faulty. The pattern of which flags fire guides your diagnosis.

The FAULT Line: A Dedicated Hardware Alert Pin
🧒 In plain English

The FAULT line is like a fire alarm pull station — completely separate from the normal communication system. Even if the SPI bus is broken or the MCU isn't listening, the FAULT line can still ring the alarm bell. And it regularly pulses to prove it's still alive.

The L9963E dedicates two GPIO pins exclusively to the FAULT signaling system: GPIO1/FAULTH (fault input from upper chip in chain) and GPIO2/FAULTL (fault output to lower chip or MCU). These pins cannot be reconfigured for any other purpose.

FAULTL is an open-drain output. In normal operation it is driven high (inactive). When a critical fault occurs, FAULTL is pulled low, asserting the fault signal. On a single-chip board, FAULTL connects directly to an MCU GPIO configured as an interrupt input with a pull-up resistor. When the fault fires, the MCU gets an immediate hardware interrupt — no SPI polling needed, no latency.

The FAULT line operates independently of the SPI bus. If SPI communication is broken (e.g., hardware failure, MCU firmware crash), the FAULT line can still signal a battery emergency directly to other hardware — for example, an external protection circuit that opens a contactor. This independence is critical for safety: the battery protection path must not depend on the communication path.

The L9963E also implements a FAULT heartbeat: the chip periodically pulses FAULTL at a fixed interval to prove it is alive and functioning. If the MCU's interrupt handler stops seeing heartbeat pulses, it knows the L9963E has stopped responding and can take protective action. The heartbeat pulse rate is configurable.

In a daisy chain, FAULTH and FAULTL enable fault propagation through the chain: a fault in chip #5 propagates through chips #4, #3, #2, #1 via optocouplers, eventually reaching the MCU.

🔧 For your firmware

Configure an MCU external interrupt on the FAULTL pin (falling edge triggered, pull-up enabled). In the ISR: set a fault_interrupt_flag. In your main loop: when flag set, immediately send burst 0x7A to read all fault registers. Check GSW, parse all fault flags, determine the fault type, and take protective action (open contactor, stop charging, alert driver). Also implement heartbeat timeout: if no pulse received in 2× heartbeat period, treat as chip failure.

Open Wire Detection for Cell Pins
🧒 In plain English

If a wire to a cell pin breaks, the ADC reads a wrong voltage — possibly looking like an OV or UV that isn't real, or missing a real fault. Open wire detection injects a tiny test current and checks if it behaves as expected. No current flow = broken wire.

A broken connection to any C-pin is a critical failure mode. Without a physical connection to a cell terminal, the ADC input floats and can read any value — possibly a falsely normal reading, or a spurious fault, or anything in between. The L9963E's open wire detection proactively tests each C-pin connection to catch this before it leads to a missed overvoltage event or false BMS shutdown.

The detection method uses a small diagnostic current (IOPEN_DIAG_CX, approximately 40 µA) applied to each C-pin sequentially. This current flows through the RLPF filter resistor (1 kΩ) to the cell terminal. If the wire is intact, the 40 µA × 1 kΩ = 40 mV voltage drop across RLPF is detectable — the ADC will measure the cell voltage shifted by exactly 40 mV. If the wire is broken (open), the diagnostic current sees infinite resistance and causes the voltage to swing to an extreme that the chip recognizes as an open circuit.

The ADC_CROSS_CHECK bit selects two variants of the check. With ADC_CROSS_CHECK = 0: standard pull-down current applied — detects physical open wires and high-resistance RLPF connections. With ADC_CROSS_CHECK = 1: both ADCs measure the same cell simultaneously without any diagnostic current — any discrepancy between the two ADC readings indicates an ADC mismatch or a leaky CLPF capacitor, rather than an open wire.

C0 (the bottom cell pin) is a special case: it uses a pull-UP current instead of pull-down, because the detection needs to work in the opposite direction at the bottom of the stack. Results are stored as individual CELLx_OPEN flags in the diagnostic registers.

🔧 For your firmware

Run open wire detection as part of startup and then periodically (e.g., every 10 conversion cycles). Enable the cell terminal diagnostic step in the conversion routine config. After conversion, check all CELLx_OPEN flags in the diagnostic registers. If any flag is set: do not trust that cell's voltage reading, set a hardware fault alarm, and require manual inspection before resuming normal operation.

ADC Swap: Limp-Home Mode
🧒 In plain English

If one ADC breaks, you'd normally lose measurements for half the cells. ADC swap is like a left-handed person who breaks their right hand — they can switch to the left hand and still do most tasks. Both ADCs can cover each other's cells in a two-pass measurement.

The L9963E has two independent ADCs (one for odd cells, one for even cells). If HWSC detects that one ADC has failed, the chip would normally lose the ability to measure half the cells. The ADC Swap feature provides a limp-home mode that maintains full cell coverage even with one failed ADC.

The swap is controlled by the CROSS_ODD_EVEN_CELL bit. In normal operation (bit=0), the odd ADC measures odd cells and the even ADC measures even cells. When this bit is set to 1, the connections are swapped: the working ADC now measures the cells that the failed ADC normally covered. By running two conversion passes — one with swap=0 and one with swap=1 — and merging the results, your firmware can reconstruct full 14-cell coverage using only one functioning ADC.

The practical implementation requires two SOC triggers per measurement cycle instead of one. Pass 1 (swap=0): read cells handled by the surviving ADC normally. Pass 2 (swap=1): the surviving ADC now covers the cells that the failed ADC normally handled. Merge both passes into a complete 14-cell dataset.

The accuracy of the limp-home readings may be slightly different from normal operation because you're using one ADC path for all cells instead of two independent paths. But it is far better than having no readings at all. This feature is specifically designed to allow continued safe operation while the vehicle drives to a service location — hence "limp-home."

🔧 For your firmware

Limp-home mode trigger: HWSC reports ADC failure via HWSC fault flags. Response: (1) log the ADC failure, (2) switch to two-pass measurement mode, (3) pass 1: SOC with CROSS_ODD_EVEN_CELL=0, read available cells, (4) pass 2: set CROSS_ODD_EVEN_CELL=1, SOC again, read remaining cells, (5) merge results, (6) continue operating with a degraded-mode alert to the driver/system.

Hardware Self-Check (HWSC): The Chip Checks Itself
🧒 In plain English

HWSC is the chip's annual physical exam — it takes a moment to check all its own internal organs. Are the comparators working? Are the ADCs accurate? It runs these tests automatically during the conversion routine and tells you if anything is broken inside itself.

The Hardware Self-Check (HWSC) is a comprehensive Built-In Self-Test (BIST) that verifies the correct functionality of the L9963E's own internal fault detection circuitry. It is the cornerstone of the chip's ASIL-D compliance — without HWSC, you can detect battery faults but you cannot verify that the detection mechanisms themselves are working.

HWSC is one of the optional steps in the voltage conversion routine (Step 7 — the last step). When this step is enabled, the chip injects internal test signals into its own ADC paths, comparator inputs, and balancing monitor circuits. It compares the results against expected values and sets individual failure flags for each component tested.

The tests performed include: all Cx pin paths to ADC (verifies each cell measurement channel), all GPIOx paths to ADC (verifies temperature measurement channels), all Sx/Bx paths to ADC (verifies balancing monitor paths), VBAT/VREG/VCOM/VTREF UV/OV comparators, and the balance comparators. Each test has its own pass/fail flag in the HWSC_result registers.

The HWSC step takes exactly 3 × TCYCLEADC_000 (the fastest ADC filter setting, regardless of the configured filter — HWSC uses its own fixed timing). When HWSC completes, the HWSC_DONE flag is set. If any individual component test fails, its corresponding flag is set in the HWSC result registers, and the fault propagates to GSW.

Best practice is to run HWSC at startup and then every N conversion cycles during normal operation (configurable via the NCYCLE_HWSC field), not on every single conversion cycle since HWSC adds time to the conversion.

⚙️ Technical Detail

HWSC enable: set bit in conversion steps config register. Duration: 3 × TCYCLEADC_000 (fixed, independent of ADC_FILTER setting). Done flag: HWSC_DONE. Result registers: BIST_COMP and related (one bit per tested component). Periodic: controlled by NCYCLE_HWSC — run HWSC every N cycles. Failure: propagates to GSW Level 2 (fault) or Level 3 (critical) depending on what failed.

The Voltage Conversion Routine in Full
🧒 In plain English

The conversion routine is like a morning inspection checklist — you always check the mandatory items first (cells, VBAT), then optionally check the extra items (temperature, diagnostics, self-test). More checks = more time, but you can skip the optional ones when speed matters.

When you trigger a conversion (SOC=1), the L9963E executes a sequence of up to seven measurement and diagnostic steps in a fixed order. The DUTY_ON flag remains high for the entire sequence. Understanding this sequence helps you configure the routine for your timing requirements.

The seven steps in order are: ① Cells — measure all enabled cell voltages (mandatory, always runs). ② VBAT — measure VBAT pin via resistor divider (mandatory, always runs). ③ GPIO — measure all enabled GPIO analog inputs (optional — enables NTC temperature readings). ④ GPIO terminal diagnostic — test GPIO open/short conditions (optional). ⑤ Cell terminal diagnostic — test C-pin open wire conditions (optional). ⑥ Balance terminal diagnostic — test Sx/Bx pin connections (optional). ⑦ HWSC — hardware self-check BIST (optional).

Steps ① and ② are mandatory and take a duration proportional to TCYCLEADC. Each optional step adds additional time. For a fast measurement loop (e.g., 100 ms cycle, need to finish in 20 ms), you would only enable steps ①+② on every cycle, and steps ③–⑦ less frequently (e.g., every 10th cycle). The configuration register for the conversion routine lets you select exactly which optional steps to include on each SOC trigger.

An important safety behavior: if any fault is detected during the execution of steps ①–⑥, the chip automatically forces all remaining steps to run, regardless of the configured optional step selection. This is the "Configuration Override" mechanism (Chapter 55). The idea is that a potential fault triggers a full diagnostic sweep to validate whether the fault is real.

7-step conversion routine. Steps ①② mandatory; steps ③–⑦ optional and configurable per trigger.

Configuration Override: Automatic Fault Validation
🧒 In plain English

When the chip detects a possible fault, it doesn't just immediately panic — it asks "let me double-check everything first." Configuration Override automatically runs all remaining diagnostic steps to validate whether the fault is real before locking in the alarm. This prevents false alarms from a single noisy measurement.

Configuration Override is the L9963E's built-in fault validation mechanism. Whenever a fault condition is detected during a conversion step, the chip automatically forces all remaining (optional) steps to execute — regardless of the configured step selection. The purpose is to run a full diagnostic sweep to confirm the fault with cross-checking before raising a final alarm.

The flow is: normal conversion runs steps ①+②. Cell OV detected in step ①. Override triggers: steps ③④⑤⑥⑦ all run automatically. After the full sweep: if the fault is still present in step ①, all other diagnostics confirm no hardware issue, so the fault is validated as real and the OVR_LATCH flag is set. If another step finds an explanation (e.g., open wire on that cell explains the false OV reading), the fault interpretation changes accordingly.

When Override runs, cyclic mode pauses. The chip will not start the next cyclic cycle until the MCU explicitly clears the OVR_LATCH flag by writing a specific clear sequence. This pause ensures the MCU has time to read and acknowledge the fault before the chip resumes autonomous operation.

The MCU firmware procedure for handling Override: (1) GSW changes to non-zero → read fault registers via burst 0x7A, (2) check OVR_LATCH — if set, an override ran, (3) trigger an on-demand SOC conversion to get fresh data, (4) re-read fault flags — if fault is still present, it's confirmed real; take protective action. (5) After resolving the fault condition, clear OVR_LATCH and restart cyclic mode.

🔧 For your firmware

State machine for fault handling: NORMAL → GSW != 0 → read faults → OVR_LATCH set? → FAULT_VALIDATE state → trigger SOC → read fresh data → fault confirmed? → FAULT_ACTIVE state (open contactor) OR fault cleared → clear OVR_LATCH → restart cyclic → NORMAL. Never just clear OVR_LATCH without first confirming the root cause.

GPIO Fault Detection (Short and Open)
🧒 In plain English

The chip can tell if a temperature sensor wire is broken (open) or if a digital output pin is stuck (shorted). For open: inject current and see if the voltage responds. For short: drive the output and check if it actually changed — if not, something is holding it stuck.

The L9963E's GPIO fault detection covers two scenarios for each GPIO pin, depending on its configured mode.

GPIO short fault (for digital outputs): When a GPIO is configured as a digital output, the chip monitors whether the actual pin voltage matches the programmed output value. If you program an output to be high but the measured pin voltage is low (because something external is pulling it down), the chip detects a "stuck-at" fault and sets the GPOx_short flag. To prevent the chip from fighting a short circuit indefinitely, it also transitions the output to Hi-Z (high impedance, effectively disconnected) after detecting a short. The output must be explicitly re-enabled by firmware after the short condition is resolved.

GPIO open fault (for analog inputs / NTCs): When a GPIO is configured as an analog input and the measured voltage is below the VGPIO_OL (output low) threshold while a pull-down current is active, the GPIOx_OPEN flag is set. This indicates that the NTC thermistor wire is broken or disconnected — the pin has no voltage reference and floats low with the pull-down active. An open NTC means no temperature data for that channel, which could be a safety risk if that cell is in a critical thermal zone.

Both short and open GPIO faults propagate to the GSW and FAULT line. To re-enable a short-protected digital output after the fault is resolved: toggle the output enable bit (set to 0, then back to 1) and clear the fault latch via the standard latch-clear procedure.

🔧 For your firmware

After any fault burst 0x7A: check GPIOx_OPEN flags for all NTC channels. If any are set: (1) that temperature sensor is unreliable — do not use for OT/UT decisions, (2) use adjacent cell temperatures as a conservative estimate, (3) log the fault for maintenance. For digital output shorts: investigate the external circuit before re-enabling the output.

Ground Loss Detection
🧒 In plain English

In automotive environments, ground connections can corrode or break. If the chip's reference ground shifts, all its voltage measurements become wrong — like a ruler with a bent zero end. Ground loss detection catches this before it causes false readings or missed faults.

The L9963E has three internal ground rails: AGND (analog ground, for ADC and cell measurement), DGND (digital ground, for the digital logic core), and CGND (communication ground, for SPI and ISO interfaces). In a well-designed PCB with proper grounding, these all connect to the same plane and have essentially zero voltage difference between them. However, in harsh automotive environments, ground connections can develop high resistance due to corrosion, loose connectors, or broken PCB traces — causing one ground to "float" relative to the others.

The chip continuously monitors the voltage difference between these internal ground references. If any of them deviates by more than approximately 300 mV from the expected value for more than 300 µs (the filter time, to avoid false triggers from transient noise), the corresponding flag is set: loss_agnd, loss_dgnd, or loss_cgnd.

A ground loss event is serious: it means all measurements referenced to that ground are potentially wrong by up to 300 mV or more. A 300 mV shift in AGND would make every cell voltage reading wrong by 300 mV — potentially missing an OV or UV condition, or generating false OV alarms. It would also corrupt the Coulomb counter and temperature readings.

Ground loss flags propagate to the GSW at the critical level. When these flags appear, the chip's measurement outputs should be considered unreliable until the ground integrity is restored and verified. This is a hardware repair situation — firmware cannot compensate for a lost ground connection.

⚙️ Technical Detail

Flags: loss_agnd, loss_dgnd, loss_cgnd. Threshold: ~300 mV deviation from expected reference. Filter time: ~300 µs (must persist to avoid false triggers from transient noise). Propagation: critical level GSW fault + FAULT line assertion. Recovery: requires hardware repair + fault latch clear + system restart.
